It is one thing to be curious about another religion. It is another to move to the other side of the world and try to live inside it. That is what a French monk named Henri Le Saux did in the 1940s.

## A monk from Brittany
Le Saux was born in Brittany in 1910 and entered a Benedictine monastery as a young man. He studied theology, was ordained and became a monk of Kergonan. But he was drawn to India for years. He read widely and wrote letters, and in 1948, at the age of 38, he got permission to travel there.

## A partner in Tamil Nadu
In India he found a friend and fellow dreamer in another Frenchman, a priest named Jules Monchanin, who had been working in a parish in Tamil Nadu since 1939. Together, they planned to create a Christian ashram in the style of a Hindu one, with simple living, Sanskrit prayer and open doors.

## Arunachala and Ramana
In 1949 Le Saux visited Tiruvannamalai and met Ramana Maharshi, who was by then very old. He spent time in the caves of the hill. Of the meeting, he later wrote that he was changed by the presence of the sage, and by the hill itself. What he took from Ramana was not a new belief but a question, *Who am I?*, which we described in [our Arunachala piece](/arunachala-the-hill-that-is-shiva-and-the-walk-around-it/).

## Shantivanam
In 1950, he and Monchanin founded an ashram on the banks of the Kaveri, at Tannirpalli, near Kulithalai in Tamil Nadu. They called it Saccidananda Ashram, using the Advaita words for existence, consciousness and bliss as a name for the Trinity. The ashram, also called Shantivanam, wears saffron, eats on banana leaves and sings in Tamil and Sanskrit. It is now run by Benedictine monks, and is still open to visitors of all faiths.

## The sannyasi
In 1957 Le Saux took the name Abhishiktananda, "the bliss of the Anointed One", a joint Hindu-Christian name. He began to spend more time in the Himalayas, and in 1968 he settled in a hermitage at Gyansu, near Uttarkashi, on the bank of the Ganga. He wrote books about his journey, including *Saccidananda: A Christian Approach to Advaitic Experience*, from 1974.

## Between two worlds
He never left the Church and said Mass until the end. But he described his own experience as an inner awakening that did not fit into the old boundaries, and he wrestled in his diaries with how to bring them together. His writings are honest about the tension. Some Christians have criticised him and some Hindus have questioned whether it is possible to hold both. His supporters say that he asked the question that the 20th century had to ask.

## The end
In July 1973, he had a heart attack in Rishikesh. He recovered, but he said later that the experience had been a form of awakening. He died in Indore on 7 December 1973, at the age of 63.

## What it says
Not everyone who comes to India converts. Some, like Abhishiktananda, stay where they are and find that the ground has shifted. If you want to see what he saw, read [Advaita in plain words](/advaita-vedanta-in-plain-words/) and the [Nirvana Shatakam](/nirvana-shatakam-i-am-not-the-mind-meaning/).
