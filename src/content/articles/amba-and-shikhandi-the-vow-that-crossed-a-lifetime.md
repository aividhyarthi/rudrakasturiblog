**Source:** Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva (chapters 170 to 193) and Bhishma Parva. The identity of Shikhandi is part of the epic and is read in different ways by scholars.

Some stories are about a battle. This one is about what happens when a person is wronged and no one will put it right. It is, in the Mahabharata, the reason Bhishma falls.

## The three princesses
The kingdom of Kashi had three princesses: Amba, Ambika and Ambalika. Their father held a *svayamvara*, in which the women would choose their husbands. Bhishma, the great elder of the Kuru house, came uninvited. To win wives for his young half-brother Vichitravirya, he carried off all three in his chariot, defeating the assembled kings.

It was an act that the epic itself treats with unease. It was within the rules of a warrior, but it was a high-handed one.

## Amba's refusal
Amba told Bhishma that she had already chosen in her heart another king, Salva. Bhishma, honourable, let her go. But when she reached Salva, he refused to take her, saying that she had been carried off by another and was no longer his to wed. Humiliated, she returned to Bhishma and asked him to marry her.

Here is the tragedy. Bhishma had taken a lifelong vow of celibacy. He could not marry her. He would not break his vow, not for a woman, not for justice. His half-brother also would not take her, since she loved another.

So Amba was refused by Salva, who would not have her, and by Bhishma, who could not. She was left with no home, no husband and no place in the world.

## The long penance
She turned to the sages, and then to Parashurama, Bhishma's own teacher. Parashurama himself fought Bhishma for many days on her behalf. It ended in a draw. Neither could win.

Amba then did something extraordinary. She went into the forest and did severe penance to Shiva, asking for just one thing: the power to bring about Bhishma's death. Shiva granted that she would be reborn and would be the cause of his end. She then built a pyre and entered it.

## Shikhandi
She was reborn as a daughter of King Drupada of Panchala, and called Shikhandi. The epic says that Drupada, told of the boon, raised the child as a son. In the story, Shikhandi was later, through the grace of a yaksha called Sthuna, given the form of a man. How we read this has been debated for centuries, and different tellers have understood it differently. In the story's own terms, Shikhandi is the one who, with Amba's memory, was born to see Bhishma fall.

## The tenth day
Bhishma, as the commander of the Kaurava army, was invincible. He could choose the time of his death. The Pandavas, in despair, asked him how he might be defeated. In a scene of great beauty and sorrow, Bhishma himself told them: *I will not raise my weapon against one who was once a woman. Put Shikhandi before Arjuna, and I will put down my bow.*

On the tenth day, Shikhandi came forward, and Arjuna, behind him, shot arrows that filled Bhishma's body until he fell onto a bed of arrows. It is the image the epic is known for.

## Why it still matters
What strikes me is the long arc: a wrong done to a young woman, ignored by every man who could have righted it, followed her for a whole lifetime and came back in the form that could not be ignored. The epic does not say that Amba was justified in her revenge. It simply says that an injustice that is not repaired does not disappear.

Readers today see different things in the story: a critique of male honour codes, a study of the cost of rigid vows, a tale of patience across births. It would be wrong to settle on one.

## Bhishma's side of it
It is also worth asking what the story looks like from Bhishma's side. He had given up the throne and marriage so that his father could marry again, and had taken a vow for his father's happiness. He had carried off the princesses to fulfil a duty to his family, and found himself caught by the same vow in a situation he could not have foreseen. In the epic he does not hate Amba, and he does what he can to find her a place, except the one thing she asked. That, in a way, is the tragedy of a vow: it can make you faithful to a promise and unfaithful to a person.

## Questions to hold
- Was Bhishma wrong to keep his vow, or to carry off the princesses?
- Was Amba wrong to seek his death?
- What could any of the men have done differently?

These are the questions the epic wants us to ask.
