Karthika Masam includes Prabodhini Ekadashi, the day Vishnu is said to wake. And one of the best Ekadashi stories ever told features a king, a short-tempered sage and a spinning discus.

## The king
Ambarisha was a king of the Ikshvaku line, a ruler of the whole earth, and a devotee of Vishnu. Although he had great wealth, he lived simply, spending his days in worship and justice. He kept the Ekadashi fast faithfully every fortnight. The rule of Ekadashi is that the fast is broken on Dwadashi, the next day, before the Dwadashi tithi ends.

## The sage arrives
One year, at the end of a year-long vow, Ambarisha was about to break his fast when the sage Durvasa came to his door. Durvasa was a great ascetic famous for his anger. Ambarisha received him with honour and invited him to eat.

Durvasa said he would go to the river first to bathe, and he went. Time passed, and he did not return. The Dwadashi tithi was about to end. If Ambarisha did not break the fast in time, the whole vow would be spoiled. If he ate before his guest, he would insult the sage.

The priests advised him: drink water. A sip of water counts as breaking the fast and is not eating, so it keeps the vow without disrespecting the guest. Ambarisha took a sip of water and waited.

## The anger
Durvasa returned and, by his powers, knew what had happened. He was furious. He said that the king had insulted him by breaking the fast before the guest was fed. He pulled a lock of hair from his head and created a fiery demon, called Kritya, and sent it against the king.

Ambarisha did not move. He stood with folded hands, thinking of Vishnu.

## The discus
Vishnu's discus, the Sudarshana chakra, which always protects his devotees, rose up and destroyed the demon in an instant. Then it turned after Durvasa.

The sage ran. He ran across the earth, up to the heavens, into the worlds of Brahma and Shiva, and into the ocean and the sky. Wherever he went, the discus followed. Brahma said he could not help. Shiva said he could not help. Finally, he came to Vishnu himself, who said: I am bound by my devotees. Go back to Ambarisha and ask his forgiveness.

## The forgiveness
Durvasa returned, a year later by the telling, to find that Ambarisha had not eaten in all that time, waiting for the sage. The king bowed, begged the discus to stop and asked the sage to forgive him. The sage was humbled. He blessed Ambarisha and praised the power of devotion.

## What the story teaches
- **Humility is greater than power.** Durvasa had all the powers of austerity and still ran in terror.
- **A true devotee is protected,** and also forgiving. Ambarisha did not curse the sage in return.
- **Rules serve devotion, not the other way round.** The sip of water was the compromise between the rule and respect.
- **Waiting for a guest** was a higher courtesy than finishing the fast.
