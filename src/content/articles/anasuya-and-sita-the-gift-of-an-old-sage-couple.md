**Source:** Valmiki Ramayana, Ayodhya Kanda (chapters 117 and 118). Puranic details about Anasuya are noted as such.

Many of us know the dramatic moments in the Ramayana: the exile, the abduction, the bridge, the war. But between the big events, there are quiet scenes where two people sit and talk. One of the loveliest is a conversation between Sita and an old woman living in a hermitage near Chitrakuta.

## The hermitage of Atri
When Rama, Sita and Lakshmana moved on from Chitrakuta, they came to the hermitage of Atri, a great sage who is one of the seven rishis. He received them warmly and asked his wife, Anasuya, to look after Sita. The text describes Anasuya as an old woman, white-haired and frail with age, but with a glow of austerities about her (Ayodhya Kanda 117).

The Ramayana tells us something about her that is easy to miss. During a time of great drought and famine, it says, Anasuya practised austerities that caused fruits and roots to grow and the river Ganga to flow near the hermitage. A woman who could do that for a forest was not a decorative figure. She was a person of real power. Yet she meets Sita as one woman meets another.

## What she gave and what she asked
Anasuya embraced Sita, praised her for following her husband into the forest, and gave her gifts: a divine garland, clothes, ornaments and an unguent that would never fade. The text says that Sita would shine with them even in the forest.

Then she asked Sita to tell her the story of her marriage. Sita told it: how Janaka found her in the furrow, how Rama strung the bow of Shiva, how the wedding happened. Anasuya listened, and the text says that as night came on, they parted with affection.

What strikes me is how ordinary that is. After all the dangers ahead of her, Sita had an evening of telling her own story to someone who wished her well. It was a kind of rest.

## What she said about dharma
In the Valmiki text Anasuya also speaks of the dharma of a wife, praising Sita's choice, and Sita responds with great respect. Some modern readers find parts of this teaching dated, and it is fair to read it that way. But one thing in it is timeless: that devotion is not about what one gets, it is about who one becomes. Anasuya praises Sita for choosing hardship with her husband over comfort without him, and the story goes on to show what that choice costs.

## The Puranic Anasuya
The Puranas tell other stories about Anasuya. In one, the three gods, Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva, come to test her devotion and she turns them into infants and nurses them. In the end they are born to her as the sons Soma, Datta and Durvasa, and Datta is Dattatreya, who is worshipped in Andhra, Karnataka and Maharashtra. These stories are from later texts, not Valmiki, and tell us how highly the tradition held her.

## What it teaches
- **Power does not always look powerful.** An old woman in a forest hut could bring a river.
- **Kindness to a young person on a hard road is a form of dharma.** Anasuya gave Sita courage as well as gifts.
- **Telling your story to someone who listens is a form of rest.**
- **The gifts one gets are not always for show.** The garland was for a woman who would soon be in captivity.

## Where it connects today
Chitrakuta, where Atri and Anasuya are said to have lived, still has a place called Sati Anasuya Ashram on the banks of the Mandakini. Dattatreya Jayanti is celebrated on Margashira Pournami.
