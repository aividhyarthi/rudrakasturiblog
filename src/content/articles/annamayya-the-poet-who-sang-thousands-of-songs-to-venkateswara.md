*పదిహేనవ శతాబ్దపు తాళ్ళపాక అన్నమయ్య తిరుమల వేంకటేశ్వరునికి వేలాది సంకీర్తనలు పాడాడని సంప్రదాయం చెబుతుంది. ఆయన పాటల్లో భక్తితో పాటు అందరూ సమానమనే మాట కూడా వినిపిస్తుంది.*

Think of a child in a Telugu home, sitting cross-legged while a teacher claps out a beat and the class sings together. Very often the first song is about a baby Krishna who is too sweet to be scolded. Do you know whose words those are? Chances are they belong to a man who lived more than five hundred years ago in a small village, and who simply could not stop singing.

## The poet of Tallapaka

Annamayya (అన్నమయ్య) was born at Tallapaka, in what is now the Kadapa district of Andhra Pradesh. The usual dates given are 1408 to 1503, though like many details of medieval lives these come from tradition and from the family's own records, not from a court chronicle. He is also called Annamacharya, and his name appears as a signature in his songs: the closing line often carries the name of the Lord, Venkatesha, as the poet's mark.

Tradition says he left home as a young man, troubled and unsure, and climbed the Tirumala hills on foot. There the Lord took him in, and from then on the poet's life and the Lord's temple were bound together. Later accounts, which we should hold gently, even call him an incarnation of the Lord's sword, Nandaka. Whether or not you accept that, the picture is clear enough: a man for whom singing was a form of living.

## The copper plates

Here is the part that makes Annamayya unusual among saints: his songs were written down in his own lifetime. Tradition holds that he composed about 32,000 sankirtanas, and that his descendants had them engraved on copper plates and stored in the temple at Tirumala. Many plates were found in a room of the temple in the early twentieth century, and they have since been studied and published. A good part of that huge number has not survived, so please treat 32,000 as what the tradition says and not a number anyone has counted.

His son Peda Tirumalacharya and grandson Chinnayya also composed, which is one reason scholars sometimes speak of a whole Tallapaka family of poets.

## Songs you may already know

Three songs you will hear again and again:

- *Brahmam Okate* (బ్రహ్మము ఒకటే), about the one Brahman present in all.
- *Muddugare Yashoda*, a mother's delight in her baby Krishna.
- *Cheri Yashodaku*, another picture of the child with Yashoda.

I name only these because they are widely known as his. Many other songs are sung in his name, and the best way to be sure of authorship is the Tirumala editions.

## A new kind of Telugu song

Before Annamayya, much devotional writing in Telugu was long and learned. He gave the language the *padakavita*: a short song with a pallavi (the refrain) and a few charanams (verses), meant to be sung. This is the same shape you see in later Telugu songs and, by influence, in the Carnatic kriti. He wrote in simple speech, close to what people actually said in their homes.

His themes range widely. Some songs are tender and childlike. Some are *shringara*, where the soul is the beloved and the Lord the lover. Others look hard at the world: the hunger, the pride, the little deceptions of everyday life. He asks where the Lord is and answers that he is in the one who is hungry and the one who feeds.

## What he says about people

Read the words of *Brahmam Okate* slowly. The song says, in plain terms, that the one Brahman is in the king and the beggar, in the learned and the unlettered, and that high and low are only names we have given. Tradition also tells that he refused to sing the praises of a human king, saying his tongue belonged to the Lord. Some tellings add that this cost him dearly. Treat that as a story with a point: a poet's voice is not for sale.

Yet he never preached against the temple. For him the Lord of the seven hills stands in the sanctum and also in every person who comes to see him. ## How to begin with Annamayya

You do not need a teacher to start. Try this for a week:

1. Pick one song and read the Telugu lines aloud, even if you cannot sing.
2. Read the meaning once, in your own language.
3. Hum it while doing something ordinary, like cooking.
4. Notice which line stays with you.

Do not worry about raga or pronunciation at first. The poet himself sang for any ear that would listen. On Saturdays, when many families think of Venkateswara, you may find a song more natural than a long prayer. See also [how to worship the Lord on Saturdays](/saturday-venkateswara-shani-hanuman-how-to-worship/).

## Where this comes from

The main sources are the copper plate inscriptions at Tirumala, the songs themselves, and the *Annamacharya Charitramu*, a biographical poem traditionally attributed to his grandson Chinnanna. Biographies that mention miracles, the sword story or his dealings with kings are later or traditional tellings, and they differ from one another. The dates 1408 to 1503 are the commonly given ones and are not certain.
