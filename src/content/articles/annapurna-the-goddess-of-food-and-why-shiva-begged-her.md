Think of the last time you left food on your plate. Perhaps you did not feel hungry. Perhaps it was just a little. Now think of the grandmother at the head of the house who, if she saw it, would pick up every grain of rice from the plate and put it in her mouth, quietly, as if a small prayer.

She is acting out a story that is about a thousand years old.

## The story
In the tradition, Shiva and Parvati were once playing at dice. In the argument that followed, Shiva said something sharp: that all of this, food and the world and *maya*, was illusion. Parvati, who is Shakti, the power behind creation, was hurt. She said nothing. She simply withdrew from the world.

With Shakti gone, nothing grew. The fields dried up. People had no food. Shiva himself felt hunger and found he could not stop it. The gods came to Shiva and begged him to undo what had happened.

Shiva understood. He went to Kashi, where Parvati, now as **Annapurna**, the one who is full of food, sat on a golden throne with a ladle in her hand and a pot of rice. And Shiva, the great ascetic, the lord of the three worlds, stood at her door with a begging bowl.

She filled the bowl. The story says that Shiva was fed, and the world's hunger ended. The lesson is plain: *even the Absolute needs food, and food is her gift.*

## What the picture shows
In the Annapurna icon at Kashi, the goddess holds a ladle and a bowl of rice or sweet. Shiva, in some pictures, stands before her as a beggar. The image upends what we usually imagine: the god of renunciation, asking. It is a picture of the household, where the one who feeds is the real sovereign.

## Shankara's verse
Adi Shankara composed a hymn to her, the Annapurna Stotram, which ends with a verse many families know:

> **annapūrṇe sadā-pūrṇe śaṅkara-prāṇa-vallabhe**
> **jñāna-vairāgya-siddhy-artham bhikṣāṁ dehi ca pārvati**

*O Annapurna, always full, beloved of Shankara, O Parvati, give me alms for the sake of knowledge and detachment.*

Look at what he asks for. Not food, not wealth. *Knowledge and the ability to let go.* In the tradition, a person who has eaten properly is able to turn to higher things, and the goddess who gives food is also the one who can give understanding.

## What people do in her honour
- **A short prayer before every meal.** Many families say the verse *brahmarpanam brahma havih* from the Gita (4.24), which sees the food, the one who offers it and the one who eats as all one.
- **Never waste food.** Take what you can eat. Leave nothing on the plate.
- **Give food (annadanam).** It is called the greatest of gifts because it satisfies at once. Temples across the South, including Tirumala, Srisailam and Annavaram, serve free meals every day.
- **Annakut.** On the day after Diwali, a huge array of food is offered to the Lord as a mountain. In Kashi, the Annapurna temple prepares a great spread.

## Annapurna at Kashi
The Annapurna temple in Varanasi is close to the Vishwanath temple, and pilgrims usually visit both. A common custom is to receive a small amount of rice from the temple as prasadam, and keep a few grains in the family grain store, in the hope that it will never run out.

## The Gita on food
Before a meal, many families say a verse from the Gita (4.24): *brahmarpanam brahma havir brahmagnau brahmana hutam, brahmaiva tena gantavyam brahma-karma-samadhina.* It says that the offering is Brahman, the fire is Brahman, the one who offers is Brahman, and the one who sees all as Brahman will reach Brahman. In a simple sense, it asks you to eat with the awareness that the meal is a sacred act and not merely a task.

## Annakut and the mountain of food
On the day after Diwali, in Kashi and in Vaishnava temples across the country, a mountain of food, cooked in hundreds of varieties, is arranged before the deity. It is called *Annakut*, "a heap of grain". It recalls Krishna lifting Govardhan, and in Kashi it honours Annapurna. After the offering, the food is shared with everyone who comes. It is a festival of abundance, and of sharing it.

## For a family
If you have children, tell them this story at the dinner table. It makes a better rule than *finish your plate*. *Rice is the goddess's gift.* Children seem to understand it without fuss.

## A small practice
For one week, try this. Before each meal, stop for five seconds, look at the food, and say *thank you* in your own language. Take only what you can finish. If you have leftovers, give them to someone or something that needs them. Many people say the meals begin to taste different.

## A thought to take away
Hunger is real for millions. The goddess Annapurna is not only a picture on a wall. In a very practical sense, she is anyone who feeds. If you have ever been fed by a stranger, or fed someone who was hungry, you have met her.
