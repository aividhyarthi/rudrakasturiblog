If you wanted an example of someone who changed her mind more than once, it would be hard to beat Annie Besant. She is often described as a woman who spent her life moving from one cause to another. But the thread that connects them is a search.

## A vicar's wife
Born in London in 1847, Annie Wood married a clergyman, Frank Besant, at the age of twenty. They had two children, and the marriage was unhappy. Her faith, tested by illness and by her own reading, gave way. In the 1870s she left her husband, lost custody of her daughter and became a public speaker for atheism and women's rights.

## The freethinker
In the 1870s and 1880s, she was one of the best-known freethinkers in Britain. She worked with Charles Bradlaugh in the National Secular Society, was prosecuted for publishing a pamphlet on birth control, and later organised the match girls' strike of 1888. At that stage, she held that religion was a barrier to progress.

## The turn
In 1889, she was asked to review a book, *The Secret Doctrine*, by Helena Blavatsky, the founder of the Theosophical Society. The review changed her course. She met Blavatsky, was deeply impressed and joined the Society. Her first encounter with Indian thought came through Theosophy, with its interest in karma, rebirth and the unity of all religions.

## India
She first came to India in 1893 and made the Theosophical Society's headquarters at Adyar, in Madras, her home for the rest of her life. In India, she studied Sanskrit and read the Hindu texts, and was moved in particular by the Bhagavad Gita, which she translated as *The Lord's Song* in 1895.

She argued that India's own traditions should be the base of Indian education. In 1898 she founded the Central Hindu College in Varanasi, which taught the Hindu tradition alongside modern subjects. Her work was behind the founding of Banaras Hindu University in 1916, to which the college was passed.

## Home Rule
As she grew closer to India, she became increasingly involved in politics. In 1916 she started the Home Rule League, calling for self-government, and was interned by the British for a time in 1917. Public protest led to her release, and the same year, she was elected president of the Indian National Congress.

## Complicated legacy
It is fair to add that Besant is also a figure of debate. Some Indian leaders felt she was too close to the British establishment at certain moments, or that her Theosophical ideas, such as promoting a young Indian boy as the new world teacher, were mistaken. She and the Congress also parted ways in the 1920s. People may reasonably disagree over her place, and what is clear is that her work for education left an institution that is still running.

She died at Adyar in 1933, at the age of 85.

## What her story shows
Besant's path from atheist platform to the Gita was not smooth. She went there by reading, arguing and working.
