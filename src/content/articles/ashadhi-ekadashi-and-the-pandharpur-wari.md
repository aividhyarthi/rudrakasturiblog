Picture a road in the monsoon. Rain on the fields, red mud on the verge, and as far as you can see, people walking. Men in white, women with tulasi pots on their heads, children keeping up as best they can. Cymbals, small drums called mridanga, and a verse that rises and falls. This is the Pandharpur wari, and it has been going on, in one form or another, for centuries.

## What the wari is

*Wari* means a regular journey, a visit that returns each year. The people who make it are called **warkaris**, "those who do the wari". Their goal is Pandharpur, on the Bhima river, where Vitthala stands on his brick. You can read about him in [Vitthala of Pandharpur, the god who waits on a brick](/vitthala-of-pandharpur-the-god-who-waits-on-a-brick/).

Many walk from their own villages. Two great streams, though, gather the largest crowds. The **palanquin (palkhi) of Dnyaneshwar** sets out from Alandi, near Pune, and the **palkhi of Tukaram** from Dehu. Each carries the silver sandals, the padukas, of the saint. Tradition holds that the two processions meet and move together on the road, then arrive in Pandharpur a few days before Ashadhi Ekadashi. The route and dates are published each year by the organising committees and the temple, so check those before you plan to join.

## The dindi

The warkaris travel in groups called **dindis**, each with its own banner, its own leader, and often its own village. A dindi is something like a travelling family. It has a cook, a singer, someone to look after the elderly. People walk in rows, singing together, and at set places they pause to do a **ringan**, a ring of devotees circling a horse, and to play a kind of simple, joyful game.

The songs are **abhangs**, short devotional poems in Marathi by Dnyaneshwar, Namdev, Eknath, Tukaram and other saints. The language is the language of the field and the market. A warkari may not have studied Sanskrit, but will often know dozens of abhangs by heart.

## Ashadhi Ekadashi

The wari is timed for the Ekadashi of the bright half of the Ashadha month (roughly June to July). It is known as **Ashadhi Ekadashi**, also **Devshayani Ekadashi**, and in some Telugu families **Toli Ekadashi**. Tradition holds that on this day Vishnu goes to rest on the cosmic serpent for four months, and wakes on Prabodhini Ekadashi in Karthika.

Dates change each year. Check [our Ekadashi page](/ekadashi/) and the [festival calendar](/festivals/) for the one that applies to you. Many families fast on the day, as on all Ekadashis, and eat light, simple food on the next morning.

## The call

If you listen to a video of the wari, you will hear a shout rise from thousands of throats: **Gyanba Tukaram**. "Gyanba" is an affectionate form of Dnyaneshwar's name, as if one were calling a beloved elder brother. It is a joined call, naming the two saints who walk at the heart of the wari, and often followed by "Jai Hari Vitthal".

## Chaturmasya begins

With Ashadhi Ekadashi, the four months of **chaturmasya** begin in many traditions, the rainy months when the Lord is said to rest. It is a time for restraint and attention: some families give up a favourite food, some read a sacred text each day, some observe extra fasts. Practice varies greatly by region and family, so follow the way of your own household. The rhythm is that the world slows, the rains come, and the heart turns inward.

## What the walk means

Why would anyone walk for two weeks, in rain, with blistered feet, to stand for a moment before an image? Warkaris and teachers give several answers.

- **Equality.** On the road, no one asks your caste or your work. Everyone is "Mauli", mother, to everyone else. Tradition remembers the saints as people from many castes, and the wari carries that spirit.
- **Surrender.** You cannot control the road, the weather or the crowd. You walk and let go.
- **Song as a path.** The abhang is both food and map. It tells you what to love.
- **Arrival.** The glimpse of the Lord, often brief, is for many the end of a year's thirst.

Others, who cannot walk, take part in their own way. Some provide food and water along the route, some host a dindi in their village for the night, and some sing the abhangs at home on Ekadashi. These, too, are part of the wari, tradition says.

## If you want to begin

Start small. Learn one abhang. Fast on Ashadhi Ekadashi if your health allows, and if it does not, offer a tulasi leaf and a lamp to Vitthala instead. If you wish to walk, join a registered dindi and listen to the leaders about food, shelter and safety.
