*అష్టావక్ర గీత అష్టావక్ర ముని జనక మహారాజుకు ఆత్మజ్ఞానం గురించి చెప్పిన సంవాదం, సుమారు ఇరవై అధ్యాయాల చిన్న గ్రంథం. ఇక్కడ క్రియలు, పూజలు ఎక్కువ కనిపించవు; నీవే సాక్షివి అనే సత్యాన్ని నేరుగా చెబుతుంది.*

Have you ever been told, for years, to do more, practise harder, fast longer, and then wondered whether freedom could be something you already have? The Ashtavakra Gita (అష్టావక్ర గీత) is the book that dares to say it, and says it in a voice with no hesitation at all.

## The two speakers

You may already know the story of [the boy with eight bends who silenced a court](/ashtavakra-the-boy-with-eight-bends-who-silenced-a-court/), where the young Ashtavakra defeats the scholars in Janaka's assembly. The Gita takes those two figures and imagines a later, quieter meeting. Janaka (జనకుడు) is the king of Mithila, remembered as a ruler who lived fully in the world yet was free within it. Ashtavakra is the sage whose body was bent and whose understanding was not.

Mark one thing at the start. The dialogue is not part of the Mahabharata's story. It is a separate work that borrows the names. Its author is unknown, and scholars generally place it well after the epic, with dates suggested across a wide range, so no single date can be given with confidence.

## What happens in the book

The first chapter begins with three questions from Janaka: how is knowledge gained, how is liberation reached, how does detachment come? Ashtavakra's answer is one of the most direct in Sanskrit. If you want freedom, he says in substance, treat the objects of the senses as poison and take forgiveness, sincerity, compassion, contentment and truth as nectar. Then he goes further: you are not earth, water, fire, air or sky; you are the witness of all these, pure awareness. Rest in that, and you are free here and now.

The chapters that follow sound, to many readers, like a song rather than a lesson. Janaka replies in wonder: he has found himself, and he sees the world as waves rising in himself the ocean. Later chapters speak of the freed person, who is neither pulled by pleasure nor pushed by pain, who does what comes without making a project of it. The tone is calm, confident and sometimes startling. In one reply Janaka even bows to himself in wonder, as the tradition reads the second chapter, which can seem strange until you hear that the self being saluted is not the small ego.

## How it is not the Bhagavad Gita

It helps to compare the two, because they are often confused.

- The Bhagavad Gita begins on a battlefield. Arjuna is confused about duty, and Krishna gives him several paths: action without clinging, devotion, knowledge. You will find [the central teaching of the Gita](/bhagavad-gita/) in it, and a lot of encouragement for a person who must keep working.
- The Ashtavakra Gita begins with a ready student. Nobody is confused about a duty. Ashtavakra states the final view and does not argue for graded steps.
- The Bhagavad Gita speaks warmly of devotion to God. The Ashtavakra Gita says little of worship and nothing of ritual.
- The first takes you to the door of freedom through practice; the second tells you, directly, that you are standing in it.

That last difference is why many teachers advise caution. For a person who still gets angry before breakfast, a text that says "you are already free" can be heard as permission to stop trying. Traditions that value it usually insist that the reader be sincere and prepared, and that a teacher help. If you want a gentler route, [Advaita in plain words](/advaita-vedanta-in-plain-words/) is a good bridge.

## Who has valued it

Shankara's lineage is Advaita, and the Ashtavakra Gita reads as Advaita at its sharpest, though it is not among the works traditionally attributed to Shankara. Several modern teachers loved it. Ramana Maharshi is reported by his devotees to have held it in high regard, and the book is often mentioned among the texts people brought to him. If you want to quote what these teachers actually said, look for the original record, since the stories are repeated loosely.

## How to read it

The book has no story, no battlefield, no gods arriving in chariots. It is mostly short verses of statement, so its force comes from slow reading and from honest looking within.

You do not need to read it all in a night. Take one chapter, read it slowly, and stop where a verse strikes you. Many people begin with the first chapter and the second, since these carry the main message. Keep a translation beside the Sanskrit, because translators differ, and ask yourself after each verse a plain question: is this something I have seen, or only something I have agreed with? Agreement is easy; recognition is rare, and the book cares only for the second.

## Where this comes from

The text is called Ashtavakra Samhita or Ashtavakra Gita, in about twenty chapters and roughly three hundred verses, depending on the manuscript. It is not part of Vyasa's Mahabharata; the story of Ashtavakra himself is in the Vana Parva, in the Tirtha-yatra section. The Gita is a later philosophical work, and its author is unknown. Telugu and Tamil commentaries exist but are less common than those for the Bhagavad Gita.
