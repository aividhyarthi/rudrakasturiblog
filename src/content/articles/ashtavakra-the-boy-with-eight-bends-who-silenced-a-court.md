**Source:** Mahabharata, Vana Parva (the Ashtavakra episode, told by the sage Lomasha to Yudhishthira).

Wisdom does not always look the way we expect. This is a story about a twelve-year-old boy, bent in eight places, who walked into a king's court full of grey-bearded scholars and silenced them.

## The voice from the womb
Kahoda was a Vedic scholar who taught his pupils every day, with his wife Sujata, daughter of the great Uddalaka, listening. Sujata was pregnant. One day, as Kahoda recited, a voice came from the womb: *Father, you are saying it wrongly.*

Kahoda was offended. A child not yet born had corrected him in front of his students. In his anger he cursed the child to be born deformed in eight places. The boy was born with eight bends in his body, *ashta-vakra*, and that became his name.

## The debate that cost a life
Sujata, poor and with a child to raise, urged her husband to go to the court of King Janaka of Videha, where, it was said, great scholars could win gifts. There was a court scholar there named Bandin who was said to be a son of Varuna. He had a terrible custom: those who lost debates to him were drowned in the river.

Kahoda went, argued and lost. He was drowned. The family never forgot.

## The boy at the gate
Ashtavakra grew up in the house of his maternal grandfather, thinking that Uddalaka was his father and Shvetaketu his brother. When he discovered the truth, at the age of twelve, he went with his uncle to the court of Janaka.

The gatekeeper stopped them. *Only the old and the learned go in,* he said. Ashtavakra answered, *age is not measured by grey hair. He who knows the Vedas is old, though a child.* The gatekeeper, impressed, let them in.

In the court, the scholars laughed when they saw his crooked body. Ashtavakra laughed back, and when asked why, said he had come to a court to find scholars and had found only leather-dealers, who judge a man by his skin. The laughter stopped.

## The debate
In the contest of verses, Bandin posed a question using numbers: one sun, two Ashvins, three fires, four Vedas, and so on, up to thirteen. Ashtavakra completed the list and went on beyond Bandin's limit. Bandin, left without an answer, was silent.

Ashtavakra won. When Janaka asked what he wanted, he asked for Bandin to be drowned, as Bandin had done with others. Bandin said calmly that he was Varuna's son and the scholars had not been drowned, but sent to Varuna's sacrificial rite, and that they would return. They did, and Kahoda was among them.

## The straightening
Kahoda embraced his son, and told him to bathe in the river Samanga. When he came out, he was straight. That is how the tradition tells it.

## What it teaches
- **Do not judge a person by the body.** The scholars laughed and were wrong.
- **Wisdom is not tied to age.** The gatekeeper learned that.
- **A teacher can be wrong, and a child can correct him.** Kahoda's anger cost him dearly.
- **A debate is meant to find truth, not to crush people.** Bandin's custom is condemned by the story.

## The other Ashtavakra
A famous Advaita text, the *Ashtavakra Gita*, is a conversation between Ashtavakra and Janaka. It is a later work, and scholars do not take it as the work of the boy in this story, though it takes his name. It is worth reading for its radical statement of non-duality.
