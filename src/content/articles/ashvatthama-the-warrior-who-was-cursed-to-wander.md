Have you ever done something terrible at the end of a long, painful stretch, when you were tired and wanted someone to pay? That is Ashvatthama (అశ్వత్థామ). He is the one major warrior in the Mahabharata who does not find peace at the end of the war.

## Drona's son

Ashvatthama was the son of Drona, the teacher of the Kuru princes, and of Kripi. The epic says he was born with a jewel on his forehead that protected him from hunger, thirst and from dangers of weapons. He grew up on his father's training and loved him deeply. Drona, for his part, spoke of this child as his dearest possession.

Then on the fifteenth day of the war Drona was killed in a manner that broke him, when Yudhishthira's half-truth about an elephant named Ashvatthama reached his ears. His son was told, and the grief turned to a single purpose. If you want the other side of that teacher's story, [Ekalavya, the archer who gave his thumb](/ekalavya-the-archer-who-gave-his-thumb/) shows the same teacher at a darker moment.

## The night

After Duryodhana fell, three Kaurava survivors, Ashvatthama, Kripa and Kritavarma, sat in a forest at night. The epic's Sauptika Parva tells how Ashvatthama, watching an owl attack sleeping crows, decided to do the same to the Pandava camp.

He went in while it slept. He killed Dhrishtadyumna, who had slain his father, and he killed Shikhandi and the five sons of Draupadi, the Upapandavas. The Pandavas themselves were not in the camp. This is one of the most painful chapters in the epic. Even his companions, it says, were troubled.

## Brahmashira

Pursued by the Pandavas the next morning, Ashvatthama took up a blade of grass and invoked the Brahmashira, a weapon of terrible force, which he did not know how to recall. Arjuna answered with the same weapon to meet it. The sages Vyasa and Narada stepped between them and asked both to withdraw. Arjuna could. Ashvatthama could not, and turned it aside to the womb of Uttara, where Abhimanyu's child lay.

Krishna promised to protect that child, who lived to be Parikshit. See [Parikshit and the curse](/parikshit-and-the-curse-how-to-face-a-known-end/) for what came after him.

## The curse and the jewel

Krishna cursed Ashvatthama. According to the epic, he was to wander for three thousand years without companions, carrying the sores of his wounds, shunned by people. Ashvatthama was made to give up the jewel on his forehead, which Bhima carried to Draupadi. Vyasa is said to have spoken in the same scene.

Notice what is being punished. It is not his anger at his father's death; that was something anyone might feel. It is that he used the power of sleep and darkness against people who could not defend themselves, and then used a weapon against an unborn child.

## Chiranjivi and the wandering

Tradition places him among the Chiranjivis, the seven long-lived beings, along with Bali, Vyasa, Hanuman, Vibhishana, Kripa and Parashurama. Different lists exist.

Across India there are local stories that Ashvatthama is still seen, that he asks for oil for his wounds, that he walked certain forts and riverbanks. Please take these as folk belief. They are later than the epic, they vary by village, and the epic gives a long period, not a promise of present-day sightings.

## What it asks of us

Is a wrong done to us ever a reason to do a wrong to the innocent? The epic's answer is hard and clear. Ashvatthama's pain was real. The punishment was not for the pain but for what he chose to do with it.

## Why Krishna's curse, not a simple death

You might ask why Ashvatthama was not killed. Tradition suggests a reason: he was a brahmana by birth and the son of a teacher, and the epic shows a reluctance to kill such a person, even for a crime like this. Instead, the punishment is a long life of what he did not want: loneliness and pain. The curse is built to match the offence. He chose darkness and violence against the sleeping, and he is given long solitude.

It is also a kind of mercy that some readers notice. A person who is allowed to live can still, in principle, change. The epic does not tell us that he did. But it does not close the door in the text.

## The jewel

The jewel on his forehead had protected him from fear and hunger. Losing it, then, is losing the thing that made him feel safe. When Bhima brought it to Draupadi she gave it to Yudhishthira, who wore it on his head, according to the tradition. Her sons were gone, and she wished only to see the man answered for what he did. When she saw the jewel she said she would end her fast of grief, and the epic moves on.

It is worth noticing that she did not ask for his death. That choice of hers is one of the quiet, strong moments in the whole story, and it is often missed.

## Where this comes from

His birth and training are in the Adi Parva; his father's fall in the Drona Parva; the night attack, the Brahmashira and the curse in the Sauptika Parva of Vyasa's Mahabharata. The exact length of the wandering and details of the curse vary across tellings, and the Chiranjivi list is from the wider tradition. I am not sure of every local place named in folk accounts, so I have not named them.

## A question to carry

When you are hurt and tired, what keeps your anger from landing on the person who cannot defend themselves?
