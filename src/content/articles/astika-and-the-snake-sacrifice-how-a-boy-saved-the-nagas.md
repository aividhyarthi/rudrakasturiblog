**Source:** Mahabharata, Adi Parva (the Astika Parva, chapters on Jaratkaru, Astika and the sarpa-satra).

Anger is understandable when someone you love has been killed. This is the story of what happens when it is allowed to become a project, and of a boy who ended it with a single word.

## A promise to the ancestors
The story begins with a sage named Jaratkaru, who had vowed never to marry. One day he came upon his ancestors, hanging upside down in a pit, holding on to a single thread of grass that was being gnawed away by a rat. They told him that they would fall into hell unless he had a son. The thread was the last of his line.

Jaratkaru agreed to marry, on condition that the bride have his own name, and that she not be given to him by the usual rites. Vasuki, king of the nagas, had a sister who was also called Jaratkaru. She became his wife. Their son was named Astika.

## The curse on the snakes
The nagas had been cursed by their own mother, Kadru, that they would be burned in a sacrifice, because they had refused to join her in a trick. They were afraid. Brahma promised that the curse would be undone by a son born to Jaratkaru and the sister of Vasuki.

## The sacrifice of Janamejaya
King Parikshit, the grandson of Arjuna, was bitten by the snake Takshaka and died. His son Janamejaya, grief-stricken and angry, began a great sacrifice to burn all the snakes of the world. The priests chanted. One by one, snakes of every kind were pulled by the mantras into the fire.

Takshaka, afraid, fled to Indra for protection. The priests said that the mantras would draw him in along with Indra.

## The boy at the gate
At that moment Astika arrived at the sacrificial hall. The gatekeepers tried to turn him away, but the boy, still young, praised the king and the rite so eloquently that Janamejaya, pleased, said he would grant any boon.

Astika asked for one thing: *End this sacrifice.*

The king, who had given his word, was reluctant. The priests pleaded to wait until Takshaka was in the fire. But Janamejaya had promised, and in the end he kept it. The sacrifice was stopped, and the snakes were saved. The text says that the nagas, in gratitude, promised that anyone who remembered Astika's name would be safe from their bite.

## What it teaches
- **Revenge feeds itself.** Janamejaya's grief was real, but the sacrifice would never have healed it.
- **A promise is a promise, even for a king.** He could have refused and did not.
- **One person with the courage to ask for the right thing can end a great harm.**
- **Even the feared have a story.** The nagas were cursed and frightened, not only dangerous.

## How it lives today
Naga Panchami, in the month of Shravana, is a day for worshipping snakes with milk and turmeric. In Andhra and Telangana the same spirit is seen at Nagula Chavithi, in Karthika masam, when people pour milk on anthills. The line *Astikasya vachah shrutva* is chanted by some families for protection from snakes. At Kukke Subramanya in Karnataka, the snake worship of the coast has a strong place in practice.
