*గోకర్ణ క్షేత్ర స్థల పురాణం ప్రకారం శివుడు రావణునికి ఆత్మలింగాన్ని ఇచ్చాడు. గణపతి బాలుని రూపంలో వచ్చి దానిని భూమిపై ఉంచేలా చేశాడు, ఇది స్థల పురాణం, చెప్పే తీరు మారుతుంది.*

Imagine carrying the most precious thing you own, and being told that you must not put it down until you reach home. Then your hands tire, the light fades, and a stranger offers to hold it for a moment. Would you hand it over? That is, in short, the story told at Gokarna (గోకర్ణ) on the Karnataka coast.

## A word before the story

This is a sthala-purana, the legend of a holy place, passed on in temples and families. It is not in Valmiki's Ramayana. Different temples, Puranic summaries and local storytellers tell it with changes, and some details below appear in some versions and not in others. I will say "tradition holds" where that matters.

## Ravana's penance and the gift

Tradition holds that Ravana's mother, Kaikasi, used to worship a lingam of sand, and that it was washed away when the tide rose. Ravana, grieving for her sorrow, went to Kailasa and worshipped Shiva with great austerity. In some tellings this is the same penance in which he sang to Shiva. Pleased, Shiva gave him the Atmalinga, a lingam said to hold the power of Shiva's self. Whoever carried it home to Lanka and installed it there would have an unshakeable kingdom.

There was one condition: the lingam must never be set on the ground before it was installed. Wherever it touched earth, it would stay.

## The gods are afraid

The devas understood what that meant. A Ravana with the Atmalinga in Lanka would be beyond anyone's reach. They went to Vishnu and to Ganesha for help. In the common telling, Vishnu covered the sun so it looked like evening, the hour of sandhya vandana, which a devout Brahmin like Ravana would never skip.

Ravana, walking near Gokarna, saw the sun apparently going down. He needed to perform his evening prayer and he could not put the lingam down. Just then a young cowherd boy appeared. That boy was Ganesha (గణపతి).

## The boy and the three calls

Ravana asked the boy to hold the lingam while he did his prayers. The boy agreed, but said that if the weight became too much he would call Ravana three times, and then he would put the lingam down.

Ravana went to the water. The boy called once, twice, and then a third time before Ravana could return, and he set the lingam on the ground. When Ravana came running, the lingam was rooted in the earth. In his anger he struck the boy on the head, and some temples say that this is why Ganesha is shown with a dent on his head in certain images. He then tried to pull the lingam up with all his strength, and it bent and twisted but did not come out. The shape of a cow's ear, go-karna, is the name tradition gives to the place for this reason, though other explanations of the name are also given.

Tradition then adds that pieces of the covering of the lingam flew to different spots on the coast, which are honoured as sacred places in their own right. Tellings differ about which places, and I would not give you a list I could not be certain of.

## Why this story is kept

It is easy to read this as a trick played on a villain. But look at what is actually said. Ravana's one weakness here is his devotion. He is faithful to his daily prayer even when it costs him his greatest prize. A wise elder might say that the gods cannot defeat him in this story except through his own dharma. Is that a victory, or a tribute?

The story also says that the greatest gifts cannot be possessed. The Atmalinga stays where it was set down, and everyone who comes to Gokarna can worship it, rather than one king alone.

## Visiting Gokarna Mahabaleshwara

The temple is in Gokarna, Uttara Kannada, Karnataka. A few practical points that families usually mention:

- Dress simply and traditionally for the temple.
- Check the temple's own notices for rules about touching the lingam and about darshan hours, since these can change.
- Many pilgrims bathe in the sea before darshan.

## Other tellings you may hear

You may hear that the boy was a cowherd who was chosen by Ganesha only to appear as such, or that Narada, the wandering sage, had a hand in setting events moving. Some say Ravana's brother or his mother was with him on the journey. Others place the story with different shrines along the same coast, each claiming a piece of the lingam's covering. None of these tellings cancel the others for the families who hold them.

If you are not sure which version your own family keeps, it is no failure. Local memory is not a single text. Ask an elder at the temple, listen to the whole version, and note it down. That small act keeps a tradition alive more surely than any argument over which telling is correct.

## Where this comes from

The Gokarna legend is carried by sthala-purana tradition and is found in various Puranic summaries and local accounts, with differences in details like Kaikasi's lingam, Ganesha's role, and the places where the covering fell. It is not in the Valmiki Ramayana. If a temple priest tells you a different version, it is not a mistake; it is the way living traditions work.
