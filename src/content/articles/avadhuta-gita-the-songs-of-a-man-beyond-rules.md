*అవధూత గీత దత్తాత్రేయుడికి ఆపాదించబడిన, అన్ని నియమాలను దాటిన స్థితిని చెప్పే చిన్న అద్వైత గ్రంథం. ఇది చాలా తీవ్రమైనది, కాబట్టి నెమ్మదిగా, జాగ్రత్తగా చదవాలి.*

Have you ever met someone who seems to need nothing: no title, no certificate, no particular place, and who still makes the room calmer? The old word for such a person is avadhuta (అవధూత). And a short book attributed to Dattatreya sings from inside that state.

## Who is an avadhuta?

The word comes from a root meaning to shake off. An avadhuta is one who has shaken off the dust of rules, castes, stages of life, even the sense of being a separate person. In our land there have been many such wanderers: in clothes or without, silent or talkative, sometimes looking mad to ordinary eyes. Tradition names Dattatreya as the great avadhuta, the one who learned from twenty-four teachers in nature. If you have not read that story, see [Dattatreya and the 24 gurus](/dattatreya-and-the-24-gurus-what-the-avadhuta-learned/).

A caution, though. Anyone can look careless. What makes an avadhuta is not the appearance but the inner freedom, and that is not something we can judge from outside. Many teachers warn against copying the outer behaviour of such a person.

## What the book is

The Avadhuta Gita is a short Sanskrit text in verse, traditionally attributed to Dattatreya. It is also known by the name Dattatreya Gita or Avadhuta Gita; as with many such texts, who set it down in writing is not known. Scholars generally place it in the medieval period, and the date and authorship are not settled. Some manuscripts are divided into eight chapters, and others differ.

The tone is the thing. Most spiritual books give you steps. This one does not. It speaks as someone who has arrived, and it keeps saying: there is no one to arrive, no path, no practice, no bondage, no liberation. It is as radically non-dual as Sanskrit writing gets.

## The opening

The text begins, in paraphrase, by saying that it is only by the grace of God that the desire for non-duality (advaita) arises in a person, and that this desire protects one from great fear. In other words, the wish to know the one reality is itself a gift, not an achievement. Does that sentence not change how we see our own longing? We think we are seeking. The book says we are being drawn.

After this opening it goes straight on, in the voice of the one who knows, to describe the self as the sky, as what is never born and never destroyed. It also questions the use of scriptures, rituals and even meditation at the highest level. Do not take this as a dismissal of practice. It is describing the view from the summit, not the way up.

## Why it speaks in songs

Notice that the book is verse, and the verses are closer to songs than to lectures. They repeat, turn, and sometimes almost laugh at the listener's questions. An avadhuta does not argue, he sings what he sees. It is a little like Shankara's [Bhaja Govindam](/bhaja-govindam-shankaras-song-for-the-confused-mind/), but without the gentle advice: there, the teacher speaks to a confused man, and here the singer speaks as if no confusion remained anywhere. Reading it can feel like standing near a very large, very quiet fire. Some feel warmth, others feel that the ground is gone.

## Admired, and debated

The Avadhuta Gita has been admired by many Vedanta teachers over the years, and accounts say that Swami Vivekananda, among others, valued it. It has also been translated into English more than once. We would not claim particular lines of praise, as we cannot be sure of the exact words. What we can say is that readers have returned to it for the sheer clarity of its voice.

At the same time, serious teachers also mention its risk. A text that says "there is no sin, no virtue" can be misread by a restless mind as permission. That has happened. The text speaks from the state of a knower, and to a hearer in that state. Someone who still feels anger, hunger and fear has work to do.

## How to read it without confusion

- Read a few verses at a time, not as a book to finish.
- Keep a simpler Advaita text beside it, such as [Nirvana Shatakam](/nirvana-shatakam-i-am-not-the-mind-meaning/), and return to it when you are lost.
- Read the negations ("not this, not that") as pointing, not as rules of conduct.
- If possible, ask a teacher. If you cannot, sit with it and let some lines stay unclear.
- Keep your ordinary duties. The text is no excuse to neglect a family or a job.

A simple test many teachers offer: after reading, are you kinder and more at ease with people, or more scornful of them? If the second, put the book down for a while. The truth it points to should loosen the grip of pride, not feed it.

## Where this comes from

The Avadhuta Gita is a free-standing text attributed to Dattatreya, and is not part of the Mahabharata or the Ramayana. The Dattatreya stories of the twenty-four gurus come from the Bhagavata Purana (Skandha 11), where an avadhuta speaks to King Yadu. The two texts have a similar spirit but are different works. Editions and chapter counts differ, so use a reliable translation with a commentary.
