Every winter, in towns across Telangana, Andhra, Tamil Nadu and Karnataka, men and women appear in black or blue dhotis, with a tulasi or rudraksha mala at the neck. They are Ayyappa devotees, and they are walking towards a hill in Kerala. Who is the god they are going to, and why does he draw so many?

## Telugu and Malayalam names

In Telugu he is అయ్యప్ప, Ayyappa, and in Malayalam he is often called Ayyappan or Sabarimala Sastha. The name Harihara Putra points to his parents, Hara (Shiva) and Hari (Vishnu).

## The birth

Tradition holds that after the churning of the ocean, Vishnu took the form of Mohini, the enchantress, to share the nectar among the gods. Shiva, seeing that form, was moved, and from their union a child was born. The tale is told in Kerala and across the south, though details differ from teller to teller. The point most people take from it is that Ayyappa carries both the Shaiva and the Vaishnava streams in one child.

## Found in the forest

The child was left near the banks of the Pampa river, wearing a small bell around his neck. The king of Pandalam, who was out hunting and had no heir, found him. Because of the bell (mani) at his neck, the child was named Manikandan. The king took him home, and the queen raised him with love.

Later the queen had a son of her own. A minister, jealous of Manikandan, plotted against him. The queen was told to feign a headache that only the milk of a tigress could cure. Manikandan went into the forest to bring it.

## Mahishi

In the forest he met the demoness Mahishi, who had received a boon from Brahma that she could be defeated only by the son of Shiva and Vishnu. Manikandan defeated her. In the older telling, she then became a woman of great beauty and asked to marry him. He said he would marry only when devotees no longer came to Sabarimala. Mahishi waits at a shrine near the main temple, known as Malikappurathamma. This is the tradition; the story is retold in different versions in different places.

Manikandan returned riding a tiger, with tigresses following him. The king bowed down, realised who the child was, and asked how he could make amends. Ayyappa asked him to build a temple at Sabarimala.

## Vavar

On his way, tradition tells, Ayyappa befriended a Muslim warrior named Vavar. A small shrine to Vavar stands near the eighteen steps, and pilgrims greet him on the way. This is a beautiful part of the story. It shows that Sabarimala has long welcomed people regardless of faith, and many devotees value that.

## The eighteen steps

The golden steps, known as Pathinettam Padi, lead to the sanctum. Only those who have kept the 41-day vow and carry the irumudi bundle on the head climb them. There are many explanations of the number eighteen. Some say they stand for the five senses, the eight passions or mental states, the three qualities of nature (the gunas), plus knowledge and ignorance. Different teachers explain them differently, and nobody knows with certainty how the number came to be.

For how the vow is kept, read [Ayyappa Sabarimala: the 41-day vratam explained](/ayyappa-sabarimala-the-41-day-vratam-explained/).

## The Makaravilakku

On Makara Sankranti, in mid-January, devotees gather to see a light on the Ponnambalamedu hill, called the Makaravilakku, along with the Thiruvabharanam procession of jewels from Pandalam. Tradition regards the light as the divine presence. Over the years, there have been public debates about its nature, and officials of the temple board have said that the light is lit by people. Many devotees keep their faith in the sacred moment itself. It is best to approach the matter with honesty and respect, and we will not claim more than is known.

## The Sastha tradition

Ayyappa's story sits within a larger one. Sastha, a guardian deity, was worshipped across Kerala and Tamil Nadu long before Sabarimala became so famous, and many believe Ayyappa is a form of him. Tradition speaks of five main Sastha shrines of Kerala:

- Kulathupuzha, where Sastha appears as a child (Bala Sastha).
- Aryankavu, where he appears as a young man with his consort.
- Achankovil, where he is the householder with his wives.
- Aranmula, near the Pampa, associated with the Pandalam jewel procession.
- Sabarimala, where he sits as a celibate yogi, a naishtika brahmachari.

Seeing them together, you can read the life of one god in five stages, child, youth, householder, protector, ascetic. This is a way many devotees understand them, not a fixed scheme. Some lists substitute other temples.

## The pilgrim's road

The road to the hill is part of the story. Pilgrims cross the Pampa, climb through the forest, and reach the steps after a hard walk. Along the way, every pilgrim is addressed as Swami, whatever his age or position, and strangers greet one another with the same cry. The vow flattens rank: a clerk and a landlord carry the same bundle, eat the same food and sleep on the same floor. That equality may be one reason the pilgrimage has spread so widely beyond Kerala, to the Telugu country and beyond.

## Why do people go?

A pilgrim may say: because I promised. Or because my father went. Or because the forty-one days of plain living, with no anger and no alcohol, are a school for the mind. The slogan Swamiye Saranam Ayyappa means, in simple terms, Lord, I take refuge in you. The hill gives no guarantees about worldly life, but many walk away steadier than they came.
