*బలరాముడు కృష్ణుని అన్న, కురుక్షేత్ర యుద్ధంలో రెండు వైపులా తన శిష్యులు, బంధువులు ఉన్నారని పాల్గొనకుండా తీర్థయాత్రకు వెళ్లాడు. చివరి గదాయుద్ధంలో మాత్రం కోపంతో నాగలి ఎత్తాడు, తటస్థంగా ఉండటానికి వెల ఏమిటో ఈ కథ అడుగుతుంది.*

Think of a family quarrel in which both sides are people you love. Everyone looks at you to take a side, and the one thing you want is not to be in the room. That is where Balarama (బలరాముడు) stands as the Mahabharata war approaches.

## The elder brother

Balarama is Krishna's elder brother, usually pictured with a plough and a pestle, in pale blue or white. Many families read him as an incarnation of Shesha, the serpent on whom Vishnu rests; in some lists he is counted among the ten avatars. You can see where he fits in [the ten avatars of Vishnu](/dashavatara-the-ten-avatars-of-vishnu-explained/), and the lists differ on whether Balarama or the Buddha is named.

He is strong, direct, fond of drink in some tellings, and quick to feel slighted. He is also loyal to those he teaches. And that is the heart of the story.

## Two pupils

Balarama taught the use of the mace. Both Duryodhana and Bhima are said in tradition to have learned from him, and the epic says he had affection for both. Duryodhana was devoted to his teacher and, in many tellings, matched Bhima in the mace; Bhima, strong and honest, was loved for his heart. When Subhadra, his sister, was of marriageable age, Balarama is said to have favoured Duryodhana as her groom. Krishna quietly supported Arjuna taking her away, and Balarama's anger passed only when Krishna spoke to him.

So when war came, Balarama was not choosing between a good side and a bad one. He was choosing between two sons he had taught.

## Why he left

In Vyasa's account, as people recount it, Balarama let it be known that he would not fight on either side. When the Pandavas and Kauravas went to Dvaraka to ask for help, Krishna gave them the choice of himself unarmed or his army; Balarama, it is said, told Krishna he had tried to dissuade him from supporting the Pandavas, and when Krishna would not be moved, Balarama said that he would go away. He set off on a pilgrimage along the Sarasvati and other holy places, visiting rivers and tirthas while the two armies gathered. Some tellings give the length of his journey in days; the details vary, so you should not treat the number as certain.

It is a gentle kind of refusal. He does not condemn either side. He simply walks away from the field, and that is both his gentleness and his limit.

How should we judge it? Perhaps the honest answer is that love made him unable to see which of two pupils was wrong. Many of us know that feeling in smaller ways: a brother, a friend, a colleague, in a quarrel where we cannot bear to name who is at fault.

## The return and the mace fight

Balarama comes back as the war ends. He arrives at Kurukshetra just as Bhima and Duryodhana are about to fight with maces. He sits down to watch. Then Bhima strikes Duryodhana on the thigh, a blow the rules of the mace forbid, since the fight is meant to be above the waist. Krishna had hinted at this, and Bhima had once vowed to break that thigh because of what Duryodhana did in Draupadi's humiliation.

Balarama cried out and lifted his plough. His anger at the foul blow was real. Krishna stepped to him and reminded him of Bhima's vow, and that Duryodhana had been party to many adharmic acts. Balarama was not fully satisfied. According to tradition he left, saying he could not approve, though he did not punish Bhima. Tellings vary on his exact words.

## What his absence asks

A good teacher asks a hard question here. Is neutrality a virtue, or a way to keep our hands clean?

Balarama's walk away can be admired as a refusal to harm his kin. Yet the epic shows that someone had to stand. Krishna, his brother, stood on the side of dharma and refused to bear arms. Balarama stood on neither, and what he feels in the end is anger, since his love could not stop the injustice that his silence did nothing to prevent. The text does not condemn him. It lets us watch him and decide.

Notice, too, how the epic leaves him human. He is never made a villain for his distance, and never a hero for his anger. He is a man with a plough and a large heart, standing in a story too big for his kind of love. If you are interested in another figure who stood apart and watched the war, [Sanjaya](/sanjaya-the-man-who-saw-the-war-from-a-distance/) offers a very different answer.

## Where this comes from

The pilgrimage and the mace duel are in the Shalya and Gada Yuddha sections of Vyasa's Mahabharata (the Shalya Parva), where Balarama's tirtha-yatra along the Sarasvati is told at some length. His affection for Duryodhana appears in the Adi Parva account of Subhadra's marriage. The Bhagavata Purana tells of his death, and of Shesha leaving his body. Tellings differ in small points, especially his words during the duel; folk and temple traditions, including those of Odisha where he is worshipped as Balabhadra, add further detail.

## A question to carry

When two people you love are in conflict, what do you tell yourself about staying out of it, and who, quietly, pays for your silence?
