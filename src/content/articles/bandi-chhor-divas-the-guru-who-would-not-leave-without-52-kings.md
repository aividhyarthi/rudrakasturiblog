Picture a prison gate that has just been opened, and a man who says: I will walk out only if everyone walks out with me. Who does that? Not many of us, if we are honest. Yet this is the picture Sikhs carry on the night of Deepavali, which they call Bandi Chhor Divas.

## Who was Guru Hargobind?

Guru Hargobind (1595 to 1644) was the sixth of the ten Sikh Gurus. He became Guru as a boy, after his father Guru Arjan was put to death in Mughal custody in 1606. He is remembered for wearing two swords, one for spiritual authority (piri) and one for worldly responsibility (miri), and for raising the Akal Takht in Amritsar, facing the Harmandir Sahib, the Golden Temple.

## The story at Gwalior

Sikh tradition tells that the Mughal emperor Jahangir had the Guru held at Gwalior Fort, which was used as a state prison. Among the prisoners were a number of princes and hill rulers, commonly counted as 52.

When the order came for the Guru to be released, he is said to have refused to leave unless the other prisoners were freed too. The emperor, tradition says, put a condition: only those who could hold on to the Guru's garment as he passed through the gate would go free. The Guru had a cloak made with 52 long tassels or strings, and each prince took one and walked out with him. For this he is called Bandi Chhor, the Liberator of the Captive.

He then travelled to Amritsar. Tradition holds that he arrived on the night of Diwali, and the people lit lamps all around the Harmandir Sahib to welcome him. That is why the pool of the Golden Temple is lined with lights on this night even today.

Think about what this asks. The Guru was already free. He could have taken the gate and gone home, and nobody would have blamed him. The story insists that real freedom is not complete while a stranger is still behind the wall. That is the thought Sikhs return to each year, and it is the reason the day is called one of liberation rather than only of arrival. Many Sikhs also say the cloak is a picture of the Guru's care, with each tassel a person he would not forget.

## What is history and what is tradition

This is where honesty matters, and where I would ask you not to let any website, including ours, flatten the details.

- Reasonably firm: Guru Hargobind was held at Gwalior by Jahangir's order, and was later released and returned to the Punjab. Early Sikh writing close to his time, such as the works of Bhai Gurdas, refers to the release.
- Debated: the length of the detention, the reason for it, and the date. Many accounts give about 1619; some older Sikh tellings say longer, and some modern historians suggest shorter. Please say about 1619, not a certain year.
- Tradition and memory: the number 52 and the cloak with 52 tassels, the condition about holding the garment, and the arrival on the very night of Diwali. These are cherished, and they are not less true for being held as tradition, but they cannot be proved from a document.
- Also told, with varied detail: that a respected Muslim saint of Lahore, Mian Mir, and a Mughal official spoke on the Guru's behalf. Tellings differ on who did what.

## Why Sikhs mark Diwali at all

Sikh tradition holds that Diwali was already a day when Sikhs gathered before the Gwalior story, from the time of Guru Amar Das, the third Guru. Bandi Chhor Divas added a deeper memory to an existing day. Some Sikhs also stress that the day is not about Lakshmi or Rama, and it is not meant to blur the faiths; it is the Guru's own day. A Hindu neighbour lighting a lamp the same night and a Sikh family lighting one at the gurdwara are doing something different and something shared: lighting a lamp against the dark.

## The custom today

At the Golden Temple, thousands of lamps and lights glow around the sarovar, with fireworks in the sky and langar served all night. In gurdwaras elsewhere, including those in Hyderabad, Chennai and Nanded (where the Sikh centre of Hazur Sahib stands), there are kirtan, prayer and langar, and homes are lit. Sweets are shared, and some Sikhs choose to skip loud fireworks. If you are a non-Sikh visitor, the gurdwara welcomes you: cover your head, remove your shoes and sit with the community. ## Where this comes from

The story is carried by Sikh tradition, including later chronicles such as Gurbilas literature written in the following centuries, and by the living memory of the Panth. Contemporary Persian records of Jahangir's court say little about it. Where those sources differ in detail, we have said so above. I am not certain of the exact titles for each chronicle, and a Sikh scholar or granthi will give you a more careful answer than any short article.
