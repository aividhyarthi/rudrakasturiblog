If you have ever been to a South Indian concert or a temple event, there is a good chance you have heard this one. The opening line is easy to remember and the tune is gentle. But the words are not.

## The story
The tradition says Shankara was walking through the streets of Varanasi with his disciples when he saw an old man repeating the grammar rules of Panini, memorising a formula called *dukrin karane*. Shankara, moved by the sight, burst into song. The old scholar, the story says, was a man who had spent his whole life on grammar and never turned to God.

The verse that came out was *Bhaja Govindam*.

## The first verse
*Bhaja Govindam, bhaja Govindam, Govindam bhaja mudhamate,*
*Samprapte sannihite kale, nahi nahi rakshati dukrin karane.*

In plain words: Worship Govinda, worship Govinda, you fool. When the appointed hour comes, these grammar rules will not save you.

It is blunt, and it is meant to be. Shankara is not scolding grammar. He is saying that if all you have done is collect knowledge, you are not ready for the question of death.

## What else it says
The hymn has 31 verses, and many of them are about the same thing: how quickly things pass and how easily we cling.

- *Maa kuru dhana jana yauvana garvam, harati nimeshat kalah sarvam*: do not be proud of wealth, friends or youth. Time takes it all in a moment.
- *Nalini dala gata jalam ati taralam, tadvat jivitam ati shayachapalam*: life is as unsteady as a drop of water on a lotus leaf.
- Another verse speaks of a man who is busy in youth with love, in middle age with wealth and in old age with worry, and never has time for God.

The hymn is also called *Moha Mudgara*, the hammer for confusion, because it is meant to knock a hole in attachment.

## Who is it for?
The funny thing is that the hymn is not written only for monks. It is for people who are busy with ordinary things: jobs, children, money, plans. It asks one question again and again: what are you going to do with the time you have?

## How to use it
Many people sing it in the morning or at the end of the day. Listening to M. S. Subbulakshmi's recording is a popular way to learn it. You do not need Sanskrit to feel it. Read one verse, with its meaning, and sit with it for a minute.
