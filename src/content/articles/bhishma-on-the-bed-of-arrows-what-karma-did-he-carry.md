Bhishma had the boon of choosing the hour of his death. He chose to wait on a bed of arrows through fifty-eight days until Uttarayana, when the sun turns north, and he left the world on the day now known as Bhishma Ashtami in the month of Magha.

## The question he asked
A popular story, told in many homes though not found in the critical text of the Mahabharata, says Bhishma asked Krishna: in this life I remember no wrong deed, so why am I in such pain? Krishna asked him to look back across a hundred births. Bhishma then recalled that as a young prince he had once flicked a snake off the road with the tip of his arrow. It fell on a thorny bush and suffered for days as it died. The arrows he now lay on, the story says, were that thorn bush returning.

## What the epic itself says
Many readers point to something heavier. When Draupadi was dragged into the assembly hall and humiliated, Bhishma, who knew the law better than anyone present, gave only a half-answer and did not stop it. The epic never lets that silence pass quietly: he is questioned about it, and he admits his helplessness as a servant of the Kuru throne. Staying silent when wrong is done is a kind of action too.

## What the story teaches
- **Small cruelties are never small.** The snake story is about carelessness towards a living creature.
- **Silence has a cost.** The more power and knowledge we have, the more silence counts as a choice.
- **Even in pain, he teaches.** From the bed of arrows Bhishma gives the Pandavas the long teachings of the Shanti and Anushasana Parvas, including the Vishnu Sahasranama. Facing the result of karma with dignity is itself a lesson.

## For today
Treat the small creatures and the small people around you with care, and speak up when you see something wrong, even if it is uncomfortable. It is the easiest karma to start fixing today.

## Who was Bhishma?
To understand what he carried, it helps to remember his story. He was born Devavrata, son of King Shantanu and the river goddess Ganga. When his father wished to marry Satyavati, her family wanted her children to inherit the throne. To make that possible, the young prince gave up his claim to the kingdom and took a vow never to marry. For that terrible vow the world called him Bhishma, the one of fearful promise. This is told in the Adi Parva.

His father, pleased with him, gave him the boon of choosing the hour of his own death. That is why he could lie on the arrows and wait.

Notice what this means. His whole life was built on keeping a promise. And yet the promise tied him, years later, to a throne that was doing wrong.

## The pillow of arrows
In the Bhishma Parva, Bhishma falls on the tenth day of the war, pierced by so many arrows that his body does not touch the ground. His head hangs down. Soft pillows are brought, and he sets them aside. He asks Arjuna for a fitting pillow, and Arjuna supports his head with arrows. When Bhishma is thirsty, Arjuna sends an arrow into the earth and water rises for him.

It is a quiet, strong scene. The great warrior does not ask for comfort. He asks to be treated as a warrior to the end, and the young man he once opposed is the one who serves him.

## Questions people ask
**Is the snake story in the Mahabharata?** As the article says, it is a popular tradition and not part of the critical text. Enjoy it as a teaching tale, and do not quote it as scripture.

**Did Bhishma suffer because of one past action?** The epic gives no single cause. Karma in the Gita is not a simple ledger. See [Karma and free will](/karma-and-free-will-what-the-gita-says/) for how Krishna speaks about it.

**Why did he wait fifty-eight days?** He had chosen to leave when the sun turned north, the period of Uttarayana, which the tradition regards as an auspicious time to depart.

**What is Bhishma Ashtami?** The day in Magha that is remembered as his departure. Some families offer water and sesame to him in remembrance. Practices vary, so follow your own family's custom.

## Why this story stays with us
Many people ask the same question Bhishma is said to have asked: I tried to be good, so why this? The honest answer in the epic is that being good and being blameless are not the same. Even the best of us can leave a duty undone.

That is not a reason for despair. Bhishma does not turn bitter on the bed of arrows. He turns teacher. He spends his last days helping the next generation understand dharma, and that, perhaps, is the real answer to what to do with the weight we carry.
