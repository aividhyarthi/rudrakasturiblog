Have you ever had a teacher who said very little, and yet you came away knowing something you could not name? There was a look, a pause, a way of sitting beside you. Hold that memory, because that is where this form of Shiva begins.

## The image

Look closely at a Dakshinamurthy figure in a South Indian temple. Shiva sits under a banyan tree, one leg folded, the other resting on a small figure of ignorance, Apasmara. His hand makes the chin mudra, with thumb and forefinger joined. In other hands there may be a rosary, a flame, a scroll. Around him sit four young sages, the Kumaras: Sanaka, Sanandana, Sanatana and Sanatkumara. They are the old students, and he is the young teacher, as the tradition delights to say, since the teacher is younger than the pupils in appearance.

He is facing south. In Sanskrit dakshina means both south and the right, and also the gift given to a teacher. The name is a small lesson in itself.

## Teaching in silence

The tradition holds that the Kumaras had learned every scripture and still felt unsettled. They came to Shiva, and he said nothing. In that silence, their doubts fell away. Many verses say that when the teacher is silent the pupil's questions go quiet too.

Why silence? Because the truth he points to is not an object that words can hold. Words can point, as a finger points to the moon, but the moon has to be seen by the one looking. If this seems strange to you, you are in good company. Even the learned said so.

## What Shankara's hymn says

The Dakshinamurthy Stotram is attributed to Adi Shankara (see [his story](/adi-shankaracharya-the-boy-from-kaladi-who-walked-across-india/)). It is a short hymn in ten verses, with a closing verse of praise. In paraphrase, it says:

- The world is like a city seen in a mirror. It appears within us, though it seems to lie outside, and the self is the mirror in which it shines.
- Just as a seed holds the great tree, this whole universe sleeps in the self before it appears.
- A person wakes from dream and knows that the dream-world was contained in the mind. So too in waking life.
- The Lord shines as the one who knows the waking, dream and deep sleep alike, and is not touched by any.

The hymn's whole point is to turn the mind back towards the knower. Shankara's teaching is Advaita, non-duality, and the Stotram is one of its most beloved doors. Reading it slowly, you may find a verse or two that stays. You do not have to understand it all at once.

## Why Thursday, and why gurus

In the Indian week, Thursday, Guruvaram, belongs to Brihaspati, the guru of the gods, and many people honour their own teachers on that day. Dakshinamurthy, as the first guru, is a natural figure for the day. In many Shiva temples there will be a small crowd in front of his niche on Thursday, with yellow flowers, a garland of chana dal, and a ghee lamp. Our piece on [Thursday and the guru](/thursday-is-for-the-guru-how-to-worship-on-guruvaram/) describes the home worship.

Students bring their books to him before exams. Parents bring children before the first day of school. Whether or not that helps marks, it teaches something good: that learning begins in humility.

## Where to find him in a temple

Walk around any Shiva temple sanctum in the South and look at the south wall, outside the sanctum. In a niche, you will usually find Dakshinamurthy, carved in stone. This is a rule you will see in Chidambaram, Madurai, Thanjavur and the smaller village shrines alike. Pradakshina is done clockwise, and many devotees pause before him to offer a lamp or a small prayer before continuing.

The practice is simple:

1. Stand before him quietly. Do not rush.
2. Join your hands and say the short prayer, or only his name.
3. Offer yellow flowers or a lamp if the temple allows.
4. Stay for a minute in silence. That is his teaching.

A beloved verse, which families recite, begins by calling him the one who is the form of the guru and the form of Shiva. If you do not know it, the name is enough: Dakshinamurthaye namah.

## What the details of the image say

Temple sculptors and teachers have read nearly every part of the figure. The banyan tree, with its seed so small and tree so vast, is said to stand for the world growing from a single source. The figure of Apasmara beneath his foot is ignorance, forgetfulness, which is not killed but held down. The chin mudra, thumb and forefinger joined, is often read as the individual self joined with the supreme. The fire, rosary and book in the other hands stand for the three ways in which knowledge is passed on: by the flame of insight, by repeated practice, and by study.

Other forms of the image exist. In some he plays the veena and is called Veenadhara Dakshinamurthy, teacher of music. In others he is Yoga Dakshinamurthy, deep in meditation, or Medha Dakshinamurthy, the giver of intellect. A few temples in Tamil Nadu, such as Alangudi near Kumbakonam, are especially famed for him, and families who go there on Thursdays are many. Do check local customs before travelling, as timings and offerings differ.

## A small question to take away

If a teacher can teach by silence, what are we doing when we are in a hurry to learn? The tradition does not scold you. It only says, sit, and let the mind settle, and perhaps you will see what the Kumaras saw.
