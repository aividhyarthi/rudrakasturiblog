Imagine you have to name your teachers. You would begin with the school, the coaching class, a parent. Now imagine the list includes a pigeon, a river and a woman most people would not speak to.

That is the list of the Avadhuta.

## The story
In the eleventh canto of the Bhagavata Purana, King Yadu meets a young, radiant wanderer who seems entirely at ease. He asks: *You are not rich, you have no family, you have no teacher that I can see. Where does your peace come from?*

The avadhuta, whom the tradition identifies with Dattatreya, the son of Atri and Anasuya, answers: *I have twenty-four gurus. Let me tell you what each taught me.*

## A few of them
- **The earth** taught him patience, because it bears everything that is done to it and keeps giving.
- **The air** taught him non-attachment: it moves through the foulest and the sweetest places and carries away nothing.
- **The sky** taught him that the Self is untouched, like space that holds clouds and does not become them.
- **Water** taught him clarity and gentleness. **Fire** taught him to burn away what is false.
- **The moon,** which seems to grow and shrink though it never changes, taught him that change belongs to the body, not the self.
- **The sun** taught him to give without being touched by what he gives.
- **The pigeon** taught him the pain of clinging. The story is of a pigeon who flew into a hunter's net to join her trapped family and died there.
- **The python,** which waits for what comes, taught him contentment.
- **The sea** taught him depth and calm, however many rivers pour in.
- **The moth** taught him the danger of being drawn by a pleasant sight. **The honeybee** taught him to take only a little from each flower, and to beware of hoarding.
- **Pingala,** a courtesan who waited all night for a client who never came, and then, tired, let go of the hope and slept in peace. She taught him that *giving up expectation is happiness.*
- **A child** taught him to be carefree. **An arrow-maker,** so absorbed in his work that he did not notice the king's procession, taught him single-minded attention.
- **A spider** and a **wasp** taught him about creation and about becoming what one meditates on.

## What this means for us
We often say, *I wish I had a guru.* The story answers gently: the world is already full of teachers, if you are ready to learn. The pigeon is in the garden. The river is at the edge of town. Your own tiredness is a teacher.

It does not replace a human guru. The tradition itself values one. But it says that the *student's attitude* is what makes anything a teacher.

## How Dattatreya is shown
In pictures, Dattatreya has three heads, for Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva, and six arms. Behind him is a cow, which stands for the earth or Kamadhenu, and four dogs, which are read as the four Vedas. The images say that the teacher of all teachers is attended by what we call ordinary animals. It is the same lesson as the twenty-four gurus.

## The Datta tradition
Dattatreya is especially honoured in the Datta Sampradaya of Maharashtra, Karnataka and Andhra. Its great figures include Shripada Shrivallabha and Narasimha Saraswati (fourteenth to fifteenth century), whose story is told in the *Gurucharitra*. Ganagapur in Karnataka and Audumbar on the Krishna river in Maharashtra are major places of worship. The common chant is *Digambara, Digambara, Shripada Vallabha Digambara*.

## Why the story of the 24 gurus still matters
We live surrounded by instruction, and often feel that we have no teacher. This story says that the shortage is not of teachers, but of attention. The moth taught the Avadhuta not to be drawn blindly by a pleasing sight, the bee to take only a little, and Pingala that the end of expectation is the beginning of peace. Those are lessons our phones could use.

## A small exercise
Today, pick one of the twenty-four. Look for it. Notice how the sky is wide and does not hold on to the clouds. See how the water in your glass takes the shape of the vessel. Ask: *what is this telling me?*

## Dattatreya today
Dattatreya Jayanti falls on Margashira Pournami, and the saint is especially honoured in Maharashtra, Karnataka and Andhra, and at temples such as Ganagapur. Thursday is his day, along with other gurus.

## A story inside the story
Pingala's story is the one many readers remember. She was a courtesan who sat up all night waiting for a customer who never came, and as dawn broke, she gave up the hope and, the Bhagavata says, slept peacefully. *Asha hi paramam duhkham, nairasyam paramam sukham*: "Hope is the greatest pain, and giving up hope is the greatest ease." The Avadhuta, a wandering sage, calls her his teacher. The point is not to despise hope. It is that clinging to one particular outcome is what hurts.
