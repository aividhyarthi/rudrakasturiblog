**Source:** Mahabharata, Vana Parva (the Vyadha Gita, chapters 198 to 206), told by the sage Markandeya to Yudhishthira.

You can be very learned and still not understand something that a person who cuts meat for a living knows well. That is the claim of one of the most surprising stories in the Mahabharata.

## The proud Brahmin
Kaushika was a young Brahmin who had left home to practise austerities. He was devoted to his study and prayer, and he had grown proud. One day, as he sat under a tree, a crane flew overhead and dropped its droppings on him. Furious, he looked at the bird, and in the story, it fell dead.

He felt triumphant. He had power. Later that day, he went begging for food at a house, and the woman of the house asked him to wait while she attended to her husband, who had come home hungry. Kaushika, annoyed, glared at her.

She said calmly: *I am not a crane, sir.*

He was stunned. How did she know? She told him that she had gained her knowledge not through tapas but through simple devotion to her duty. She served her husband and her home, and that was her yoga. She also told him that he did not yet know what dharma was. If he wished to learn, he should go to a butcher in the city of Mithila, who would teach him.

## The butcher
Kaushika went. He found the shop of Dharmavyadha, a seller of meat, in the crowded market. The butcher saw him and greeted him: *I know you have come because the lady sent you.* The Brahmin was startled again.

The butcher took him home and showed him what his life was. His parents were old, and he served them with the greatest devotion. He fed them and bathed them. He told Kaushika that this was his highest worship.

## What he taught
What follows, in the long section called the **Vyadha Gita**, is a discussion of dharma that is among the best in the epic. The butcher says, in effect:
- **Do your own work honestly, whatever it is.** He had inherited his occupation, and he did it without cruelty or greed, and he did not apologise for it.
- **Your parents are your first gods.** Serving them is the first duty, ahead of austerities in the forest.
- **Dharma is subtle.** There are no simple rules, and one must judge with care.
- **The pure person is not defined by birth or work.** He says that a person is seen in his conduct.
- **Truth, restraint and compassion** are the root of everything.

He also says something that is hard to read now: that he does not kill animals himself, but sells what others have killed, and that he regrets that his livelihood involves violence. The story does not resolve all the questions it raises, and it is wise to read it as a conversation, not a rule.

## Kaushika's reaction
The Brahmin was moved. He saw that he had left his own old parents to pursue his austerities, thinking it a high path. He went home, served them and understood what the butcher had taught.

## Why this story matters
It turned the usual order upside down. The teacher is the lowest in the social order. The student is the highest. The lesson is that learning and character do not run along the lines of birth. It is a story that has been quoted by reformers over the centuries, including in the Bhakti tradition.

It also has a message for those of us who feel that our spiritual practice has to be *special*. The woman at the door and the butcher in the market both said: your daily duty, done with love, is already the practice.

## The wife as the first teacher
Do not skip the woman at the door. In the epic she is the first to speak to Kaushika, and she does so with perfect calm. She tells him that she has not studied the Vedas or done austerities. She serves her husband and her home. And she says plainly that this is her yoga, and that it has given her a clear mind. The epic gives the same weight to what she says as to the long teaching of the butcher. Many readers see a quiet rebuke to those who think that spiritual progress must happen away from daily life. In the *Vyadha Gita*, the world of work and family is not a place you leave to find truth. It is where it is found.

## A closer look at the teaching
The butcher's discourse is long, but a few strands run through it. First, that the world rests on dharma, and that every person's dharma depends on their situation. Second, that one should be honest, free of anger and not greedy. Third, that the senses are like horses, and a person must hold the reins. Fourth, that the soul does not die when the body does. And finally, that serving those who raised you is the greatest of worships. If you ever want to read it, it takes about an hour, and English translations are widely available.

## For modern readers
- **Respect your work.** Whatever you do, do it honestly and with care.
- **Look after those who raised you.** It is often the quietest and hardest duty.
- **Watch for spiritual pride.** Kaushika's anger at the crane was the sign of it.
- **Learn from whoever can teach you.** The teacher may be at a market stall.

## A thought
The old teachers say a guru can appear anywhere. The question is whether we are humble enough to ask.
