Navaratri begins, and in some house on your street someone is sitting by a lamp with a thick, worn book, reading in a low steady voice. It is the Durga Saptashati, and it has been read this way for many centuries. Perhaps you have always wondered what is in it.

## What it is

Saptashati means seven hundred. The text is a set of about seven hundred verses in thirteen chapters, and it is part of the Markandeya Purana. It is also known as the Devi Mahatmya, the glory of the Goddess, and as Chandi or Durga Path. For devotees of the Mother, it is the central scripture, as the Gita is for those who love Krishna. Our piece on [Navadurga](/navadurga-the-nine-forms-of-the-goddess-and-their-days/) gives the nine nights its home.

Counts of verses vary slightly by edition, since some count the introductory lines and prose passages differently. So 700 is the traditional name rather than an exact tally.

## Three stories

The thirteen chapters are in three parts, often called the three charitas or episodes:

1. **Madhu and Kaitabha** (chapter 1). While Vishnu sleeps on the cosmic waters, two demons arise from his ear and threaten Brahma. Brahma praises the Goddess as Yoganidra, who withdraws her hold on Vishnu. He wakes and defeats the demons.
2. **Mahishasura** (chapters 2 to 4). The gods, defeated by the buffalo demon, gather their radiance, and from it the Goddess appears, armed with their weapons. She slays Mahishasura. The gods then praise her in a famous hymn.
3. **Shumbha and Nishumbha** (chapters 5 to 13). Two brothers claim the rule of the three worlds. The Goddess meets their generals, including Chanda and Munda, the great Raktabija, and finally the brothers themselves. In this section Kali, Chamunda and the Matrikas appear.

Details differ between tellings, but the shape is the same: when the world cannot protect itself, the Mother comes.

## The extra hymns around it

A reading usually has other pieces before and after.

- The **Devi Suktam** is a hymn from the Rigveda in which the Goddess speaks of herself as the power behind all things. It is often read after the main text.
- The **Kavacha** is a protective armour of the Goddess's names laid over each part of the body.
- **Argala** and **Kilaka** are two short stotras, said to open and unlock the main reading.

In many families the order is Kavacha, Argala, Kilaka, then the thirteen chapters, then the Devi Suktam and a closing prayer asking pardon for any faults in the reading. Local customs vary. Follow your family's order if you have one.

## How families read it in Navaratri

There are several common plans:

- **One chapter a day** over thirteen days, which some stretch from the day of Pratipada.
- **Nine days:** a few chapters each day, so the text is complete by Navami.
- **Three days:** the three episodes, one each, for those with time.
- **Full reading in a single day**, which often takes several hours, usually with a priest.

For a first attempt, a short practice is wiser than a heroic one. Light a lamp, clean the place, sit facing east or north, and read aloud or softly. If Sanskrit is hard, you may read a transliteration in Telugu or Tamil script, or a translation. Many people listen to a recording and follow along. If you stumble, simply go on.

For dates, check the [festivals page](/festivals/). A few notes on care:

- Do not worry about rules you have not been told. Sincerity is the base.
- If you have a family guru or elder, ask them about any special practice.
- Some prefer to avoid reading when ritually unclean in the family's custom; this differs widely, and we will not decide for you.

## Who tells the story

The Devi Mahatmya is framed as a conversation. The sage Markandeya is the narrator in the Purana, and within the text a king named Suratha and a merchant named Samadhi, both robbed of their kingdom and family, come to a sage called Medhas. They ask why they still feel attached to the very people who wronged them. The sage answers by telling them of the Goddess as Mahamaya, the power that binds even the wise, and then of her three appearances. The two then worship her, and tradition says each receives what he sought.

That frame matters. It tells you the book is for someone who is troubled in the heart, not only a collection of battles. You may find, reading slowly, that the first chapter speaks more directly than you expected.

## What the demons are

Tradition's inner reading is that the demons are not only outside. Madhu and Kaitabha are said to be the pulls of attachment and aversion that rise from the mind of the sleeping. Mahishasura, the buffalo, stands for dull, stubborn ego. Shumbha and Nishumbha are read as I-ness and mine-ness, with Raktabija, whose every drop of blood makes another demon, as desire that multiplies whenever you strike at it. Chanda and Munda are anger and wild thought.

Viewed this way, the Goddess is the awakened power that does not fight with a human's tired will, but with her own. Teachers often say the battle is fought in the reader.

## One last word

You do not need to finish the whole book to have read it. Many families only begin, and return to it year after year, reading a little further each Navaratri.
