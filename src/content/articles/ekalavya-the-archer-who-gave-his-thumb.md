**Source:** Mahabharata, Adi Parva (the section on the training of the princes). The remark about Krishna and Ekalavya is from Drona Parva.

Some stories in the Mahabharata make us feel proud. This is one that makes many readers uneasy, and I think it should.

## The boy at the gate
Ekalavya was the son of Hiranyadhanus, a chief of the Nishadas, a forest people. He heard of Drona, the great teacher of archery who taught the Kuru princes, and went to him asking to be taught. Drona refused. He taught only the princes of the Kuru house, and he did not take a Nishada as a pupil.

Ekalavya went back to the forest. He made an image of Drona from clay, set it up, and practised before it as if it were the teacher himself. Day after day, with total devotion, he trained. In time his skill grew until it matched that of any of the princes.

## The dog and the arrows
One day the princes were hunting in the forest, and their dog wandered off and found Ekalavya. The dog began to bark at the strange dark figure. Ekalavya, without so much as hurting it, fired seven arrows into its mouth so that it could not bark and ran back to the princes with its mouth full of arrows.

The princes were stunned. They followed the dog's trail and found the man who had done it. When they asked, he said he was Drona's pupil.

## The fee
Arjuna, who had been promised by Drona that no one would ever be a better archer than him, went to his teacher in distress. Drona went to see Ekalavya himself. Ekalavya bowed down, honoured him as his teacher and offered whatever he wished. Drona said, *then give me your right thumb as my fee.*

Without hesitation, Ekalavya cut off his thumb and gave it to him. The text says that afterward he could still shoot, but never again with his earlier speed.

## How do we read it?
It is a story that has been read in many ways, and I do not think the Mahabharata wants us to pick one.
- **As devotion.** Ekalavya's guru-bhakti is among the great examples of surrender to a teacher.
- **As injustice.** A teacher who refused a pupil because of his birth, and then took away his skill to keep a promise to another pupil, is hard to defend. Many readers today see this as the epic's own indictment of a system of unfair access.
- **As tragedy.** Both the giver and the taker lose something. Drona's reputation in the epic is never entirely clean after this.

The epic itself does not tell us what to think. It tells the story, and lets the discomfort stand. That, I think, is part of its honesty.

A later passage in Drona Parva has Krishna listing, among those whom he removed to protect Arjuna's cause, Ekalavya. That is how the tradition remembers the sequel: the story does not end with the thumb.

## What can we take from it?
- **Talent without access is not enough.** Ekalavya shows that a gift can grow without a teacher, and that it still needs recognition.
- **A teacher's responsibility is greater than a teacher's promise.** Drona kept a vow to one pupil at the cost of another's life's work.
- **Devotion needs a worthy object.** The Gita does not ask us to give anything to anyone just because they ask.
- **Some stories are meant to be uncomfortable.** If it troubles you, you are reading it correctly.

## A modern thought
Ekalavya's name is used today for schools for tribal children, such as the Eklavya Model Residential Schools, which is a way of trying to finish what the story left undone.
