*గాంధారి తన భర్త అంధుడని తెలిసి తన కళ్ళకు గంతలు కట్టుకుంది. ఆ నిర్ణయం ఆమె ప్రేమ, కానీ కొడుకులను సరిదిద్దే అవకాశాన్ని కూడా కోల్పోయేలా చేసిందని చాలామంది భావిస్తారు.*

Imagine being a young bride, told on the wedding day that your husband cannot see. What do you do with that? Gandhari (గాంధారి) answered with a cloth. She bound her own eyes, and kept them bound for the rest of her married life.

## The choice

The epic says Gandhari, princess of Gandhara, was married to Dhritarashtra at Bhishma's insistence, and her brother Shakuni brought her to Hastinapura. On learning her husband was blind, she covered her eyes. The text presents this as her own decision, not a rule imposed on her. A short account of her brother is in [Shakuni, the man who never forgave](/shakuni-the-man-who-never-forgave/).

It is easy to read this as simple sacrifice, and many do. But sit with it a little longer. She was a woman of great spiritual power, remembered for her tapas and her truthfulness. She was not meek. This was a deliberate act.

## What does it mean?

Tradition and later commentary give more than one reading, and it helps to hold them side by side.

- **Devotion.** She would not see what her husband could not. The most common reading, and the one the epic's own words support.
- **Solidarity.** Her marriage would be a shared life, not a life in which she had one more gift than he did.
- **A burden.** Some readers ask whether, with open eyes, she might have seen her son Duryodhana more clearly and said to him what the king would not.

That last reading is an interpretation, not a verse. But it is a real question. The mother who sees is often the only person in the house who can say a hard thing.

## When she did look

The epic does record that after the war, in the Stri Parva, Gandhari came to the field and saw the dead, through her power of inner sight, and her grief was overwhelming. She spoke to Krishna.

Her words are among the most direct in the whole epic. She said that he, with all his power, had let the families kill each other when he could have prevented it. And she cursed him: that thirty-six years later his own people, the Yadavas, would destroy one another. Krishna, the epic says, accepted it. You can read what happened next in [Gandhari's curse and Krishna](/gandharis-curse-and-krishna-karma-of-the-yadavas/).

The tradition also says that when she looked at Yudhishthira, her gaze, even through the cloth, damaged his toenails. Hold that gently as tradition; the point is the force of a mother's sorrow.

## The later story

There is a popular tale that Gandhari opened her eyes once and let the light of her tapas fall on Duryodhana's body so that it became like iron, and that Krishna told him to cover himself, so one part remained weak. This is a later folk and literary tradition. I have not seen it in Vyasa's text, and tellings differ on the details, so I would call it a beautiful addition and no more.

## What it asks of us

Gandhari's story is uncomfortable because both of the easy readings are true. Loyalty can be love. Loyalty can also mean not looking at what is in front of you.

How many of us bind our eyes in small ways? A relative's drinking, a child's cruelty to a friend, an employer's shortcut. We call it peace, or respect, or not interfering. Sometimes it is that. And sometimes it is the cloth.

## Her tapas and her speech

It is easy to forget that Gandhari was not only a grieving mother. The epic describes her as a woman of great ascetic power, whose words carried weight. When Duryodhana came to her before the war, asking for her blessing of victory, she is said to have answered that victory would be where dharma is. That reply, in many tellings, is one of the clearest statements of her character: a mother who loved her son and would not lie to him.

This matters for how we read the blindfold. She was not blind to the truth about her sons. She was a woman who saw clearly and spoke honestly, and who also lived in the dark by choice. That is a painful pairing, and the epic leaves it unresolved.

## A way to sit with her

If you wish to remember Gandhari at home, there is no fixed rite in the epic for it, and I will not invent one. Some families simply read the Stri Parva passages during a quiet evening, or light a lamp and think of mothers who have buried their children. Others do not remember her at all. Tradition varies, and nothing here is required.

What you can do is notice, today, one person who is choosing not to see something, and ask whether you can love them enough to describe it gently. The epic does not claim this would have changed Hastinapura. It only asks if we are willing to try.

## Where this comes from

Gandhari's marriage and the blindfold are in the Adi Parva of Vyasa's Mahabharata. Her grief, her speech to Krishna and her curse are in the Stri Parva. The Telugu Andhra Mahabharatam follows this story. The story of making Duryodhana's body strong is not in the critical edition as far as I know; it belongs to later retellings, and regional versions differ.

What is the one thing in your own house that you have decided not to see, and who is paying for that decision?
