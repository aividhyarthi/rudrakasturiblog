The Stri Parva of the Mahabharata contains one of its most searching scenes: a grieving mother and the one she holds responsible.

## The mother
Gandhari, who had chosen to blindfold herself for life out of love for her blind husband, lost all one hundred of her sons in the war. Walking the battlefield with Krishna, she was overcome. She blamed him: he could have stopped the war and did not. She cursed him that, as the Kuru clan had destroyed itself, so his own Yadava clan would destroy itself, thirty-six years later, and that he himself would die alone in a forest.

Krishna listened, and according to the text, he accepted it calmly. He said that he knew what was coming and that the Yadavas were beyond any other power's reach, so only they could destroy themselves.

## What came to pass
The Mausala Parva describes the end. Thirty-six years later, at Prabhasa on the western coast, the Yadavas got drunk, quarrelled and killed one another with clubs. Krishna withdrew into the forest, where the hunter Jara's arrow ended his life on earth.

## What the story teaches
- **A mother's grief carries weight.** Whatever else is said about the war, the tradition does not wave away the pain of those who lost the most.
- **Power does not buy exemption.** Krishna was the teacher of the Gita and the guide of the Pandavas, yet he did not argue with the curse.
- **Prosperity can breed its own ruin.** The Yadavas' end begins with pride and excess, a pattern the epic repeats.
- **Accepting is not the same as giving up.** Krishna accepts what cannot be changed and still sets out to protect what can be.

## For today
When someone who is hurt blames you, listen before you defend yourself. Their pain may be misdirected, but it is not nothing.

## A closer look at the scene
The curse is not shouted in a moment of rage and then forgotten. It comes in the Stri Parva, the book of the women, which is given over almost entirely to the grief of the war's widows and mothers. Vyasa makes the reader walk the battlefield with them. Gandhari is led by Krishna and shown the dead, and the text says her heart broke at the sight of her sons.

It is worth noticing who Gandhari is. She had covered her own eyes with cloth on the day she married Dhritarashtra, so that she would not see what her husband could not. She had lived by a hard vow for decades. When she turns on Krishna, she speaks as a woman who has given up her own sight for love and now has nothing left.

## Why did the Yadavas fall?
The Mausala Parva (*mausala* means "of the clubs") gives a story that is told in the epic itself. In it, some young Yadava men play a prank on sages visiting Dwaraka, and the sages respond with a curse that the youth will give birth to an iron club that destroys the clan. The club is ground to powder and thrown into the sea, but the powder grows into reeds, and at Prabhasa these reeds become the weapons of the fight.

So the epic gives two strands that meet: a mother's curse and the sages' curse. Neither is presented as a simple punishment from outside. Both fall on a clan that had grown proud, wealthy and careless of its elders.

## Questions people ask

**Did Krishna try to stop it?**
In the text he sees the signs coming and advises the Yadavas to go on pilgrimage to Prabhasa. Tradition reads this as an attempt to bring them to a place of purification, but the quarrel still broke out.

**Is the curse about blame?**
The epic does not give a tidy answer. Krishna does not say Gandhari was wrong to grieve. That is part of why the scene is trusted.

**What happened to Arjuna?**
He came to Dwaraka afterwards to take away the women and children, and the text says that his great bow felt weak in his hands. It is one of the saddest passages in the epic.

## What you can take from it
Great institutions, families and even great teachers do not last forever. What lasts is how we behave while we are here. If someone in your life has been hurt and holds you responsible, sit with them. You do not need to agree with them to hear them.

## Reading it with modern eyes
Some readers ask why the epic lets a grieving woman's words come true. Others read it the other way: the epic shows that Krishna, who is God to his devotees, still lives inside a story in which actions have consequences. Either way, the scene refuses an easy ending. There is no victory song after Kurukshetra, only a long walk among the dead and a mother who will not be comforted.

That honesty is part of why people return to the Mahabharata. It does not tell you that the right side wins and everyone is happy. It tells you that even a righteous war leaves a wound, and that the wound has to be faced.
