The Stri Parva of the Mahabharata contains one of its most searching scenes: a grieving mother and the one she holds responsible.

## The mother
Gandhari, who had chosen to blindfold herself for life out of love for her blind husband, lost all one hundred of her sons in the war. Walking the battlefield with Krishna, she was overcome. She blamed him: he could have stopped the war and did not. She cursed him that, as the Kuru clan had destroyed itself, so his own Yadava clan would destroy itself, thirty-six years later, and that he himself would die alone in a forest.

Krishna listened, and according to the text, he accepted it calmly. He said that he knew what was coming and that the Yadavas were beyond any other power's reach, so only they could destroy themselves.

## What came to pass
The Mausala Parva describes the end. Thirty-six years later, at Prabhasa on the western coast, the Yadavas got drunk, quarrelled and killed one another with clubs. Krishna withdrew into the forest, where the hunter Jara's arrow ended his life on earth.

## What the story teaches
- **A mother's grief carries weight.** Whatever else is said about the war, the tradition does not wave away the pain of those who lost the most.
- **Power does not buy exemption.** Krishna was the teacher of the Gita and the guide of the Pandavas, yet he did not argue with the curse.
- **Prosperity can breed its own ruin.** The Yadavas' end begins with pride and excess, a pattern the epic repeats.
- **Accepting is not the same as giving up.** Krishna accepts what cannot be changed and still sets out to protect what can be.

## For today
When someone who is hurt blames you, listen before you defend yourself. Their pain may be misdirected, but it is not nothing.
