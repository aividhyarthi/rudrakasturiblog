*Matra-sparshas tu Kaunteya shitoshna-sukha-duhkha-dah, agamapayino 'nityas tams titikshasva Bharata.*

This is verse 14 of Chapter 2 of the Bhagavad Gita, spoken early in Krishna's advice to Arjuna.

## The meaning
The contact of the senses with their objects, says Krishna, gives cold and heat, pleasure and pain. They come and go. They are not permanent. Therefore, he says, endure them, O Bharata.

The key word is *titiksha*, translated as endurance or patient bearing. It does not mean suppressing feeling or pretending nothing hurts. It means not letting passing experiences make decisions for you.

## Where Arjuna was
Arjuna had just put down his bow, overwhelmed by grief and fear. In that state he wanted to stop fighting, leave and become a wanderer. Krishna's advice here is not to ignore what Arjuna feels, but to see its nature: it is a wave, and waves pass.

## In daily life
- **A bad day at work** is a day, not a verdict on your career.
- **An insult** stings, and then fades. The reply you make in the first minute is what stays.
- **A pleasant day** also passes. The mind that clings to it suffers when it ends.
- **Illness, heat, hunger, waiting in a queue:** the feeling is real, and so is the fact that it will change.

## The next verse
Verse 2.15 says that the person who is not troubled by these, who stays even through pleasure and pain, becomes fit for immortality. The practical meaning is steadiness: someone who remains calm through the ups and downs becomes reliable to others and to themselves.

## A small practice
The next time you feel a strong wave of irritation, boredom or craving, say to yourself: *this too will pass*. Watch it for a minute without acting. Then decide.

Read in a line, it is almost a small weather report: the senses meet the world, the weather changes, it does not stay, so bear it.

## Two names, one friend
Krishna calls Arjuna "Kaunteya" and then "Bharata" in the same verse. These are family names, one from his mother's side and one from his ancestors. In the middle of a hard talk, Krishna reminds Arjuna who he is and where he comes from. It is the voice of a friend who knows you well, not a stranger lecturing.

## What titiksha is not
The word is often misunderstood, so here is what it does not mean:
- **It is not pretending.** You do not have to smile when you are hurt.
- **It is not staying in harm.** If something is truly harming you, a person, a job, a pain in the body, steady courage may include leaving or asking for help. Any pain or illness that worries you should be shown to a doctor. This is not medical advice, and the Gita does not ask you to ignore your health.
- **It is not coldness.** Someone who endures well can still be kind, and often is kinder, because they are not being tossed about by every mood.

It is better described as *not letting the weather be the driver of your day.*

## Questions people ask
**Does this mean I should not enjoy anything?**
No. The verse names pleasure along with pain. It simply says that both arrive and leave. You can enjoy a good meal fully, and still be at peace when it is finished.

**How is this different from just being tough?**
Toughness often means pushing feelings down. Titiksha means letting them be there, noticing that they are changing, and choosing your action anyway.

**Is this the same as the "steady mind" of verse 2.56?**
They are close cousins. Verse 14 gives the starting practice, and later verses describe the one who has grown steady in it. See [Read the line: Gita 2.56, the person of steady mind](/read-the-line-gita-2-56-the-person-of-steady-mind/).

## A three-breath pause
When a strong feeling arrives, try this:
1. Stop whatever you are saying or typing.
2. Take three slow breaths, longer on the way out.
3. Name the feeling quietly: *this is irritation*, or *this is craving*.
4. Ask: *will I still care about this tomorrow?*
