**Source:** Valmiki Ramayana, Ayodhya Kanda (chapters 50 to 52 and 84 to 86). The boatman washing Rama's feet is from later retellings, and is marked below.

On the first night of his exile, Rama did not sleep in a palace. He slept on a bed of leaves on the bank of the Ganga, under a tree. And someone sat awake beside him, bow in hand, all night.

It was not Lakshmana alone. It was a man most of us have never heard of.

## The friend on the riverbank
The Valmiki Ramayana (Ayodhya Kanda 50) tells us that when Rama reached Shringaverapura, the king of the Nishadas, Guha, came out to meet him. The Nishadas were a forest people who lived by the river and the boat. Guha is called Rama's *sakha*, his friend, and Valmiki says that Rama embraced him. Guha offered the best food and bed he had in his kingdom. Rama thanked him and refused. He was now an ascetic and would eat only forest fare.

What happened next is the part that stays. Rama lay down, and Lakshmana sat up to guard him, as he would for fourteen years. And Guha stayed up with Lakshmana. Lakshmana, sitting awake, poured out his grief at the sight of his brother on the ground, and through it Guha stayed beside him and listened.

There is something very moving about this. The king of the Nishadas had no obligation to Rama. He was not a subject of Ayodhya. He did it because he loved him.

## The crossing
In the morning, Rama asked for the milk of the banyan tree, and used it to matt his hair into the locks of an ascetic (Ayodhya Kanda 52). Guha arranged a boat and boatmen, and Sita, Rama and Lakshmana crossed the Ganga. Sita prayed to the river for their safe return, and the text gives us her vow to worship Ganga when they come back.

*A later image:* in popular retellings, such as Tulsidas's *Ramcharitmanas*, the boatman, here called Kevat, insists on washing Rama's feet before allowing him in his boat, saying that the dust of his feet turned a stone into a woman (Ahalya) and might turn his boat into one. The scene is not in Valmiki, but it has become one of the best loved moments in the story, and it shows how the tradition remembers the friendship.

## Guha and Bharata
Guha appears again. When Bharata, returning from his uncle's home, heads for the forest with the whole army of Ayodhya, Guha sees the great force coming along the Ganga and suspects the worst. He calls his people to arms, thinking that Bharata has come to kill Rama (Ayodhya Kanda 84).

It is a very human moment. Guha, who loved Rama, could not imagine that anyone from the palace meant him well. And then he sees Bharata, thin, in plain clothes and weeping. Guha is ashamed of his suspicion, falls silent, and leads him across the river and onward to Rama. He takes him to the exact spot where Rama had slept, and Bharata, seeing the bed of grass, cries again.

## What it teaches
- **Love is not about status.** Guha had no claim on Rama. The friendship was a gift from both sides.
- **A night spent beside someone in pain is a great gift.** He did not fix anything. He stayed.
- **Suspicion is natural, and kindness can undo it.** Guha's first thought about Bharata was wrong, and he was glad to be shown he was wrong.
- **Small people carry great stories.** Many of the best moments in the epic belong to those who are not kings.

## Where to see it
Tradition places Shringaverapura near Prayagraj, at the modern Singraur on the Ganga, where there is a temple to Shringi Rishi. In Kamban's Tamil Ramayana, Rama says that with Guha, he and his brothers are now five.
