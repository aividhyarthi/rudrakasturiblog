A community kitchen serves a meal to anyone who arrives. People sit together, share the same food and are not asked about their wealth, caste or social position. This practice, known as *langar*, expresses one of the central ideas associated with Guru Nanak: every human being deserves dignity.

Guru Nanak Jayanti, also called Parkash Gurpurab, commemorates the birth of Guru Nanak Dev Ji, the first Sikh Guru. In 2026, the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee's Nanakshahi calendar lists the observance on **Tuesday, 24 November**, or 9 Maghar 558. The date coincides with Kartik Purnima in the Hindu calendar this year, but Sikh observance follows its own calendar and tradition.

## The early life of Guru Nanak

Guru Nanak was born in 1469 at Rai Bhoi di Talwandi, now known as Nankana Sahib in present-day Pakistan. His parents were Mehta Kalu and Mata Tripta. His elder sister was Bebe Nanaki, who is remembered in Sikh tradition as one of the first people to recognise his spiritual nature.

Stories about Guru Nanak's childhood describe a boy who asked questions about the meaning of life and the value of outward status. One well-known account tells of his refusal to treat the sacred thread ceremony as a matter of social display. In the traditional narrative, he asks for a thread made of qualities such as compassion, contentment and truth. The point of the story is not a rejection of goodness or discipline. It is a challenge to the belief that an external symbol alone can make a person spiritually pure.

As he grew older, Guru Nanak worked and lived among ordinary people. He married Mata Sulakhni and had two sons, Sri Chand and Lakhmi Das. He also spent time with Bhai Mardana, a close companion and musician who accompanied him during many of his travels.

## The experience that shaped his message

Sikh tradition recounts that while living at Sultanpur Lodhi, Guru Nanak would bathe in the river Bein. One day he disappeared from view and returned after a period of deep spiritual experience. The traditional account says that he then spoke the words often translated as, "There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim."

This statement is sometimes misunderstood as saying that religious identities do not matter. In Sikh understanding, it points to a deeper truth: the Divine is not confined to one community, and human beings should not be divided by claims of religious superiority. The test of faith is how a person lives.

Guru Nanak's teaching is rooted in the oneness of the Divine. The opening of the Guru Granth Sahib, known as the Mool Mantar, describes the One as timeless, without fear and without enmity. It is a statement about the nature of the Divine and a foundation for the ethical life that follows.

## The journeys known as Udasis

Sikh tradition remembers Guru Nanak travelling widely to share his message. These journeys are commonly called the *Udasis*. Traditional accounts describe visits to places across the Indian subcontinent and beyond, including pilgrimage centres, towns and sites associated with different religious communities.

The stories of these travels show Guru Nanak speaking with priests, scholars, householders, rulers and ordinary workers. He questioned practices that had become empty or exploitative. He challenged the idea that a person could become spiritually superior through pilgrimage alone while continuing to treat others unfairly.

Some travel details and routes differ across historical sources and later Janamsakhi traditions. It is therefore better to describe the Udasis as the journeys remembered in Sikh tradition rather than present every episode as a modern, independently verified itinerary.

## Kartarpur and the model of an honest community

Towards the later part of his life, Guru Nanak settled at Kartarpur, now in Pakistan. There he gathered a community around prayer, work and shared living. People remembered his teaching not only through words but through the way the community organised daily life.

Kartarpur is important because it brings together the practical elements of his message. Spiritual remembrance was joined with honest work. Food was shared. People were encouraged to live responsibly rather than withdraw from the world.

Before his death in 1539, Sikh tradition says Guru Nanak appointed Bhai Lehna as his successor. Bhai Lehna became Guru Angad Dev Ji, the second Sikh Guru. The succession established the continuing line of Sikh Gurus.

## Three principles that explain his teaching

Sikh ethical teaching is commonly explained through three connected principles:

**Naam Japna** means remembering and meditating on the Divine Name. It is a way of keeping the Divine present in ordinary life, not only during a formal prayer.

**Kirat Karni** means earning a living through honest work. A spiritual life should not depend on cheating, exploitation or avoiding responsibility.

**Vand Chhakna** means sharing what one has with others. Food, income, time and care become more meaningful when they are not kept only for oneself.

These principles connect spiritual life with everyday responsibility. Prayer is not separated from work, family or care for people around us. The three-part summary is widely used in Sikh education and community life, though it should not be presented as a verbatim list from one single verse.

## Why is langar central to Gurpurab?

Langar is the community kitchen found at gurdwaras. Food is prepared and served as an act of seva, or selfless service. Everyone is welcome, and people sit together to eat.

The practice gives a visible form to equality. A person does not need to be wealthy, influential or from a particular background to receive a meal. Volunteers prepare food, clean, serve and organise the space. The person who serves and the person who eats are both part of the same community.

On Gurpurab, many gurdwaras serve langar to large numbers of visitors. The meal is not a side event. It is one way the community puts Guru Nanak's teachings into action.

## How is Guru Nanak Jayanti observed?

Common observances include Akhand Path, a continuous reading of the Guru Granth Sahib; kirtan, the singing of hymns; Nagar Kirtan processions in some towns; prayer gatherings; and langar. Gurdwaras are decorated and illuminated, and volunteers take part in seva.

The exact programme differs from one gurdwara to another. Visitors should follow the local gurdwara's instructions, cover their heads, remove footwear where required and behave respectfully inside the prayer hall.

## What can we learn from Guru Nanak today?

His message is practical. Remember the Divine, earn honestly and share. These ideas ask for a life in which faith is visible in conduct, not only in words.

For a person who is busy with work, the message is not to abandon daily responsibilities. It is to bring honesty and care into them. For a community, it is a reminder that dignity should not depend on status or wealth.

## Questions people ask

**Is Guru Nanak Jayanti the same as Gurpurab?**

Gurpurab is the name used for a Sikh commemorative festival associated with a Guru. Guru Nanak Jayanti is one of the most widely observed Gurpurabs.

**Where was Guru Nanak born?**

He was born at Rai Bhoi di Talwandi, now Nankana Sahib in Pakistan.

**Can people of other faiths attend langar?**

Yes. Langar is open to all, and sharing a meal without discrimination is central to its practice.
