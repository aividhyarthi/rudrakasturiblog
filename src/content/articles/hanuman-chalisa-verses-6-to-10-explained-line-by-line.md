Imagine you have said the Hanuman Chalisa a hundred times, and someone asks you what the sixth line says. For many of us the honest answer is: *I know the tune.*

In an [earlier piece](/hanuman-chalisa-meaning-first-five-verses/) we read the opening doha and the first five verses. Here are the next five, the verses where Tulsidas begins to tell us what Hanuman actually *did*.

## Verse 6
*Sankar suvan Kesari nandan, Tej pratap maha jag bandan*

**Meaning:** Son of Shankar (an aspect of Shiva), delight of Kesari, whose glory and splendour are honoured by the whole world.

The tradition holds that Hanuman is an *amsha*, a portion, of Shiva, born to Anjana and Kesari with the blessing of the wind god Vayu. That is why he is also called Pavan-putra and Anjaneya. In Telugu and Kannada homes, calling Hanuman a form of Shiva is a quiet way of saying that strength and stillness belong together.

## Verse 7
*Bidyavan guni ati chatur, Ram kaj karibe ko aatur*

**Meaning:** Learned, virtuous and very clever, always eager to do Rama's work.

This is the verse that surprises people. We usually think of Hanuman as strong. Tulsidas first calls him *learned*. In the Kishkindha Kanda, when Hanuman first meets Rama in disguise, Rama turns to Lakshmana and says that no one could speak so well without having mastered the Vedas and grammar. The strongest being in the epic is also the best speaker.

## Verse 8
*Prabhu charitra sunibe ko rasiya, Ram Lakhan Sita man basiya*

**Meaning:** He delights in hearing the Lord's story, and Rama, Lakshmana and Sita live in his heart.

There is a lovely picture that goes with this verse. Hanuman tears open his chest and shows Rama, Sita and Lakshmana living within. It is a later tradition, not in Valmiki, but it explains the verse: Hanuman's love is not for show. It is a love that listens. Wherever the Ramayana is recited, the old belief says, Hanuman is present, with folded hands, tears in his eyes.

## Verse 9
*Sukshma roop dhari Siyahin dikhava, Bikat roop dhari Lank jarava*

**Meaning:** Taking a tiny form, he showed himself to Sita. Taking a terrible form, he burned Lanka.

This is the Sundara Kanda in a line. To enter Lanka unseen, Hanuman made himself small as a cat. To reassure Sita that he was truly Rama's messenger, he stayed small so as not to frighten her. Then, when Ravana's men set fire to his tail, he grew vast and leapt from roof to roof until the city was in flames. If you want the whole story, see our piece on [Sundara Kanda](/sundara-kanda-why-people-read-it-in-hard-times-and-how/).

The verse carries a quiet lesson: *real power knows when to be small.*

## Verse 10
*Bhim roop dhari asur sanhare, Ramchandra ke kaj sanvare*

**Meaning:** Taking a fearsome form, he destroyed the demons, and fulfilled all of Rama's tasks.

*Sanvare* means to set right, to complete properly. Hanuman is not only the warrior who fought. He is the one who *finished* what was assigned, and finished it well. When the war was over and Rama asked what he could give him, Hanuman asked for nothing but devotion.

## A pattern in these five verses
Read them together and a shape appears.
- Verse 6: *who he is* (son of Shankar).
- Verse 7: *what he knows* (scholar and wise).
- Verse 8: *whom he loves* (Rama's story).
- Verses 9 and 10: *what he does* (small when needed, vast when needed).

It is almost a character sketch for an ideal person: grounded, learned, devoted, able to adjust size to the situation.

## How to use this on a Tuesday
Choose one of the five verses and let it be the day's thought. On a day you must be gentle, take verse 9 and be small. On a day you must be firm, verse 10. On a day you feel foolish, verse 7. Say it slowly, three times, before you leave the house.

## What comes next
Verses 11 to 20 move to the famous scenes: the bringing of the Sanjivani herb that saved Lakshmana, Rama's embrace of Hanuman, and the line that says Hanuman crossed the sea holding Rama's ring in his mouth. Verses 21 to 30 turn to what Hanuman gives his devotees. The last ten close the Chalisa with prayers. We will explain them in turn.

## Saying it well
- **Slowly.** The pace of a verse is about one breath. Rushing makes it lose its sense.
- **With the meaning in mind.** Even if you only recall the sense of each verse, you are reciting it better than a hundred rushed repetitions.
- **Without worrying about the Awadhi.** The poet wrote in a living language, and the tradition is that it should be said in your own accent.
- **Together.** The Chalisa is often said in a group. It is a good practice for a family, and it is an easy way for a child to learn it.
