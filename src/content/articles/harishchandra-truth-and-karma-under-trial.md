Harishchandra is the king whose name has become a byword for truthfulness in India. The story is told in the Markandeya Purana and in many regional retellings, including popular Telugu and Kannada versions.

## The test
The sage Vishwamitra, testing the king's reputation for truth, asked him for his entire kingdom as a gift, and Harishchandra gave it without hesitation. Vishwamitra then demanded the customary additional payment (dakshina). With no kingdom left, Harishchandra left Ayodhya with his wife Shaibya (Chandramati) and young son Rohitashva and was given a deadline to pay.

## The fall
Unable to pay, he sold his wife into service and himself to a keeper of a cremation ground in Kashi. He worked in the burning ground, collecting the fee from every family who brought their dead. One day his own son Rohita died of a snakebite, and his wife came to the cremation ground carrying the body. Harishchandra, bound by his duty to his master, asked her for the fee, even for his own child.

## The ending
At that moment the gods appeared. The whole thing had been a test. Rohita was restored to life, the kingdom was returned, and the king was honoured. In the retelling, truth and karma meet: the one who kept his word through every loss was lifted above all of them.

## What the story teaches
- **Character shows in loss.** Harishchandra's truth was not tested while he was a king, but when everything had been taken.
- **Do what is right, not what is rewarded.** Harishchandra did not know the ending. The story is a picture of *nishkama karma*, acting without calculating the result.
- **Duty can be hard.** The cremation ground scene is painful on purpose. The tradition does not say that truth is easy, only that it is worth it.

## A closer look at the people in the story
**Harishchandra** is a king of the solar line, and the story presents him as someone who had made a firm vow never to speak a falsehood. The Purana does not describe him as perfect in every way. What it holds up is a single quality: when he had given his word, he treated it as binding, even when the cost was everything he owned.

**Shaibya**, also called Chandramati, is the other hero of the tale. Many readers feel she carries the heaviest part. She is sold, she works as a servant, and she must come to the cremation ground alone with her dead child. In the retellings, she does not break the vow either. Her courage matches his.

**Vishwamitra** is the hardest figure to read. In the Purana he is the tester, and many tellers make him a stern, even harsh, examiner. Other tellers point out that the story is set up as a test of truth and not as a judgement on the sage. It is wise to read him as a role in a parable and not as a full portrait of the great sage.

**Rohita** is the young prince whose death is the last blow. His return to life at the end is what lets the story close in mercy.

## Where the story lives today
- **Varanasi.** The Harishchandra Ghat on the Ganga is one of the cremation ghats of the city, and local tradition links it with the story of the king who worked there.
- **On stage and screen.** The tale has been retold in plays, folk theatre and films across India. The 1913 film *Raja Harishchandra* by Dadasaheb Phalke is regarded as the first Indian feature film.
- **In a famous childhood memory.** Mohandas Gandhi wrote in his autobiography that he saw a play about Harishchandra as a boy and was deeply moved by it. He says the figure of the truthful king stayed with him.

## Is it history?
The story comes from the Puranas, which are a mix of legend, teaching and memory. It should be read as a moral story and not as a record of events. Different regions tell it differently, with different details about the debts, the buyers and the final scene. What stays the same is the shape: loss, endurance, return.

## Questions people ask
**Does the story say we should never lie, even to save a life?**
The story itself does not argue that case. Other parts of the tradition do, and they are more subtle. In the Mahabharata, for example, Yudhishthira's single half-truth has heavy consequences, and the tradition often says that truth should be spoken with care for the listener. You can read about that in [Yudhishthira's glimpse of hell](/yudhishthira-glimpse-of-hell-the-weight-of-one-lie/).

**Is it fair that the king and his family suffered?**
Many readers feel this is the hardest part of the tale. The tradition does not offer a neat answer. It treats the trial as a way of showing what a person is made of, and ends in restoration. If you are asking this question because of your own troubles, our page on [why good people suffer](/searching-for-karma-is-it-fair-why-do-good-people-suffer/) takes it up with more care.

**How do I teach this to children?**
Start with a small question: "Have you ever kept a promise when it was hard?" Let them tell their own stories first. Then share the tale as a story of keeping one's word, and not as a threat.

## For today
Few of us will lose a kingdom for a promise. But we all have small tests: the true receipt, the honest answer, the work done properly when nobody checks. Those are our Harishchandra moments.
