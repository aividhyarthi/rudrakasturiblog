*దీపావళికి చాలా కథలు ఉన్నాయి, కానీ అన్నీ ఒకే రకం కావు. కొన్ని గ్రంథాలలో ఉన్నాయి, కొన్ని పురాణ సంప్రదాయం, కొన్ని ప్రాంతీయ జానపదం, కొన్ని తరువాతి ప్రజల నమ్మకం; ప్రతి కథకు ఒక చిన్న తీర్పు ఇక్కడ ఉంది.*

Ask five people in one train compartment why we light lamps on Deepavali (దీపావళి), and you may hear five answers. Rama coming home. Krishna and a demon. A king named Bali. A goddess in a churning sea. Which one is true?

Perhaps the better question is: what kind of story is each one? Here is a plain verdict on ten of them.

## Rama's return to Ayodhya

Valmiki's Ramayana describes Rama's return after fourteen years and Ayodhya's joyous welcome. It does not tie the return to the Deepavali new moon, and many readers place it in a different season. Verdict: **later popular belief**, very strong in North India and beloved everywhere. It is worth keeping; just know what it is.

## The Pandavas' return

Many say the Pandavas came back from their exile on this day. The Mahabharata does not say so. Verdict: **later popular belief**.

## Yama Deepam and Hima's son

A prince fated to die on the fourth night after marriage is saved when his wife covers the doorway with lamps and tells stories all night, and Yama turns away. Read it in [the Yama Deepam story](/yama-deepam-the-prince-who-was-to-die-on-the-fourth-night/). Verdict: **regional folk**, kept in family custom and in later ritual writing.

## Narakasura

Krishna defeats the king of Pragjyotisha and frees the captive princesses. The Bhagavata and Vishnu Purana tell it, while Satyabhama's blow is a regional addition. See [the Naraka Chaturdashi article](/naraka-chaturdashi-satyabhama-and-the-oil-bath-before-dawn/). Verdict: **Puranic tradition**.

## Bali Padyami

Vamana asks Bali for three steps of earth. The story is in the Bhagavata (Book 8); that Bali returns once a year is a regional belief. Read [the Bali Padyami story](/bali-padyami-the-king-who-returns-once-a-year/). Verdict: the story is **Puranic tradition**, the yearly visit is **regional folk**.

## Bandi Chhor Divas

In 1619, Guru Hargobind was released from the Gwalior fort and, tradition says, refused to leave unless fifty-two imprisoned kings could leave with him. Read [the full story](/bandi-chhor-divas-the-guru-who-would-not-leave-without-52-kings/). Verdict: a **historical tradition of the Sikhs**, which does not belong in the four boxes at all, because it sits in a different record.

## The Jain Diwali

Jains remember the nirvana of Mahavira, observed on this night by tradition, and the lighting of lamps for the light that has gone out. See [the Jain Diwali article](/mahavira-nirvana-and-the-jain-diwali-the-lamps-for-lost-light/). Verdict: **Jain scriptural tradition**, with the date as the tradition reckons it.

## Dyuta Pratipada

On the day after the new moon, Shiva and Parvati are said to have played dice, and Parvati won, which is why in many homes games of chance are played on this day. Read [the story](/dyuta-pratipada-when-shiva-and-parvati-played-dice/). Verdict: **Puranic tradition** by the account of later ritual texts, with plenty of regional variation.

## Kali Puja

In Bengal, Odisha and Assam, the new moon night is for the dark goddess. This is the subject of [the Kali Puja article](/kali-puja-the-night-the-dark-goddess-is-worshipped-on-diwali/). Verdict: **regional tradition** with Shakta and tantric roots; the form of the festival is much more recent than the goddess.

## Lakshmi and the churning

Lakshmi rises from the ocean of milk when the devas and asuras churn it; this is told in the Puranas and in the Mahabharata. That this happened on Deepavali night is traditional, not a statement of those texts. Verdict: the birth is **Puranic tradition**, the date is **traditional**.

## Why so many stories for one festival?

Deepavali is not one festival that began on one day. It is a cluster of five days, and each day gathered its own memory. Dhanteras belongs to wealth and health, Naraka Chaturdashi to Krishna, the Amavasya to Lakshmi (or Kali), the day after to Bali, Govardhan and Dyuta, and the last to brothers and sisters, as Yama Dvitiya or Bhai Dooj.

Across a land as wide as India, every region took the light and hung its own story on it. That is not confusion. It is how living festivals grow, and the oldest thing in all of them is the lamp itself. Whether you light it for Rama, Lakshmi, Mahavira or a lost relative, the small flame asks the same thing of you: to stay lit through the dark and to share its light with the next lamp.

## What the verdicts do not mean

A verdict is not a ranking of holiness. A story of the later popular kind may be held more closely in a family than any Purana. A scripture's story may be unknown in the village next door.

What the verdicts do is help you speak truthfully to a child who asks, "Is it really true?" You can say: this one is in the book, this one our people have told for centuries, and this one we added because we loved it.

## Where this comes from

The Ramayana of Valmiki, the Mahabharata, and the Bhagavata and Vishnu Puranas are the written sources for the stories above. Tamil and Telugu retellings, such as the Kamba Ramayanam and Ranganatha Ramayanam, add their own details. The Sikh account is in Sikh tradition; the Jain account is in Jain tradition. Folk customs come from family practice. Details differ between regions, and we have tried to say where.
