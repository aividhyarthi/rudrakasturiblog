Drive across Andhra Pradesh and you will notice something. A great many of the best-known temples are not in cities. They are on hills. Climbing is part of the worship.

## Simhachalam, Visakhapatnam
On a hill overlooking the city, the Varaha Lakshmi Narasimha Swamy temple is one of the richest in the state. The deity is a form of Vishnu with the face of a boar and the body of a lion. The idol is covered all year round with sandalwood paste. Only on Akshaya Tritiya, in April or May, at the festival of Chandanotsavam, is the paste removed, and devotees see the deity's actual form, in what is called Nijaroopa Darshanam, for a few hours. The paste is then applied again. Lakhs of people come that day.

## Annavaram, East Godavari
On the Ratnagiri hill, the temple of Veera Venkata Satyanarayana Swamy is the centre of the Satyanarayana Vratam. Couples come to perform the vratam at the temple itself, which is believed to be a special blessing, and the temple conducts thousands of them every day. The temple is built in the shape of a chariot, and the pilgrims who arrive for the vratam often stay a night or two in the town.

## Ahobilam, Kurnool
In the Nallamala forests, Ahobilam is the place where, according to tradition, Vishnu appeared as Narasimha to destroy the demon Hiranyakashipu and save his devotee Prahlada. There are nine separate shrines, called the Nava Narasimha temples: Jwala, Ahobila, Malola, Kroda, Karanja, Bharghava, Yogananda, Chatravata and Pavana Narasimha. Some are a short walk and some need a forest trek. The Upper Ahobilam temple is built around a cave. Many people say it is the toughest, and most atmospheric, pilgrimage in the state.

## Mangalagiri, Guntur
The Panakala Narasimha temple on a small hill near Vijayawada has a peculiar custom. Devotees offer panakam, a drink of jaggery in water, which is poured into the mouth of the deity, who is carved with a wide-open mouth. By the temple's account, only half of what is poured is accepted, and half comes back, and the sound of gurgling is heard. There are 11 storeys in the gopuram at the foot of the hill.

## Srisailam, Nandyal
In the Nallamala hills, above the Krishna river, Srisailam is both a Jyotirlinga, for Mallikarjuna, and a Shakti Peetha, for Bhramaramba. It is the only place in India where both are in the same temple. The setting is spectacular, with a forest and a dam. See our [Srisailam guide](/temples/srisailam-mallikarjuna-temple/). Shivaratri and the month of Karthika are the busiest times.

## Why hills?
The Indian tradition has long linked a hill with a god. A hill is closer to the sky and, in some ways, a god's house. Climbing it is a practice in itself. The steps, the breath and the quiet are the prayer.

## For a trip
Simhachalam is a day trip from Visakhapatnam, and Annavaram can be combined with a visit to Rajahmundry. Mangalagiri is minutes from Vijayawada. Ahobilam and Srisailam each take at least a day. Be careful with the heat in summer.

## A closer look at the Narasimha story
Two of these hills, Simhachalam and Ahobilam, are linked with Narasimha. The story is told in the Bhagavata Purana (canto 7). A king named Hiranyakashipu had been granted a boon that made him almost impossible to kill: not by day or night, not indoors or outdoors, not by man or beast. His young son Prahlada, however, was a devotee of Vishnu, and would not give up his prayers.

When the king mocked him and asked whether his God was in a pillar, Vishnu appeared from it as Narasimha, half man and half lion, at twilight, on the threshold, and ended the tyrant. Prahlada, who had suffered for his faith, was safe.

Each temple tells the story in its own setting. At Ahobilam, the place itself, with its caves and forest, is part of the telling. At Simhachalam, the deity combines two forms of Vishnu, the boar and the man-lion, which is unusual.

## Questions people ask
**Do I need to be fit to visit these hills?**
Simhachalam, Annavaram and Mangalagiri have road access or steps that most people can manage. Ahobilam's upper shrines and the forest paths are harder. If you are elderly or have heart or joint problems, plan carefully and ask local guides which shrines suit you.

**What should I wear?**
Simple, modest and comfortable clothes. Temples have their own dress rules, so check before you go. You will leave your footwear outside, so carry a bag for it and be careful on hot stone.

**When is the best time?**
Most people prefer the cooler months, from about November to February. Festival days bring wonderful atmosphere and huge crowds, so decide which you prefer.

## Small things that make the climb easier
- Start early, before the sun is high.
- Carry water and a light snack, but check what the temple allows.
- Walk at your own pace. Nobody is timing you.
- Keep your phone in your bag at the top. The quiet is the point.
- Be gentle with the place: no plastic, and no leaving offerings on the path.

## A thought for the road
Many of these temples were once reached only on foot, through forest or over rock. The effort changed the traveller before they arrived. If you go today by car, you can still keep a little of that old spirit by pausing at the last stretch and walking it slowly.
