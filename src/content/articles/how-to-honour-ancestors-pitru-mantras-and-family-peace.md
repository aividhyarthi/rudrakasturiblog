Almost every Hindu family has an elder who says, "Don't forget the *pitrus*, the ancestors." For many people it is a source of anxiety, linked to horoscope warnings about *pitru dosha*. But at its heart, the practice is about something simpler and warmer: remembering the people who made your life possible.

## What the tradition says
The belief is that we are part of a chain, with debts to parents and ancestors, the *pitru rina*. Honouring them is the way of paying that debt. It is one of the three debts in Hindu thought, the others being to the gods and to teachers.

## The main rites
- **Tarpanam.** A cupped-hand offering of water mixed with black sesame, poured with the names of ancestors and a prayer. It takes a few minutes.
- **Shraddha.** The ceremony on the death anniversary, in which food is cooked and offered to the departed, and then given to the priest and the family. It is done according to the lunar calendar, on the tithi of the death.
- **Amavasya.** The new moon is the monthly day to remember ancestors. See [the Amavasya dates](/amavasya/).
- **Mahalaya Amavasya.** The last day of Pitru Paksha, in the dark fortnight of Bhadrapada, when all ancestors are honoured, including those whose date of death is unknown.
- **Gaya.** Many families travel to Gaya, in Bihar, for a special shraddha once in life.

## A simple version at home
You do not need an elaborate ceremony. If you wish to remember your ancestors on Amavasya or a death anniversary:
1. **Bathe,** and sit facing east or south.
2. **Light a lamp** in front of a photo or just at the shrine.
3. **Take water with a few sesame seeds** in your hand and say each ancestor's name, and then pour it into a plate or onto a plant. Say, *Om Pitrubhyo Namah*.
4. **Offer food,** such as rice or a favourite dish of the person, and give some of it to a crow, a cow or someone in need.
5. **Sit quietly** for a minute, remembering them.
6. **Share a meal** with family.

If your family has a tradition, ask an elder or a priest for the correct form. Methods vary by community.

## About pitru dosha
Astrologers sometimes say a horoscope shows *pitru dosha*, a belief that the ancestors are unhappy, and that this causes problems such as delays or disputes. It is a matter of belief and cannot be proved. Nobody should be frightened into spending large sums on remedies. If you feel the need, a simple tarpanam and acts of charity in their name are enough in the tradition.

## What the practice gives you
- **A way of remembering.** Saying the names of grandparents to your children builds a sense of belonging.
- **A way of grieving.** Many people find that a yearly ritual gives grief a place to go.
- **A way of giving.** Feeding others in the name of the dead is a charity as well as a memory.

## If you have a difficult family history
Some people have complicated feelings about a parent or grandparent. The ritual does not require you to pretend. Many find a short prayer, "May you be at peace," is enough.

## Questions people ask

**What if I do not know the date of death of an ancestor?**
Many families honour such ancestors on Mahalaya Amavasya, when all are remembered together. Ask an elder or your family priest for guidance.

**Can daughters and women do tarpanam?**
Practice varies between families and communities. Many families now include daughters, and some priests are happy to guide them. Ask someone you trust in your own tradition.

**What if we live far from home and cannot do the full ceremony?**
A lamp, a cup of water with sesame and a few minutes of remembering are enough, and you can phone the elders at home so that they feel included. Tradition values sincerity far more than perfection.

**Is a photograph needed?**
No. A lamp and a name are enough.

**What if I feel guilty that I was not there when someone died?**
Many people carry this. A prayer of thanks and of asking forgiveness, said quietly, is accepted. If the weight is heavy, talk to someone you trust, or to a counsellor.

## A closer look at the sesame
Black sesame appears in many of these rites. Tradition links it with purity and with the ancestors, and it is also a simple, humble seed that is easy to keep at home. You do not have to go searching for rare things. Water, sesame and a sincere heart are the heart of the offering.

## Bringing children into it
Children often love hearing about the people they come from. On the day you remember an elder, tell the family a story about them: what they cooked, the songs they sang, a funny habit. Let the children place a flower or pour a little water. They will remember the feeling, even if they forget the details. You may also read [what Krishna says to someone who has lost someone](/gita-on-grief-what-krishna-says-to-someone-who-has-lost-someone/) for a gentle way to speak about death with older children.

## Remembering in your own way
Some families plant a tree in memory of an elder, sponsor a meal at a temple, or give books to a school on the anniversary. These acts honour the same thought as the old rite: that what they gave us should be passed on. Do what is within your means and your heart.
