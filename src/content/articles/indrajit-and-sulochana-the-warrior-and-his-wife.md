*ఇంద్రజిత్తు రావణుని గొప్ప కుమారుడు, యుద్ధకాండలో నికుంభిలలో లక్ష్మణుని చేతిలో పడ్డాడని వాల్మీకి చెబుతాడు. భార్య సులోచన కథ ప్రాంతీయ, తరువాతి సంప్రదాయంలోనిదని గుర్తుంచుకోవాలి.*

Think of the child who is told from the beginning that he must be the best, and then grows up to be exactly that, and never once asks what it is for. That is a way to read Indrajit (ఇంద్రజిత్తు).

## The boy called Meghanada

Ravana's eldest son was born to Mandodari. Tradition says that at his birth his cry sounded like thunder, which gave him the name Meghanada, the one with the voice of clouds. He grew into a warrior of unusual power, skilled in illusion and in divine weapons.

He is called Indrajit because, in the tradition, he defeated Indra, the king of the gods, and carried him off to Lanka. Brahma came to free Indra and granted Meghanada a boon in return. The details of that boon vary, but the sense is that he would be hard to defeat while he completed a certain fire rite before entering battle.

## What the boon was really about

This is where the story becomes interesting. The boon is not a gift of invincibility. It is a condition. The text, as tradition tells it, says that if he were interrupted before completing the yajna at Nikumbhila, he could be killed. His strength depended on a ritual, and his enemies would learn that.

## Nikumbhila and Lakshmana

In the Yuddha Kanda of Valmiki, Indrajit had already used the Brahmastra and bound Rama and Lakshmana in the serpent-weapon, from which Garuda released them. He had also created an illusion of a slain Sita to break Rama's spirit. Vibhishana, now with Rama, knew the secret of Nikumbhila and led Lakshmana there.

Lakshmana, with Hanuman and the vanaras, broke into the grove while Indrajit was still performing the rite. After a fierce fight Lakshmana killed him. The text treats him as a worthy opponent, and Rama's side counts his death as the real turning of the war. If you want to read about the brother who helped Lakshmana reach him, see [Vibhishana, the brother who chose dharma](/vibhishana-the-brother-who-chose-dharma-over-his-brother/).

Notice what is hard here. A man is struck down in the middle of his prayer. The story does not ask us to pretend that this is easy. It asks us to see why Vibhishana, who was his own uncle, did it.

## Sulochana, the wife in regional tradition

Valmiki does not give Indrajit's wife much space. Regional and later traditions fill that gap with a woman named Sulochana, a daughter of Shesha Naga in some tellings, and called Pramila in others.

In these stories, when Indrajit falls, Sulochana comes to Rama's camp to collect his head and arms so she can enter his funeral pyre. She is said to have asked Rama's permission with great dignity, and Rama honours her as a woman of devotion. Some tellings say Lakshmana, humbled, bows to her.

Please hold this as folk and later tradition. The details change from region to region, and I do not want to give you one version as if it were the only one. What is steady in all of them is the picture of a wife who walks into the camp of her husband's killer and is treated with respect.

## A poet who took Lanka's side

In the nineteenth century, the Bengali poet Michael Madhusudan Dutt wrote Meghanad Vadh Kavya, a long poem that treats Indrajit with striking sympathy and views the war from the Lanka side. That is a true and well-known fact of Bengali literature. The poem is a literary work, not a different Ramayana, but it shows that readers have long felt the sorrow of Lanka as well.

## What it asks of us

You can hold two things together here. Indrajit chose to fight for an unjust cause, and he died doing it. And he is also a son who obeyed his father, a husband who was loved. Several of us live in this position, loyal to something we privately doubt. Kumbhakarna is the same kind of figure; read [Kumbhakarna, who knew it was wrong and fought anyway](/kumbhakarna-the-brother-who-knew-it-was-wrong-and-fought-anyway/).

## The weight of a son's duty

Spend a moment with the position Indrajit stood in. Whatever he thought privately, he was his father's son and the army's chief hope, and the text shows him going out to fight again and again, and never turning away from the rite that made him strong, even though a rite can be interrupted and a man cannot always be ready.

That is a hard picture for many families to recognise. A father makes a mistake, and the children are left to decide whether to follow, to argue, or to leave. Vibhishana left. Kumbhakarna stayed and argued. Indrajit stayed and fought. Three sons of one house, three answers. Is any of them easy? The epic does not say so, and perhaps that is why it is still read.

## Where this comes from

Indrajit's boon, Nikumbhila and Lakshmana's victory are in the Yuddha Kanda of the Valmiki Ramayana, and are also told in Kamba Ramayanam and the Telugu and other regional versions, with differences in detail. Sulochana (or Pramila) and her sati episode belong to regional and later tradition, including folk and stage retellings. Dutt's poem is a nineteenth-century Bengali composition.

## A question to carry

When you stay loyal to someone out of love, how will you know the moment loyalty has become a way of not looking?
