**Source:** Valmiki Ramayana, Kishkindha Kanda (chapters 65 and 66) and Yuddha Kanda (74). The Syamantaka story is from the Bhagavata Purana (10.56).

Imagine a group of people at the edge of a task that seems impossible. A sea lies in front of them, a hundred yojanas wide. The deadline has passed. They have decided that it would be better to starve than to go back and tell the king they failed.

Among them is one person who could do it, and he is sitting quietly, not knowing it.

## At the edge of the sea
The vanara search party, led by Prince Angada, has come to the southern shore, to the place where the sea begins. They have searched everywhere. Sampati has told them where Sita is: in Lanka, across the water. But who can cross a hundred yojanas of sea?

One by one, the vanaras are asked what they can leap. Angada says he can cross, but he is not sure he can return. Others name smaller distances. Hanuman, the strongest of them, sits apart.

## The old bear
Then Jambavan, the aged king of the bears, steps in. He is too old to go himself, and he says so. He recounts what he was able to do in his youth: how, when Vishnu took his great form as Trivikrama to measure the three worlds, he went around him twenty-one times, and how he had helped in the churning of the ocean. He is a witness to ages.

And then he turns to Hanuman, and says something that changes the story.

In the text he says, in effect: *why are you silent, Hanuman? You are the son of Vayu, equal to him in speed. You were born with the power to leap across the sky.*

He recalls Hanuman's birth: how, as a child, he mistook the Sun for a fruit and leapt to catch it, and how Indra struck him down, and how the wind god withdrew his breath from the world in anger until the gods gave Hanuman boons. Later tradition, in the Uttara Kanda, adds that sages had cursed him in childhood to forget his strength until someone reminded him. In the Kishkindha Kanda it is simply Jambavan who does the reminding.

Hanuman rose, and as he spoke, his body grew, and the vanaras cheered. He leapt.

## The elder who sees
It is easy to read past this scene. But think of what Jambavan does. He does not push Hanuman. He does not offer a plan. He tells him *who he is*. Often, that is all that is needed. A person who has been told, by someone they trust, of their own abilities, is often ready to act.

We all need a Jambavan sometimes. A teacher who says, *you can do this.* A grandparent. A friend who knew you before you doubted yourself. And, if we live long enough, we are asked to be a Jambavan for someone younger.

## Jambavan elsewhere
He appears again at the war's low point (Yuddha Kanda 74), when Rama and Lakshmana lie wounded. It is Jambavan who tells Hanuman where to find the healing herbs in the Himalayas. Later, in the Bhagavata Purana, an old bear called Jambavan, who is said to be the same, fought Krishna for the Syamantaka jewel for many days before recognising him as Rama, and gave him his daughter Jambavati and the jewel. We have told that story in [Why me? When you are blamed for something you did not do](/why-me-when-you-are-blamed-for-something-you-did-not-do/).

## What this teaches
- **Remind people of their strengths.** It is among the kindest things you can do.
- **Age does not have to mean being finished.** Jambavan was too old to cross the sea, and he turned out to be the most necessary person there.
- **We forget what we can do.** Sometimes the obstacle is not the sea but our memory of ourselves.
- **A good elder sends others forward.** He did not take credit.

## How to be a Jambavan
Some of us will be asked to play this part. A few things the story suggests:
- **Name the strength specifically.** Jambavan did not say *you can do it*. He said *you are the son of the wind*, and told Hanuman what he had done as a child.
- **Do not take over.** He did not try to cross himself, and he did not choose the way.
- **Say it at the right time.** He waited until the others had finished speaking. Then he spoke, and he spoke to the one who needed it.
- **Remember that you may need one too.** Even the strongest people forget what they can do.
