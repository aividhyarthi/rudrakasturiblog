*కైకేయి మొదట రాముణ్ణి తన కొడుకులా ప్రేమించింది, మంథర మాటలు ఆమె మనసు మార్చాయి. దశరథుడు ఇచ్చిన రెండు వరాలతో ఆమె రాముని వనవాసం కోరింది, తరువాత భరతుడు ఆమెను తిరస్కరించాడు.*

Has someone's advice ever slowly turned you against a person you loved, without you noticing the day it started? This is the oldest question in the Ayodhya Kanda, and the person it happens to is Kaikeyi (కైకేయి).

## The queen who loved Rama

We tend to remember Kaikeyi as the villain. But the story shows that on the eve of Rama's coronation she was happy. Valmiki has her give a gift to the maid who brings the news, and she says that Rama is as dear to her as Bharata. She says it is a joy that he will be king.

That matters. She does not begin with hatred. Whatever happens next, it does not come from her heart as it was that morning. It comes in from outside.

## Manthara's whispering

Manthara (మంథర) was the hunchbacked maid who had come with Kaikeyi from her father's house. She is first angry at the news and then begins to speak. She does not demand anything. She asks small questions. What will happen to Bharata? What will become of you once Kausalya is queen mother? Do you think Rama will keep you close?

Notice how it works. She does not say that Rama is bad. She works on fear for the future and the sense of being left out. Step by step, the picture in Kaikeyi's mind changes, from Rama as a son to Rama as a rival's heir.

## The two boons

Then Manthara reminds Kaikeyi of a debt. Tradition holds that in a war between the devas and asuras, Dasharatha was wounded and Kaikeyi saved his life, in some tellings by holding the axle of his chariot with her own arm. He promised her two boons, which she had left unclaimed.

Now she asks: that Bharata be crowned, and that Rama go to the forest for fourteen years in the garb of an ascetic. Dasharatha, bound by his own word, collapses. You can read how this ended for him in [Dasharatha and the curse of Shravana's parents](/dasharatha-and-the-curse-of-shravanas-parents/).

## The regret

What did Kaikeyi feel afterward? In Valmiki, the answer is mostly through Bharata. When he returns from his uncle's home and learns what has happened, he rebukes his mother in the hardest words, and refuses the throne. The text does not soften what she has done. Her husband is dead, her stepson is exiled, and her own son will not speak to her kindly.

Kamba's Tamil Ramayanam gives her a more sympathetic place. Kamba lets us see the sorrow and shame that follow, and Bharata's refusal becomes also a moment of pain for a mother. Other regional poets differ again. Some make her a victim of Manthara and of fate. Some let her stay hard. I would not choose for you.

## What it says about advice

The honest point of this story is not that women are jealous or that servants are wicked. It is that a mind can be taken apart gently.

Ask yourself a few questions. Who do I listen to when I am anxious? Does that person usually leave me calmer, or more afraid? Do they speak about what I might lose, rather than what I already have? The Gita says something close to this when it describes how thought turns to attachment, then to desire, then to anger, and finally to the loss of memory and judgment. You may wish to read [the Gita on suspicion and doubt](/gita-on-suspicion-and-doubt-when-questions-turn-into-poison/) alongside this.

Notice too that Kaikeyi did not make one terrible choice. She made many small ones. Each listened a little more.

## A practice

When you feel a sudden, hot certainty about someone close to you, try this. Wait one night. Ask yourself how you felt about that person last week. Say the thing aloud to someone who loves both of you. If it still stands, then you can act. Kaikeyi had no night to wait, and the story shows what it cost.

## Dasharatha's part

It is easy to put all the weight on Kaikeyi and Manthara, but the text also shows Dasharatha's weakness. He had given a promise without asking what it might one day be used for. When she claims it, he pleads, weeps and begs her to take anything else, but he cannot break his word.

This is a second lesson: a promise made lightly may be collected at the worst hour. Every family has some small promise like that, made in love or in a hurry. It is worth asking, before you give your word, whether you would still stand by it on the hardest day.

Bharata's refusal makes the point from the other side. He is offered a kingdom his mother has bought at terrible cost, and he says no. The story does not let power cancel the wrong that bought it.

## Where this comes from

The scenes of Manthara, the two boons and the exile are in the Ayodhya Kanda of Valmiki's Ramayana, and the devasura war and the earlier debt are part of the traditional account, with details that differ between tellings. Bharata's rebuke is in Valmiki. Kamba Ramayanam, the Telugu Ranganatha Ramayanam and Tulsidas each treat Kaikeyi and Manthara differently, and I have only described the general shape.

## A question to carry

Whose voice has been quietly rearranging your fears, and have you ever checked what they gain?
