You are used to Deepavali night meaning Lakshmi: a clean house, a row of lamps, a quiet puja at dusk. Now imagine travelling east, to Kolkata, on the same night. The lamps are there. But what stands in the pandal is a dark goddess with a tongue stuck out, standing on Shiva, wearing a garland of heads, with a crowd waiting for midnight.

This is Kali Puja (కాళీ పూజ), also called Shyama Puja, and for millions it is Deepavali.

## One night, two goddesses

Across much of India, the Amavasya of Ashwin or Kartika is Lakshmi Puja. In Bengal, Odisha and Assam, the same night is given to Kali. Both are the Mother. The difference is mostly one of region and tradition: Vaishnava and merchant homes tend to keep Lakshmi, Shakta homes keep Kali. Some families in the east keep both, Lakshmi in the evening and Kali later.

If you have not met her before, [the article on Kali](/kali-the-goddess-people-fear-and-the-mother-they-pray-to/) explains why a form so frightening is called Mother.

## Why the new moon?

Kali is the goddess of time and of the dark. A night with no moon is her night in the Shakta view. The Amavasya of this month is considered an especially powerful one, and for tantric practitioners it was historically a time of nighttime worship, called nishitha puja, at midnight.

The roots are in the tantric texts of eastern India. The image that most Bengalis know today, with four arms, the sword, the severed head and the garland, is credited by tradition to the 17th-century scholar Krishnananda Agamavagisha. That is the later popular account, and historians would add that the form had earlier sources too. The Kali Puja as a public festival, with pandals and large idols, is a more recent development; its spread in Bengal is often linked to the 18th and 19th centuries.

## The singer who made her a mother

If tantra gave Kali her form, the songs gave her a face you could talk to. Ramprasad Sen, a poet of the 18th century in Bengal, wrote devotional songs to Shyama, many of them scolding her, questioning her, and begging her like a child. These are still sung today as Shyama Sangeet. Dates and stories about his life vary in the telling, so take the details with some care; the songs themselves are what remain.

This is the heart of Kali Puja that visitors tend to miss. Underneath the sword and the garland is a very human relationship, that of a child complaining to a mother who is late.

## Dakshineswar and Ramakrishna

The temple most linked with the Mother in Bengal is the one at Dakshineswar, built in the 19th century by Rani Rashmoni and served by Ramakrishna. You can read the whole story in [the Dakshineswar article](/dakshineswar-kali-the-temple-a-widow-built-and-ramakrishna-served/). On Kali Puja night, the temple draws large crowds, and Ramakrishna's own devotion is remembered in many homes that night.

## Why the fierce look?

Teachers in the tradition say the tongue, the sword and the dark skin are not there to frighten the devotee. They are there to frighten what the devotee is afraid of: ego, fear, and the clinging to what cannot last. The dark colour is read as the space in which all colours disappear. This is interpretation rather than text, and not every teacher says it the same way, but it is widely held.

## What the night looks like

- The image is dark, often blue-black, and adorned with red hibiscus. In some places 108 flowers are offered.
- Puja begins late and reaches its height at midnight.
- Lamps and candles fill the courtyard; in many neighbourhoods there are crackers too.
- Prasad varies by family, from sweets and fruit to a simple cooked offering.
- Many Bengali families light fourteen lamps and eat fourteen greens on the previous day, called Bhoot Chaturdashi, as a family custom.

A brief word on sacrifice. In some temples and homes, an animal offering has been part of the custom, according to local practice. Many families do not do this, and offer a pumpkin, gourd or sugarcane in its place, or only fruit and sweets. It is not uniform, and you will find both within the same city.

## If you do not live in the east

Most readers of Southwala keep Lakshmi Puja on this night, and nothing here asks you to change that. But you can borrow the spirit. Before you light the lamps, sit for two minutes in the dim light, with the electric lights off. Notice how the dark feels. Many people find it less frightening than they expected.

You may also have your own Mother of the dark close to home. In Telugu country, Kali shares ground with village goddesses such as Mahankali, whose festival is told in [the Bonalu article](/mahankali-and-bonalu-the-vow-made-in-a-time-of-plague/). The Mother who guards, who frightens, and who is addressed as Amma is one Mother in many forms.

## Where this comes from

Kali herself appears in the Devi Mahatmya (part of the Markandeya Purana) and in the tantric literature, but the specific festival on the Deepavali Amavasya is a regional Shakta tradition rather than a single text's command. The image attributed to Krishnananda Agamavagisha is a traditional attribution. Ramprasad's songs are folk-literary tradition in Bengali. The Dakshineswar history comes from the temple's own record. Tellings differ, and different homes keep different forms.

## A question to carry

What do you do with the part of life that frightens you: look away, or speak to it the way a child speaks to a mother?
