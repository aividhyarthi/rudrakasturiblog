This is a story that is told in many South Indian homes, and one worth knowing, because it connects two things we have been writing about: the great philosopher, and a very ordinary kind of kindness.

## The story
When Shankara was a boy of about eight, still a student, he went out to beg for alms, as students did. He stopped at the door of a very poor Brahmin family. The woman of the house had nothing to give him. She was ashamed, and went in. She came back with a single dried amla fruit, the only food in the house, and put it into his bowl.

Shankara was moved. He saw how little she had and how much she had given. He closed his eyes and began to pray to the goddess Lakshmi, asking her to look with compassion on this house. The twenty-one verses he composed are known as the Kanakadhara Stotram, the stream of gold.

The story says that as he finished, golden amla fruits began to fall from the sky into the courtyard. The woman's poverty ended that day.

## What Lakshmi said
A detail often told is that Lakshmi, when asked why she had not blessed the family earlier, replied that the woman had no merit to receive wealth, because of actions in her past. Shankara answered that the gift of the amla was itself a great merit, and that the goddess should reward it. She agreed.

This is a lovely point about karma. The story does not say that the woman's past could be wiped out. It says that one act of selfless giving was weighty enough to change the future. It is close to the idea in [our karma series](/three-kinds-of-karma-sanchita-prarabdha-kriyamana/) that the present action is always in your hands.

## The hymn
The first verse begins *Angam hareh pulaka bhushanam ashrayanti*, speaking of Lakshmi's glance falling on Vishnu like a female bee on a dark tamala tree. The verses go on to praise her glance as a source of every good thing, and ask that it fall, even briefly, on the one who prays.

It is not a hymn that asks for mansions. Most of it asks for her kindness.

## Where and when it is chanted
The hymn is recited on Fridays, the day of Lakshmi, and at the festival of Deepavali. Many people play it at home in the morning. At Kaladi, in Kerala, the house where the story is said to have happened is remembered as Swarnathu Mana, the golden house.

## What to take from it
There are two lessons in the story. The first is that generosity does not depend on how much you have. The second is that a great teacher shows respect to a small gift.
