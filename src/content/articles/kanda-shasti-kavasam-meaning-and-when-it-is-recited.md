Picture a grandmother in a Tamil home on a November evening, lamp lit, a small brass vel in front of her. Her voice rises slowly through a long, rhythmic Tamil hymn, and the children in the room, who may not follow every word, know the tune by heart. That hymn is very likely the Kanda Shasti Kavasam.

## What is a kavasam?

A kavasam is armour. In old battles a warrior wore a coat of metal so that blows would not reach him. In devotion, a kavasam is a hymn that does the same work with words. The devotee names a part of the body, then asks the god to guard it.

Kanda Shasti Kavasam is addressed to Murugan, also known as Kartikeya, Subramanya and Skanda. If you want the wider picture of who he is, [Murugan, Kartikeya, Subramanya: one god, many names](/murugan-kartikeya-subramanya-one-god-many-names/) is a good place to begin.

## Who composed it?

Tradition ascribes the hymn to Devaraya Swamigal, a Tamil saint-poet, and most accounts place him in the 19th century. Stories are told about his devotion at Tiruchendur, but we would not claim firm dates or details. What we can say plainly is that the hymn has been in daily use in Tamil homes and temples for a long time, and it is sung widely today.

## What does it ask for?

The hymn moves in a clear order, and it helps to know the shape.

- It begins with a salutation to Ganesha, Saraswati and the guru, as Tamil devotional poetry often does.
- It invokes Murugan's vel, his spear, and his six faces and twelve arms.
- It then goes through the body: head, forehead, eyes, ears, nose, mouth, neck, chest, belly, arms, and on down to the feet. At each place it asks that Murugan's grace stand guard.
- It asks that fears, harmful influences and troubles keep away from the one who recites.
- It ends with praise and a promise of benefit for those who recite it with faith.

Notice what it is not. It is not a bargain for riches. It asks for safety, steadiness and the company of the god. Whether a particular worry lifts is not something any hymn can promise, and the hymn is best held as a prayer of trust.

## When is it recited?

The most important time is Skanda Shashti. This is the six-day observance in the bright fortnight after Deepavali, in the Tamil month of Aippasi or Karthigai depending on the year, and it ends with Soora Samharam. At Tiruchendur, on the sea coast, Murugan's victory over the demon Surapadma is shown in front of huge crowds. Many devotees fast or eat lightly through the six days and recite the Kavasam each morning or evening. The exact dates change every year; please check [the festivals page](/festivals/) for the current ones.

Outside that week, many families recite it:

- on Tuesdays, which are associated with Murugan,
- on Shashti tithi days, the sixth day after the new or full moon,
- before an exam, a journey or a new venture, in the morning after a bath.

Customs differ. Some families recite it once, some forty-eight times over forty-eight days as a personal vow. Neither is more correct than the other.

## How to recite it if you do not read Tamil

Many Telugu-speaking and other families love this hymn without being Tamil readers. Here is a simple way.

1. Listen first. Play a clear, slow recording a few times. The rhythm carries the hymn, and the rhythm is learned by the ear.
2. Keep the text open in Telugu script or English transliteration. Both are easy to find in devotional booklets and temple shops.
3. Read along quietly while the recording plays. After some days, try reciting on your own.
4. Do not worry about mistakes. Tamil has sounds that are new to many mouths. Murugan is said to listen to the heart behind the words.

For the setting, keep it simple: a clean place, a lamp, a picture or a vel, and perhaps a flower. Sit comfortably. Recite slowly. A full recitation takes roughly fifteen to twenty minutes at an easy pace.

## What to keep in mind

It helps to remember why armour is needed at all. Life brings small fears every day: a long journey, an exam, a worry about a child. The Kavasam gives the mind something steady to hold. You say the name of each part of the body, you place it in Murugan's care, and by the end you have slowly quieted yourself. Many people find that this calm is itself the first gift of the hymn.

Some people feel the hymn should only be recited with strict rules, and others recite it in the car on the way to work. Tradition leans toward cleanliness and sincerity, and it does not ask for more than you can do. If you are unwell, tired or busy, a few verses said with attention are better than a rushed whole.

The Kavasam also connects naturally with the six great shrines of Murugan. If you wish to read about them, see [Arupadai Veedu, the six abodes of Murugan](/arupadai-veedu-the-six-abodes-of-murugan/). Tiruchendur is the second of the six, the one by the sea where the Soora Samharam is held.
