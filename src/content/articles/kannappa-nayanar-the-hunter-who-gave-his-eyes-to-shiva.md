Suppose you are a priest who has spent your whole life learning the correct way to bathe and feed the Lord. One morning you find, at the very foot of the sanctum, scraps of meat, wet leaves and flowers that clearly came out of someone's hair. You would be shocked. Now ask yourself the harder question: what if the Lord was not?

That is the heart of the story of Kannappa (కన్నప్ప), the hunter of Srikalahasti.

## The hunter named Tinnan

The Tamil Periya Puranam, the twelfth-century account of the 63 Nayanmars, tells of a boy called Tinnan (Tinnanar), born to a hunter chief of the forest people near Uduppur, whom the text calls Nagan. He grew up strong and brave and was made chief of the clan. One day, tracking a boar, he reached the foot of a hill beside a river. A companion pointed to a lingam on the hill, and Tinnan felt a pull he could not explain.

He climbed up. It is said that he saw the Lord there alone, with no one looking after the shrine, and his heart went out to him. He decided to look after the Lord himself, the way he knew how.

## What a hunter knows

The service Tinnan gave was a hunter's. He brought water from the river in his mouth, because he had no vessel. He carried flowers tucked in his hair. He offered the meat he had cooked and tasted first, to make sure it was good enough for the Lord. In the morning he would do it again, and at night he stood guard with his bow, afraid that wild beasts might harm the Lord.

Meanwhile a Brahmin priest, whom the text names Sivagochariyar, came each day to worship in the proper manner. He found the sanctum defiled every morning and cleaned it, weeping, never knowing who was doing this. Tradition says Shiva came to the priest in a dream and told him to hide and watch, and then to see which of the two was the greater lover.

## The eyes

On the day of the test, the priest hid and watched. The lingam began to bleed from one eye. Tinnan was overcome. He tried herbs and leaves, and nothing stopped it. Then he did the one thing he could think of: he took an arrow and removed his own eye to place on the wound. The bleeding stopped.

Then the second eye of the lingam began to bleed. Tinnan, nearly blind, understood that he had only one eye left. How would he place it correctly? The story says he put his foot on the lingam to mark the spot, and raised his arrow toward the other eye. At that moment, tradition holds, the Lord stopped him and gave him his grace. From then on he was called Kannappa.

The tale is often told as "the sixth day": in many tellings the priest's secret watch and the test come on the sixth day of Tinnan's stay at the shrine. The number varies between tellings, so hold it loosely.

## The Telugu telling

Telugu readers know this story best through the *Sri Kalahasti Mahatyam* of Dhurjati, a poem traditionally placed in the sixteenth century and linked with the court of Krishnadevaraya's time. That is a hedge, since the exact dating and court connection are debated. Dhurjati's strength is that he writes the hunter's love so tenderly that readers forget the oddness of the act. You can read more about the place in [Srikalahasti and the story of Kannappa](/srikalahasti-the-rahu-ketu-temple-and-the-story-of-kannappa/).

## Devotion versus correct ritual

It would be easy to say that the story is against ritual. It is not so simple. The priest is not the villain. He does his duty faithfully, and the Lord does not scold him. What the tale does is ask something harder: what is ritual for?

Rules exist to help us love. When they become a wall between the person and God, the story warns us, they have failed. And when love is real but careless of rules, the story does not make it a model for everyone. Few of us will literally give an eye; we are not being asked to. We are being asked to look at what our worship is made of.

## How to read this story

Try asking three questions after hearing it:

1. When do I perform a rite without feeling anything?
2. When do I feel something and refuse to perform any rite at all?
3. Whom do I quietly consider "not good enough" for the temple?

Notice that Tinnan did not know the mantras. The text counts him among the saints anyway. And the Lord did not need his offering; he needed Tinnan.

## Where this comes from

The earliest full account is in Sekkizhar's *Periya Puranam* (about the twelfth century). Tamil tradition also remembers Kannappar in the hymn that lists the devotees. The Telugu telling is Dhurjati's, with older folk versions behind it. The details, including the hunter's father's name, the number of days, and the dream, differ across tellings. Some Sanskrit Puranic accounts connect Kannappa to the epic hero Arjuna in a former birth, but that is a later tradition and not part of the Tamil text.

## A question to carry

Whom, in your own life, have you judged as not doing things the right way, and what if they love better than you do?
