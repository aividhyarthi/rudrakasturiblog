Kansa has arranged a wrestling festival in Mathura. The strongest fighters in his kingdom are waiting in the arena. A powerful elephant has been placed at the entrance. The king expects the two young visitors from Gokul to be killed before they can reach him. Instead, the arena becomes the place where his rule ends.

Kansa Vadh remembers Krishna's killing of Kansa, the ruler of Mathura. The observance is especially associated with Mathura and the Braj region. Drik Panchang lists **Friday, 20 November 2026** as Kansa Vadh for New Delhi. Local programmes and dates should be checked against the calendar followed in Mathura.

## The prophecy that turned a king into a tyrant

The Bhagavata Purana begins the story with a wedding. Devaki, the sister of Kansa, is married to Vasudeva. Kansa himself drives the couple's chariot after the ceremony. During the journey, a voice from the heavens warns him that Devaki's eighth child will be the cause of his death.

The warning changes Kansa immediately. The same sister whose wedding he had celebrated now becomes, in his mind, a threat. He draws his sword and prepares to kill her. Vasudeva pleads with him. He points out that Devaki is not the child named in the prophecy. He promises to bring each child to Kansa when it is born.

Kansa accepts the promise but imprisons Devaki and Vasudeva. The decision marks the beginning of years of fear inside the royal household. The Bhagavata Purana says Kansa kills the couple's first six sons. The seventh child, Balarama, is transferred from Devaki's womb to Rohini through divine intervention. Kansa believes the child has been lost.

## Krishna's birth and the night journey to Gokul

When the eighth child is born, it is Krishna. The Bhagavata Purana describes a series of extraordinary events in the prison. The guards fall asleep, the restraints open and the doors become accessible. Vasudeva understands that the child must be taken away from Mathura.

He places the newborn in a basket and carries him through the night towards Gokul. The Yamuna is in flood in the traditional account. As Vasudeva enters the river, the water parts or lowers enough for him to cross. The serpent Shesha shelters the child from the rain. Vasudeva reaches the home of Nanda and Yashoda, where a baby girl has just been born. He leaves Krishna there and returns to the prison with the girl.

When Kansa hears that Devaki has delivered, he rushes to the prison. He tries to kill the newborn, but the child slips from his hands and appears as a divine figure in the sky. She warns him that the one destined to end his life is already elsewhere. Kansa's fear does not disappear. It becomes an obsession.

## Kansa's repeated attempts to stop Krishna

Krishna grows up in Gokul and Vrindavan among cowherds. The Bhagavata Purana tells of Kansa sending different beings to kill the child. Putana arrives disguised as a nurse and attempts to poison him. Krishna survives and kills her. Trinavarta carries him into the sky but is defeated. Other episodes describe Krishna overcoming the cart demon, the calf demon, Bakasura, Aghasura and other dangers.

These stories are not just a list of battles. Each failed attempt increases Kansa's anxiety. He hears reports of the child who has survived every danger and begins to understand that the prophecy cannot be stopped by sending another attacker.

Krishna's childhood stories also show the contrast between the atmosphere of Kansa's palace and the life of the cowherd community. In Mathura, fear controls the king. In Gokul and Vrindavan, Krishna is surrounded by affection, play and ordinary village life.

## The invitation that was really a trap

Kansa finally decides to bring Krishna and Balarama to Mathura. He organises a great bow festival and wrestling contest. He sends Akrura, a relative and devotee of Krishna, to invite the brothers. Akrura knows the king's intention, but he also sees the journey as an opportunity to meet Krishna.

When Krishna and Balarama reach Mathura, the city reacts with excitement. The Bhagavata Purana describes people coming out to see the brothers. But Kansa has prepared another danger. A powerful elephant named Kuvalayapida is stationed at the entrance to the wrestling arena, with instructions to attack them.

The elephant charges. Krishna faces it, defeats it and takes away its tusks. The brothers then enter the arena carrying the tusks. Kansa's plan to kill them before the contest has failed.

## Krishna, Balarama and the royal wrestlers

Inside the arena, Kansa's chief wrestlers are ready. Chanura challenges Krishna, while Mushtika faces Balarama. The women watching from the galleries are troubled. They question whether it is fair to make young visitors fight experienced and powerful wrestlers. Their concern is important because the contest is not presented as an equal sporting match.

Chanura and Krishna begin their fight. The Bhagavata Purana describes their movements, the blows they exchange and the crowd's reaction. Krishna eventually defeats Chanura. Balarama defeats Mushtika. The other wrestlers who come forward are also overcome.

The king's confidence collapses. Kansa orders the music to stop, calls for Krishna and Balarama to be driven out, and commands that Nanda be arrested and Vasudeva killed. The same ruler who arranged the contest now tries to use force to escape its outcome.

## How Krishna kills Kansa

Krishna does not leave the arena. He leaps onto the royal platform where Kansa is seated. Kansa tries to defend himself, but Krishna seizes him, pulls him down and kills him. The Bhagavata Purana describes the end of Kansa's rule in direct terms. Balarama then deals with Kansa's brothers, who rush forward in anger.

The moment is more than the death of one ruler. For years, Mathura has lived under the fear created by the prophecy and Kansa's attempts to escape it. The king's death ends that immediate tyranny.

## What happens after Kansa's death?

Krishna and Balarama go to the prison and release Devaki and Vasudeva. The parents who had spent years in captivity are finally reunited with their sons. Krishna also honours Ugrasena, his grandfather and Kansa's father, and places him back on the throne.

This ending matters. The story does not stop with revenge or victory in the arena. The family is reunited, the rightful ruler is restored and the city is given a chance to return to order. The Bhagavata Purana presents the episode as the restoration of dharma after a period of fear and injustice.

## What does Kansa Vadh mean?

Kansa's power depends on fear. He tries to control the future by imprisoning a family and attacking children. The story says that power built on cruelty cannot make itself secure.

There is another detail worth noticing. The women in the arena speak against an unfair contest even before the outcome is known. Their concern is about the rules of the fight, not simply which side will win. The episode gives space to that voice too.

For devotees, Krishna's return to Mathura is a story of protection and the restoration of justice. The release of his parents and the return of Ugrasena to the throne show that ending injustice also means repairing what it damaged.

## How Kansa Vadh is observed

Kansa Vadh is particularly associated with Mathura and the Braj region. Local observances may include readings or enactments of Krishna's story, temple worship and community programmes. The form is not identical in every town or household.

If you are visiting Mathura, check the local temple or district administration for the current year's programme. A date listed for New Delhi does not confirm the timing of a local performance.

## Questions people ask

**Is Kansa Vadh mentioned in the Bhagavata Purana?**

Yes. The story of Kansa's death is described in the Tenth Canto, Chapter 44, after the wrestling match in Mathura. The events leading to Krishna's birth are described earlier in the same canto.

**Did Balarama also fight Kansa's wrestlers?**

Yes. The text describes Balarama defeating Mushtika, while Krishna fights Chanura and then Kansa.

**Is Kansa Vadh a major festival everywhere in India?**

No. It is especially associated with the Braj region and Mathura. Other regions may remember the episode within the wider Krishna story.
