Most of us are taught to look after our bodies, to dress well, to be seen. So what do you make of a woman who looked at her own beauty and asked God to take it back? The story of Karaikal Ammaiyar is not comfortable, and it was never meant to be. But it is one of the oldest voices of a woman in Tamil devotional poetry, and it deserves to be read carefully rather than flinched from.

## The woman of Karaikal

Karaikal Ammaiyar (காரைக்கால் அம்மையார்), "the revered mother of Karaikal", is named Punitavati in Sekkizhar's *Periya Puranam*, the twelfth-century account of the 63 Nayanmars. She was born into a merchant family in the port town of Karaikal and married a man called Paramadattan. From childhood she loved Shiva and fed his devotees.

Her dates are uncertain. Many scholars place her around the sixth century, among the earliest of the Shaiva saints, but this is a matter of debate. Tradition holds that her hymns are among the oldest in the Panniru Tirumurai, the twelve-part Tamil Shaiva canon, and some call her the first of the saints in order of time. Please take that as tradition and not as settled history.

## The mango

The central story is simple and strange. One day Paramadattan sent two mangoes home. Punitavati set them aside; then a hungry devotee of Shiva came to the door and she fed him with rice and one of the mangoes. When her husband came home he ate the other and asked for the second. She went to the kitchen in distress and prayed. Tradition says a mango appeared in her hand. He ate it and found it so sweet that he knew it was not from this world.

He asked where she got it. She told him the truth, and he did not believe her. "Do it again," he said. She prayed, a second mango appeared, and when he took it, it vanished. He was afraid. He decided that a woman who could do this was no ordinary wife, and soon afterward he left, sailing away and starting a new family in the Pandya country.

## The skeleton

When Punitavati later met him, he and his new wife and child bowed to her as a goddess. This was the end of her life as a householder. She understood that the body her husband had loved was now only a barrier. She prayed that Shiva remove the beauty of her form and give her the form of a *pey*, a skeletal being, a creature of the cremation ground, so that she might serve him freely.

Tradition says this was granted. She then went north to Mount Kailash, climbing on her hands because she could not bear to put her feet on the holy ground. Shiva greeted her as "Ammai" (mother), and told her to go to Tiruvalankadu to see his dance.

## The macabre, read with respect

It is worth pausing here. The imagery is jarring: bones, hair, fire, ghouls. Yet the word *pey* in Tamil does not mean something evil. It describes the ones who live near Shiva in the burning ground, where everything false is burned away.

She is not praising death. She is saying that what the world calls beautiful and frightening are both small beside the Lord. Her hymns on Tiruvalankadu describe Shiva dancing in the cremation ground, with the ghouls around, and she, the skeleton, watching at his feet. The picture is meant to wake us, not to disgust us.

## What her devotion asks

Karaikal Ammaiyar asks something few of us would dare: *What do I hold onto that is not really mine?* It is not a call to harm our bodies, and no reading of her story advises that. She takes nothing from anyone else. What she asks is detachment, and a willingness to be seen as odd.

She also reminds us that the early Shaiva tradition made room for a woman's voice speaking in her own right. Her poems are short, direct and full of wonder. Her *Arputat Tiruvantati* is a sequence of verses where each begins with the last word of the one before; her *Tiruvirattai Manimalai* is a garland of paired verses. ## Why she is remembered today

In Tamil Nadu her image is often shown seated with a pair of cymbals, thin and smiling, near Shiva's dance. At Tiruvalankadu and at Karaikal itself, a festival called the Mangani Thiruvizha (the mango festival) is held each year, when mangoes are offered and thrown among the crowd. Dates and customs vary from year to year, so check with the temple before you plan a visit.

## Where this comes from

The story comes mainly from the *Periya Puranam* of Sekkizhar, in the section on Karaikal Ammaiyar. Her own works are in the eleventh Tirumurai. The details of the mango, the husband's flight and her walk to Kailash are in the Periya Puranam but not in her own poems, where she speaks of herself with the word *pey* and of Shiva's dance. Folk and temple tellings add colour that varies.
