We have spent two weeks with the epics, watching karma find kings, sages and even the gods. The question that every reader asks sooner or later is: if all this is so, how much is left to me?

## The five factors
The Gita answers directly in Chapter 18, verses 14 and 15. Whatever a person does, it says, five factors are at work:
1. **The seat** of action, the body and the place.
2. **The doer**, the one who acts.
3. **The instruments**: senses, mind, tools.
4. **The many kinds of effort.**
5. **Daiva**: the unseen, which is often translated as fate or the divine.

Four of the five are in our hands, in whole or in part. Only the fifth is not. The Gita does not say fate is nothing, and it does not say you are helpless. It says it is one factor among five.

## Action cannot be avoided
In Chapter 3 (verse 5), Krishna says no one can remain without action even for a moment, since the qualities of nature make everyone act. So "not choosing" is also a choice. The only question is whether the action is done with awareness.

## The course of karma is hard to understand
In Chapter 4 (verse 17), Krishna admits that the way of karma is difficult to grasp. This is an honest warning. It means we should not be sure we understand why things happen to us or to anyone else. It is a reason for humility, and against judging others for their misfortune.

## What to focus on
1. **The present action.** That is kriyamana karma, the only part you hold.
2. **The quality of the action**, not only its result: honest, careful, kind.
3. **Acceptance of what has already happened.** The arrow that has left the bow has left it.
4. **Without attachment to the fruit**, as in [Gita 2.47](/bhagavad-gita-2-47-meaning-karmanye-vadhikaraste/).

## The Gita's last word on choice
Near the very end of the Gita, at 18.63, Krishna has finished teaching. He tells Arjuna to reflect on everything fully, and then do as he chooses. Think about that. After eighteen chapters of the most serious advice, the teacher does not give an order. He hands the decision back.

This is perhaps the clearest answer to the question of free will in the Gita. Fate is a factor, the nature you were born with is a factor, but the final act of choosing still belongs to you.

## Nature, habit and the pull
The Gita is also honest about how hard choosing is. In 3.33, Krishna says that all beings follow their nature, and even a wise person acts in line with their own. What can mere restraint do? Then, in 3.34, he gives the practical advice: attraction and dislike sit in the senses, so do not come under their power.

Here is the picture. You are not a blank page. You carry habits, likes and fears, shaped by this life and perhaps by earlier ones. These pull on you. Free will is the small space between the pull and the act, where you can notice and say, "not this way." The tradition does not say the space is large. It says it is real, and that it grows with practice.

## Two mistakes to avoid
**"Everything is fate."** If that were true, effort would be pointless, and the Gita would not have been taught. Krishna asks Arjuna to stand up and act. In 6.5, he says you must lift yourself by yourself (see [Gita 6.5: lift yourself by yourself](/gita-6-5-lift-yourself-by-yourself/)).

**"Everything is my doing."** This is the other extreme, and it leads to pride when things go well and crushing guilt when they do not. The five factors remind us that many things go into a result. We do our part and no more.

## A small practice: the pause
Try this for a week.

1. Notice a moment when you are about to react, such as an angry reply, a second helping you do not need or a scroll you cannot stop.
2. Take one slow breath before you act.
3. Ask: *is this my habit speaking, or my choice?*
4. Act, whichever way you decide.
5. At night, note one moment when you paused, even if you did the same thing in the end.

You are not trying to win every time. You are widening the gap between pull and act, which is where free will lives.

## Questions people ask
**If my past karma decides my life, why try?**
Because the past is only one factor. The Gita asks you to act, and your action today becomes part of tomorrow's picture.

**Can I change my nature?**
Slowly, and with steady practice. The Gita speaks often of practice and detachment as the way. Do not expect quick change.

**Is it wrong to feel that some things were meant to happen?**
No. Many people find comfort in it. The only caution is not to use it as a reason to stop trying.
