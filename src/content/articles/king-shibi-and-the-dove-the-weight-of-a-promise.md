**Source:** Mahabharata, Vana Parva (the Shibi episode, in the story told to Yudhishthira). The story also appears in Buddhist literature.

There are two kinds of promises. One is a promise to keep someone safe when it costs you nothing. The other is a promise to keep someone safe when it costs you everything. This is a story about the second kind.

## A bird at the king's feet
King Shibi sat in his court. A dove, shaking with fear, flew into his lap and hid in the folds of his robe. A moment later a hawk landed in front of him, panting and fierce. It said: *O King, that dove is my food. I have been chasing it all day. Give it to me.*

Shibi said that he could not. The dove had come to him for refuge, and he had given his word. *I will give you anything else,* he said. *I will give you a bull, a boar, a deer, a hundred of whatever you like.*

The hawk replied calmly: *Where is the justice in this? I must eat. A starving bird has dharma too. If you save this dove, you starve me.*

## The compromise
The king thought. Both were creatures that needed help. Then he said: *I will give you flesh from my own body, equal in weight to the dove.*

The hawk agreed. A balance was brought. The dove was placed on one pan. Shibi cut a piece of flesh from his own thigh and placed it on the other. The dove was heavier. He cut again, and again. Each time the dove's pan stayed down. At last, when there was almost no flesh left to give, the king himself climbed into the pan.

## The truth revealed
At that moment the hawk and the dove turned into gods. Indra and Agni stood before him and said that they had come to test him. They blessed him, healed his wounds and promised him lasting fame. The tradition says that his body was restored to its former beauty.

## An echo in the Ramayana
If this story sounds familiar, it is because the same ideal appears in the Ramayana. When Vibhishana arrived in Rama's camp and the vanaras wanted to turn him away, Rama declared that he would give protection to anyone who sought it, even Ravana himself (Yuddha Kanda 18.33 to 34). He also recalled a related tale, in which a dove welcomes the hunter who has caught his mate and offers his own body for the hunter's meal. The same value runs through both epics: *the one who seeks refuge must not be turned away.*

## Is it fair to the hawk?
Many readers notice that the hawk had a point. It is a very honest part of the story that the king does not simply dismiss it. He tries to find a way to meet both needs. The solution costs him dearly, and the story is better for it.

## What it teaches
- **Refuge is a sacred trust.** You do not pass a frightened creature back to its hunter.
- **Compassion is not cheap.** Shibi paid with his body.
- **A real test is to give when it hurts.** The gods were not interested in a generous gesture. They wanted to see what he would do when it cost him.
- **Two needs can be real.** Listening to the hawk made Shibi's choice more honest.

## Where it appears
The Shibi story appears in Buddhist Jataka tales and in Indian sculpture. It is carved at Amaravati in Andhra Pradesh and at Nagarjunakonda in the Krishna valley, where it is among the stories shown on ancient Buddhist reliefs.
