Kerala is known for its calm, orderly temples. Then there is Kodungallur, which has a different mood altogether.

## The goddess of the old port
Kodungallur, on the Kerala coast, was once the ancient port of Muziris, a centre of trade with Rome. The Kodungallur Kurumba Bhagavathy temple is among the oldest and most powerful goddess temples in the state. The goddess is Bhadrakali, a fierce form of Parvati. The story is that, at Shiva's order, she took form to kill the demon Darika, who had been terrorising the world, and the goddess's red-eyed ferocity in the temple is a memory of that fight.

## The Kannagi tradition
Another tradition, popular among many devotees, says that the goddess was Kannagi, the heroine of the Tamil epic *Silappathikaram*. Kannagi, wronged by the Pandya king of Madurai, who wrongly executed her husband Kovalan, tore off her breast and burnt Madurai with her anger. She then walked west, to the land of the Cheras, and in this tradition was received as a goddess at Kodungallur.

Not everyone accepts this link. Scholars treat it as one of several traditions about the origin of the cult, not as history. It does show how South Indian goddess worship brings together Sanskrit and Tamil stories.

## The Bharani festival
In the Malayalam month of Meenam (March to April), the temple holds Bharani. Thousands of devotees, many of them in red, arrive. The *komarams*, the oracles of the goddess, enter a trance, dance with swords and strike their own heads, so that blood flows. They run around the temple in a procession. Red cloth, turmeric and cockscombs are part of the display. In the past, offerings of animals were made, but this has been banned.

In an older version of the festival, the *Bharani pattu*, songs of a bold and earthy kind, were sung. They are said to be part of the goddess's own unorthodox character.

## A different kind of devotion
What makes Kodungallur remarkable is that the festival belongs to many communities, including those that the temple's conservative neighbours might once have kept out. It is a place where the usual order is turned over.

The old customs are changing. The trance, the blood and the animal offerings have become matters of debate. Many devotees today ask for the rituals to be kept in a gentler form. Like every living tradition, it is being rethought by the people who keep it.

## What the story says
- **The goddess belongs to the margins as well as the centre.** The festival is wide open.
- **Anger can be sacred.** Kannagi's rage is remembered as justice.
- **Traditions argue with themselves.** The origins are disputed, and the practices evolve.
- **A temple can hold both discipline and ecstasy.**

## Visiting
The temple is in Kodungallur, in Thrissur district, about 40 km from Kochi. Bharani is extremely crowded, and not for everyone. On ordinary days the temple is quiet. Dress modestly and follow the instructions of temple staff.
