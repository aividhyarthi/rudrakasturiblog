It is nearly midnight. The house is quiet, the children are fighting sleep, and a small cradle swings by the puja room, a smooth pebble of a baby doll tucked inside. Somebody has just finished the last row of tiny rice-flour footprints. Everyone is waiting for one moment. If you have ever kept this night, you know the feeling, and if you have not, this is how to begin.

## Which day do you keep?

The Bhagavata tells that Krishna was born in the dark half of the month of Shravana (or Bhadrapada, depending on whether the month is counted from the new moon or the full moon), on the eighth lunar day, Ashtami, at midnight, when Rohini nakshatra was ascendant. So the rule most families follow is simple: keep the day on which Ashtami is present at midnight.

Sometimes Ashtami begins in the afternoon of one day and ends in the evening of the next, and then panchangams, and sects within the tradition, may choose different days. Smarta and Vaishnava households do not always agree. Do not worry about this. Check the date on our [festivals page](/festivals/), and if your family follows a particular panchangam or a family priest, follow that.

## The fast

Many families fast through the day and break it after the midnight puja. Some take only fruit and milk; some keep a full fast without even water until midnight, though that is not for everyone. If you are a child, elderly, pregnant or unwell, a light meal does not take away anything from the day. Krishna, of all gods, is not strict about such things.

## Little footprints

This is the part children love. With rice flour mixed in water, draw tiny footprints from the front door to the puja room, as if the baby had walked in himself. Start with the threshold, since tradition holds that Krishna comes into the house on this night. Add a few more near the kitchen, where the butter is.

## The cradle and the midnight puja

Set a small cradle, even a basket hung with cloth, and place a little Krishna image or a Bala Krishna figure inside. Keep ready:

- a lamp and incense
- fresh flowers and tulsi leaves
- milk, curd, honey and water for a simple abhishekam of the idol
- new clothes or a small cloth, and a peacock feather if you have one
- the offerings, and a flute if there is one in the house

At midnight, bathe the idol gently, dress him, and place him in the cradle. Offer flowers and tulsi, light the lamp, and sing or chant what you know: a Krishna bhajan, the Hare Krishna names, or the Vishnu names. Then rock the cradle gently. Many families blow a conch or ring a bell at the moment of birth. Offer the food, take it as prasadam, and break the fast.

## What to offer

Krishna's great love is butter, so fresh white butter (venna in Telugu, vennai in Tamil) and sugar candy (kalkandu or mishri) are the heart of it. Beyond that, each family has its own plate.

In Tamil homes the plate is usually seedai, murukku, thattai, cheedai and pal payasam, with fruit, beaten rice (aval) and butter. In Telugu homes you may find similar savouries, pongal or payasam, and sweets of jaggery and coconut. Whatever you make, offer a little of everything and keep the tulsi on the plate. If you cannot cook, butter, fruit and sugar candy are enough.

## Gokulashtami and the day after

In Tamil Nadu this festival is called Gokulashtami, recalling Gokula, where the child grew up with Nanda and Yashoda. The next day, in Maharashtra, Gujarat and elsewhere, young men form human pyramids to break a pot of curd hung high, called Dahi Handi. In the Tamil country the same idea is called Uriyadi, where a pot hung on a rope is struck by a blindfolded or leaping player. It recalls the boy who stole butter from the hanging pot and shared it with friends. If you cannot go to see one, a child can tie a small pot of curd with a ribbon and play at home.

## Reading to keep the night alive

If you like to read, the best source is the Bhagavata Purana, Canto 10, which opens with the birth in the prison of Kamsa, the crossing of the Yamuna with the baby, and the exchange with Yashoda's child. You do not need to read the whole canto; a few pages aloud while waiting for midnight is a lovely way to pass the time. To follow his life further, see [Krishna the cowherd, the king and the teacher](/krishna-the-cowherd-the-king-and-the-teacher/).

## What children can do

Children can draw the footprints, string a garland of flowers, pick tulsi, decorate the cradle and put the peacock feather in place. They can dress as little Krishna or Radha, which is a Janmashtami tradition in many homes, and be the one to rock the cradle at midnight. Let them be the ones to offer the butter. Their wonder is the whole point of the night.

You may also read about a related celebration, [the hill Krishna lifted and the Annakut](/govardhan-the-hill-krishna-lifted-and-the-annakut/).

## Keep it simple

You do not need an elaborate setup. A lamp, some butter, a picture, and a few minutes of real attention at midnight are enough. Krishna came to a prison on a stormy night with nothing; he will come to a modest home as well.
