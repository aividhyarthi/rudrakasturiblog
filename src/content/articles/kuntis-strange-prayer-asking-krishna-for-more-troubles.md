**Source:** Bhagavata Purana, Canto 1, Chapter 8 (the prayers of Kunti). It is not in the Mahabharata.

Suppose Krishna stood in front of you and said, *ask for anything.* What would you ask for? Health, wealth, long life, peace? A mother who had lost almost everything was given this chance, and she asked for something no one expects.

## The scene
The Kurukshetra war is over. The Pandavas have won. Yudhishthira has been crowned. Krishna, who has been with them through all of it, prepares to return to Dwaraka. As his chariot is about to leave Hastinapura, Kunti, the mother of the Pandavas, steps forward to speak to him.

What she says is set down in the first canto of the Bhagavata Purana, in a hymn of twenty-six verses (1.8.18 to 1.8.43). It is regarded as one of the most beautiful prayers in the tradition.

## The prayer
In the middle comes the verse that people remember:

> **vipadaḥ santu naḥ śaśvat tatra tatra jagad-guro**
> **bhavato darśanaṁ yat syād apunar bhava-darśanam**

*Let calamities come to us again and again, O teacher of the world, for in them we see you, and seeing you, we do not see rebirth again.*

It is an astonishing thing to say. Kunti is not a naive woman. She had been widowed young. She had been exiled with her sons. She had left a first child on a river, never knowing what became of him, and had hidden her heart about it for her whole life. She had watched her sons humiliated, the war, and the death of friends and family. If anyone had the right to ask for peace, it was she.

## What she understood
Her reasoning is quiet. In the verses just before the famous line, she thanks Krishna for saving them again and again: from poison, from a great fire, from man-eating demons, from the assembly of the wicked, from the hardships of the forest, from the battles with great warriors, and, most recently, from the weapon of Ashvatthama that threatened the unborn child in Uttara's womb (1.8.24). It was in hardship, she says, that she knew him.

She follows with a line that has been quoted by teachers for centuries: *prosperity, learned birth, wealth and beauty make a person proud, and such a person cannot call your name* (1.8.26). She says that those with nothing find God more easily, and that God is dear to those who have nothing of their own.

## A hard teaching
It is important to be careful here. Kunti is not saying that suffering is good, or that we should seek it. She is not telling a grieving person to be grateful. She is speaking from *her* own experience, as a woman who has already lived through it, and she is offering her own conclusion.

For anyone who is in hardship now, the message is gentler: *you are not forgotten in it.* Many people find that, in the worst times, something, a person, a prayer, an unexpected kindness, held them up. Kunti simply gave it a name.

This is why we put this story beside our series [Why me?](/why-me-when-prayers-seem-to-go-unanswered/). The Gita says the distressed person is the first of the four kinds who turn to God. Kunti is, in a way, the proof.

## A mother's strength
What also strikes the reader is the person. Kunti was a woman who had chosen, at a young age, to send away a child, to bear a husband's death, to protect five sons in exile and to survive the war. She was not a figure of passive sorrow. She is, in the epic, the one who tells Draupadi to share her sons' hardship, and who tells her sons to fight. In the Bhagavata prayer she shows another side: a woman who, having done everything, turns to simple devotion.

## What can we take from this?
- **Notice when you remember God.** For many of us, it is in difficulty.
- **Do not ask others to be grateful for their pain.** Kunti spoke for herself.
- **You can ask for what is not on the usual list.** She asked for nearness, not comfort.
- **A person who has been through much is not weak.** She is often the one who understands.

## The moment it was said
It helps to remember when she said it. Just before this, Ashvatthama had hurled a divine weapon at the womb of Uttara, the widow of Abhimanyu, to destroy the last of the Pandava line. Krishna had stepped in to save the child, who was later born as Parikshit. Kunti's prayer comes at that moment of relief, when Krishna is about to leave. She is not praying in despair. She is praying with the memory of every rescue behind her. That makes it all the more striking: in the hour of safety, she asks not to forget what it felt like to need him.
