You are standing in a queue at the temple. It is quiet, the lamps are lit, and you are trying to think of God. And then, without any invitation, a thought arrives that you would never say out loud. Maybe it is about the person standing in front of you. Maybe it is about nobody in particular. You feel ashamed, and then you try harder to push it away, and it comes back stronger.

If that sounds familiar, you are not alone, and you are not wicked.

## Why does the mind do this?
The mind does not have an off switch for the things it is wired to notice. It wanders, it pulls up old images, and it throws up thoughts at random. A temple does not change that. In fact, a few things about temples can stir it up:
- You are in a crowd, close to other people.
- You are quiet and have nothing to do while waiting, so the mind fills the space.
- You are trying to keep a thought out, and psychologists have shown that when we try not to think about something, we think about it more.

In other words, this is the ordinary behaviour of a human mind, not a verdict on your character.

## What Indian tradition says
It might surprise you how calm the tradition is about desire.
- **Kama is one of the four goals of life.** Dharma, artha, kama and moksha: duty, wealth, desire and liberation. Desire is a part of human life, and the old texts treat it as such.
- **The temples themselves say it.** The carvings at Khajuraho and Konark are famous, and even in the South, many temple walls and gopurams carry figures that show couples. They are not hidden. The tradition does not pretend that people are free of desire.
- **The Gita warns about being ruled by desire, not about having it.** In 3.37 Krishna calls desire and anger the great enemy, and in 2.62 and 2.63 he describes how dwelling on something leads step by step to attachment, desire, anger and confusion ([we wrote about the chain here](/bhagavad-gita-on-anger-krishna-teaching/)). The point is the dwelling, not the first flicker.
- **Ramana Maharshi's advice** was not to fight thoughts but to ask, "To whom does this thought come?" Watch it without joining it, and it loses its strength. See [Advaita in plain words](/advaita-vedanta-in-plain-words/).
- **Patanjali's Yoga Sutras** (2.33) suggest replacing a troubling thought with its opposite, a practice called *pratipaksha bhavana*.

## What to do in the moment
1. **Do not shame yourself.** Shame gives the thought energy. Say quietly to yourself, "A thought came. That's all."
2. **Do not argue with it.** Pushing it away makes it stick.
3. **Give the mind a job.** Count a mantra on your fingers or on a mala, such as *Om Namah Shivaya* or *Om Namo Narayanaya*. See [how to start japa](/mantra-japa-how-to-start-count-and-keep-going/).
4. **Look at the deity's feet first.** The old way of darshan is to start with the feet and move upward, slowly. It gives the eyes a path and calms the mind.
5. **Breathe out slowly.** A long exhale tells the body to settle.
6. **Stay respectful in what you do.** A thought passing through is private. Staring at or following a stranger is not. Your behaviour is where dharma lives.

## What not to do
Do not conclude that your prayer was spoiled, that the temple visit was wasted or that you are a bad devotee. That is the kind of thought that makes people stop going.

## When it is more than a passing thought
If the thoughts are constant, if they frighten you, if they make you avoid temples or if you feel that you cannot control them, talk to a counsellor or doctor. This is a common issue, and there is help that works. Speaking to someone is not a lack of faith. It is looking after your mind, which is what the teachers ask you to do.

## A last thought
The saints did not describe a mind that was empty of desire from the first day. They described a mind that was trained, patiently, over years. Everyone starts where you are.
