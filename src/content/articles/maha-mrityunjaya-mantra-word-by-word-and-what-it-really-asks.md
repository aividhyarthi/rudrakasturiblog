Someone you love is in hospital. You are in the corridor, and a relative, quietly, writes a few lines on a piece of paper and presses it into your hand. *Say this.*

It is the Maha Mrityunjaya mantra. Many of us have said it without knowing what the words mean. Let us read it slowly, because it is more interesting than its reputation.

## The mantra
> **om tryambakaṁ yajāmahe sugandhiṁ puṣṭi-vardhanam**
> **urvārukam iva bandhanān mṛtyor mukṣīya mā amṛtāt**
> (Rig Veda 7.59.12)

## Word by word
| Sanskrit | Meaning |
|---|---|
| tryambakam | the three-eyed one (Shiva) |
| yajāmahe | we worship |
| sugandhim | fragrant, sweet-smelling |
| puṣṭi-vardhanam | the one who increases nourishment, strength |
| urvārukam | a cucumber or gourd |
| iva | like |
| bandhanāt | from its bond, its stem |
| mṛtyoḥ | from death |
| mukṣīya | may I be freed |
| mā | not |
| amṛtāt | from the immortal |

## In plain words
*We worship the three-eyed one, fragrant and nourishing. Like a ripe cucumber from its stem, may I be freed from death, and not from immortality.*

## The picture
Think of a cucumber on a vine, ripe and ready. It does not need to be torn off. It slips from its stem. That is the picture the verse paints: a death that comes like ripeness, without struggle, not like a branch snapped in a storm.

## The last word that is often dropped
The popular translations end at *freed from death*. But look at the last two words: *mā amṛtāt*, "not from immortality." The verse does not ask to escape life. It asks to be freed from death *without losing the deathless*. The old commentators read that as *the Self*, which is not born and does not die. In that reading the mantra asks for a particular kind of freedom: to live without being owned by the fear of dying.

That is why it is often used for the ill, but also for the healthy, as a daily mantra of courage.

## How people use it
- **108 times with a mala,** or three, eleven or twenty-one times, in the morning.
- **For someone who is ill,** said aloud at their bedside, or quietly in your own heart.
- **On Mondays,** the day of Shiva, with a lamp at the shrine.

There are no rules about the number. Sincerity matters more than count.

## A word of care
Chanting gives strength to many people. It is not a substitute for medical care. If you are praying for someone who is unwell, do both: call the doctor and say the mantra.

If you are the one who is afraid, you do not have to carry it alone. In India, Tele-MANAS (14416) is free and open at all hours.

## Where the mantra comes from
The verse is from the Rig Veda (7.59.12), and appears again in the Yajur Veda, so it is among the oldest mantras still in daily use. The tradition also calls it the *Mrita-sanjivani*, the life-restoring mantra, and the Puranas say the sage Shukracharya used it to revive the fallen warriors of the asuras. Tryambakeshwar, near Nashik, one of the twelve Jyotirlingas, is named after its first word.

## How to chant it
- **Sit** comfortably, spine straight, and breathe slowly for a minute.
- **Say it aloud or softly,** at a steady pace, not rushing the long vowels.
- **Use a mala** if it helps you keep count, or simply say it for ten minutes.
- **Chant for others too.** Many families say it for a sick relative, a traveller or a friend. Say their name in your mind before you begin.

## What it is not
It is not a bargain, and it is not a guarantee. Many sensible teachers add a simple line: do your part, and let the mantra hold the rest. That is also what the verse says. A ripe cucumber has done its ripening; the stem lets go.

## Reading the mantra as a whole
Notice how the verse moves. It begins with *worship* (we worship the three-eyed one), then names a quality (fragrant, nourishing), then makes a request, and the request is made through a picture of ripening. There is no panic in it. It does not say *save me*. It says, in effect, *let me ripen well, and let go when I am ready*. Teachers say this is why it is useful both for someone fighting an illness and for someone grieving: it makes a place for acceptance without giving up care. Try saying it once slowly and noticing which word catches you. For many people, it is the cucumber.

## Other Shiva mantras to know
The Panchakshari (*Om Namah Shivaya*) is the simplest. The Shiva Gayatri is the next. The Rudram is the longest and most ancient. You do not have to choose; many families use all three at different times.
