It is almost midnight and your eyes are closing. The temple down the road is still lit, the bells are still going, and your grandmother, who has eaten nothing since morning, sits straight on her mat with a rudraksha mala. How does she do it? And must you?

Let us go through Maha Shivaratri slowly, so that you can keep it as your body and your home allow.

## What the night is

Maha Shivaratri falls on the night of the fourteenth day of the dark fortnight of Magha or Phalguna, usually in February or March. Check the [festivals page](/festivals/) or your panchangam for the date and the timings. Tradition holds it as the night Shiva is most easily reached, and it is kept across the South, from Srisailam to Kalahasti to Chidambaram, and in every home with a lingam.

## The story people tell

The Shiva Purana tells of a hunter, who is often called Lubdhaka. Lost in the forest at dusk, he climbed a bilva tree to be safe from beasts, and stayed awake through the night, plucking leaves to keep himself from sleep. Below the tree was a lingam, and the leaves fell upon it. He had also gone hungry all day, and a little water dropped from his pot onto it. Without knowing it, he had kept the fast, the vigil and the worship. Tradition says that Shiva accepted it, and the hunter was freed of his sins.

Do not take this as a recipe. The tale says that a sincere heart, even an unknowing one, is received.

## The fast

On the day, many families bathe at dawn and take a vow, a sankalpa, to keep the fast. Practices differ:

- Some take no food and no water until the next morning.
- Many take only fruit, milk and water, or a single meal without grains.
- In some homes, sabudana or other non-grain food is allowed.

Whichever your family keeps, do not make it a contest. The point is to lighten the body so that the mind can be still.

## The night in four praharas

A prahara is about three hours. Tradition divides the night into four, with a puja in each. If you do it at home:

1. **Set up.** Place a lingam, or a picture, on a clean stand. A lamp, flowers, incense, and a plate for the offerings.
2. **First prahara (evening).** Abhishekam with milk, then bilva leaves, then Om Namah Shivaya.
3. **Second prahara.** Abhishekam with curd. Many homes also chant the Rudram or a Shiva stotra.
4. **Third prahara.** Abhishekam with ghee or honey, depending on the family.
5. **Fourth prahara (before dawn).** Abhishekam with water, or with sugar or rose water, and the final aarti.

Items vary by family and temple; some use all five (milk, curd, ghee, honey, sugar), some use only milk and water. Keep it simple if you are alone.

## The bilva leaf

The three-leaved bilva is dear to Shiva. Offer it with the smooth side facing the lingam, the stalk pointing toward you. Tradition says to avoid plucking it on certain days; if you buy it, that is fine. Do not worry if you cannot find fresh leaves, as a flower or even clean water offered with love is accepted.

## Japa and jagaran

Jagaran means keeping awake. It is not meant to be a test of the body. Sit with your mala and chant Om Namah Shivaya; see [what the mantra means](/om-namah-shivaya-meaning-of-the-panchakshari-mantra/). Others sing bhajans, read the Shiva Purana, or listen to the Rudram. Some go from temple to temple to see the four poojas. If sleep comes, let it come and then rise again. No one is keeping score.

## Breaking the fast

In the morning, bathe, offer a final puja, and take a little prasada. Many families begin with tulasi or water from the abhishekam, then a light meal. Eat slowly after a long fast. A heavy meal right away does not suit the body.

## If you are unwell or elderly

This matters, so let us say it clearly. Illness, age, pregnancy, diabetes or any medicine that must be taken with food are good reasons not to fast fully. Eat as your doctor advises. Sit rather than stay standing. Sleep a little if you need to. You can keep the day with the mantra, a lamp, and a bilva leaf, and the night with whatever hours you can give. Tradition does not ask for harm to the body; the Lord is said to see the intention.

## What to keep ready

A short list helps, so that you are not running to the kitchen at midnight:

- A lingam or picture, a clean plate or tray, and a bell if you use one.
- Milk, curd, ghee, honey, sugar and water in small bowls; or just milk and water.
- Bilva leaves, flowers, vibhuti, sandal paste and incense.
- A lamp with enough oil or ghee to last, and a spare wick.
- A rudraksha mala for japa, and a book of stotras or the Rudram if you read.
- A shawl, since nights in February can be cool, and a glass of water for yourself if your fast allows.

Do not feel that every item must be present. Where an item is missing, water and a flower stand in for it, and the elders in most families say so.

## Children and the household

If there are children, give them a small share. A child can hold a bilva leaf, ring the bell, or sit for the first prahara and sleep through the rest. Tell them the hunter story rather than the rules, since stories stay longer. Elders may rest between praharas and be woken for the aarti. In a shared home, one person can keep the lamp lit while others take turns.

Shivaratri is a festival of the whole house, and it does not have to be done by one strong person alone. A temple visit at some point of the night, even briefly, is a nice addition if one is nearby and the roads are safe.

## A quiet thought

The word ratri means night, and the tradition reads night also as the darkness within. A vigil is then a way of staying with that darkness until it gives way.
