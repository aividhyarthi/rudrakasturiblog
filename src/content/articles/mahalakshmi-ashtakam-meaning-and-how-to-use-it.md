It is Friday, the lamp is lit, and the house is quiet. Somewhere in the room a voice begins a verse that most people in South Indian homes know by its first words: Namastestu mahamaye. The tune may vary between families, but the line is the same. What is this hymn, and what does it say?

## Where it comes from

The Mahalakshmi Ashtakam, the eight verses to Mahalakshmi, is attributed in tradition to Indra, the king of the gods, and is placed in the Padma Purana. The tale told around it is that Indra, who had lost his kingdom and glory, praised Lakshmi, and she restored him. Whether or not you take the story literally, it explains the spirit of the hymn: it is a prayer from someone who feels something has been lost.

The Sanskrit is easy to chant. Each of the eight verses has two lines, and the hymn ends with a short benefit verse, the phalashruti.

## The opening

The first verse begins: Namastestu mahamaye, shripithe surapujite. In plain words, it says: salutations to you, great power, seated on the sacred seat, worshipped by the gods. It then names the conch, discus and mace in her hands, and ends with salutations to Mahalakshmi. The word mahamaye here is not a complaint about illusion. It praises her as the power that makes the world appear and sustains it.

## The verses in paraphrase

What follows is a short paraphrase, not a translation.

1. The goddess is saluted as great power, worshipped by the gods, holder of conch, discus and mace.
2. She is saluted as the one who rides Garuda, who is terrible to the demon Kolasura, and who removes all sins.
3. She knows all, gives all boons, and is fearsome to all wrongdoers, and removes all sorrows.
4. She grants both enjoyment and liberation, and her form is the sacred mantra.
5. She is without beginning or end, the great power, the great Yogini born of yoga, the support of yogic knowing.
6. She is both gross and subtle, terrible and great, the great power, the great sin-remover.
7. She sits on the lotus, is the supreme Brahman, the supreme goddess, the mother of the world.
8. She is adorned with ornaments and white cloth, and she is the world's mother.

Notice how the hymn moves. It begins with outward things, weapons and ornaments, then turns inward to yoga and the absolute. In this order it quietly teaches that the Lakshmi of the coin and the Lakshmi of the highest knowledge are the same goddess.

## Reading it as a prayer of gratitude

There is a way of reading the hymn that many families find helpful. Indra praises the goddess when he has already been restored, so it is as much a thanksgiving as a request. Try the same on a Friday. Before you ask for anything, say aloud the things you already have: a roof, food, someone who calls you. Then begin the verses. The hymn then becomes a way to see what is already present, and a quiet reminder that Lakshmi is not far away. Even a few lines said with this feeling are enough.

## The phalashruti

The ending verses say that whoever recites this hymn with devotion will receive the goddess's grace, and then list graduated results: recited once, it destroys sin; recited twice, it brings wealth and grain; recited three times, it destroys great enemies. Many traditional hymns end like this. It is best to read such lines as encouragement, a way of saying that regular practice matters, rather than as a guarantee. A prayer is not a financial plan.

## How and when to recite

Friday is the traditional day, and dusk is the natural hour.

1. Clean the place of worship and light a ghee or sesame oil lamp.
2. Place a flower, a few coins or a small bowl of rice before the picture of Lakshmi.
3. Sit down, take a breath, and say her name or a short salutation.
4. Recite the eight verses slowly. If you do not read Sanskrit, use Telugu or English transliteration, and listen to a recording first.
5. Offer a small sweet or fruit and share it at home.

The whole hymn takes about three to five minutes. Some families also recite it on Deepavali evening when Lakshmi puja is performed; see [Deepavali 2026: dates, Dhanteras, Naraka Chaturdashi and Lakshmi puja](/deepavali-2026-dates-dhanteras-naraka-chaturdashi-lakshmi-puja/). ## If Sanskrit is difficult

Many people hesitate because the Sanskrit seems long or hard. It is shorter than it looks. Learn one verse a week. Listen to the verse, say it with the recording, and then without it. Within two months you will know all eight. Pronunciation will improve slowly, and the goddess is not said to mind. If you prefer, you can recite in the language you pray best in, or simply say her name and the opening line each Friday.

## An honest line

If you are in financial trouble, this hymn will not solve it. It can steady the mind, and a steady mind makes better decisions. Please keep doing the sensible things as well: work, save, ask for advice.
