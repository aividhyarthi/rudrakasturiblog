**Source:** Temple tradition of the Ujjaini Mahankali temple, Secunderabad, and the Bonalu festival. Dates are those given by the temple and in local histories.

A festival can start with a vow. Bonalu, now the state festival of Telangana, began, as the story is told, with fear and with a promise.

## The plague of 1813
In 1813, according to the tradition, a battalion from Hyderabad was posted at Ujjain in Madhya Pradesh. While they were there, they heard that cholera was spreading badly in Hyderabad and Secunderabad. A soldier named Suriti Appaiah went to the temple of Mahakali in Ujjain and prayed. He vowed that if the goddess protected his people, he would install her image in Secunderabad.

The plague ended. Appaiah, true to his word, brought an image of Mahakali back and built a shrine in what is now the Ujjaini Mahankali temple in Secunderabad.

## Bonalu
Every year, in the month of Ashadha (July or August), Telangana celebrates Bonalu, which means *bhojanalu*, meals. The festival begins at the Golconda Jagadamba temple and moves through Secunderabad and then Hyderabad. Each Sunday has its own temple, and on each, thousands of women arrive with a *bonam*, a clay or brass pot containing cooked rice with milk and jaggery, decorated with neem leaves, turmeric and vermilion, with a lamp on top. They carry the pot on their heads, with a long dance procession.

The offering is a *thanksgiving* for protection from disease, and a request for the goddess's continuing care of the family and the city.

## Potharaju and the drums
The procession is led by *Potharaju*, a powerful man with a huge moustache, dressed in red, who is said to be the brother of the goddess. He dances with whips, bells and tremendous energy as the drummers beat the rhythm. The beat, the dust and the sound of the *dappu* make the festival one of the liveliest in India. Some women, caught up in the drums, are overtaken by the trance known as *poonakam*.

## Rangam
On the morning after the main festival day, at the temple, a woman stands on an unbaked earthen pot, in front of the goddess. She speaks in the voice of the goddess and gives a *Bhavishyavani*, the forecast of the year ahead: rain and harvest, the safety of the city, warnings of trouble. This is the *Rangam*. It is heard by thousands and reported in the press.

It is a rare example of a ritual in which a woman speaks for the goddess in public and is heard by a city.

## What it says
- **A vow is a bond.** The temple exists because a man kept a promise.
- **Faith and public health meet.** A plague produced a shrine and a festival of thanksgiving.
- **Food is worship.** The offering is rice, jaggery and curd, shared afterwards.
- **A woman may speak for the goddess.** The Rangam is a very old practice and still draws huge crowds.

## Visiting
Bonalu has several Sundays. The big temples are crowded. Follow police and temple instructions, and carry water. In the middle of the festival, public transport and roads around Secunderabad and Lal Darwaza are busy, so plan ahead.
