When your neighbour in a Jain family lights a lamp on Deepavali night, ask her what it is for. She may not say Lakshmi, and she may not say Rama. She may say, quietly, that it is for Mahavira. It is easy to miss that the same night means a different thing in a different home.

## The night at Pavapuri

Lord Mahavira (వర్ధమాన మహావీరుడు), the 24th and last Tirthankara of the present age, lived about seventy-two years, according to Jain tradition. He spent his last rainy season at Pavapuri in present-day Bihar, in the hall of a king named Hastipala. The tradition says that after teaching for a long time, he sat in his final discourse through the night, and in the last part of it he attained nirvana, leaving the body and the cycle of rebirth for ever.

Tradition places this in the sixth century BCE. Many Jain calendars put it at 527 BCE, and the Vira Nirvana era is counted from it, but modern scholars discuss other years. The honest way to say it is: the sixth century BCE, as tradition holds, with the exact year not settled.

## Why lamps?

The Kalpa Sutra, a revered Svetambara text, is the usual source for what happened next. It tells that on that night the kings gathered, and said that since the moon of knowledge, the great light, had departed, they would light lamps of ordinary matter to stand in its place. This is the root of the Jain Diwali. The lamp is not a celebration of victory over a demon. It is a quiet answer to a loss: the light has gone out, so we light a small one ourselves.

The Kalpa Sutra is a Svetambara text, and Digambara tradition also keeps the day, with some differences in how it is done and what is emphasised. The month is named differently too: the new moon is called Kartika Amavasya in the north Indian (Purnimanta) reckoning, and Ashwin Amavasya in the Amanta reckoning followed in the South and in Telugu panchangams. It is the same night, which is why many Jains simply say it is Diwali.

## The next morning

The tradition does not end in grief. Gautama Svami (Indrabhuti Gautama), Mahavira's chief disciple, was away that night. On hearing the news, he is said to have been overcome with sorrow, and then, at dawn, he attained kevala jnana, perfect knowledge. In this telling, the guru's departure does not leave the disciple empty; it frees him. That is why many Jain families worship Gautama Svami and Mahalakshmi (as a symbol of the wealth of liberation or of a good account book) together on this day, a point on which Jain practice varies by community.

## The new year and the accounts

The next day begins the Jain new year, the Vira Nirvana Samvat, in many traditions. Jain merchants in Gujarat, Rajasthan and elsewhere traditionally begin new account books at this time. In the South, Jain communities in Karnataka, Tamil Nadu and Telangana keep the day with temple worship, and old Jain centres such as Shravanabelagola keep the day in their own way.

Think for a moment about how unusual this is. Most festivals mark a birth or a victory. This one marks a passing, and the first lamp in it is lit out of loss. Jain teaching holds that Mahavira's liberation is the goal every soul is working towards, so the sorrow is also joy. It is a hard double feeling, and perhaps that is why the night is kept so quietly.

## How it sits beside the Hindu festival

The two are not in conflict, though they are not the same. In a Hindu home the night is about Lakshmi, about Rama's return or Krishna's defeat of Naraka, and the victory of light. In a Jain home it is about nirvana and the knowledge that lasts. Both speak of lamps and of dark. A child in a mixed neighbourhood sees the same lights and hears different stories, and that does no harm. ## What families do

In many Jain homes the morning has temple worship, the evening has a lamp and prayers with hymns in praise of Mahavira. Some observe a fast on the preceding days, and some hold an all-night recitation. Many Jains also avoid crackers and loud noise out of ahimsa, since the festival is about the soul and not about spectacle. Practices differ widely, so ask your own community.

## Where this comes from

The main source is Jain tradition, with the Kalpa Sutra (traditionally attributed to Bhadrabahu) as the best-known text for the lamps. Pavapuri as the place is the constant of Jain tradition. The details of Gautama Svami's dawn are found in Jain narratives, and the order of events is accepted widely, but I am not sure of the exact chapters and verses. The ancient Buddhist texts also mention the death of a teacher called Nigantha Nataputta at Pava, and many scholars link that to Mahavira; this is an outside witness, but the details vary.

## A question to carry

When a teacher or an elder in your life is gone, do you light the lamp and wait for dawn, or do you wait for the light to return on its own?
