**Source:** Valmiki Ramayana, Yuddha Kanda (chapters 7 and 111 to 114). Later traditions about her parentage and her later life are noted.

The first thing most people learn about the people of Lanka is that they were wicked. The second thing, if they read on, is that the epic does not leave it there. Among the rakshasas there are people of great character. Vibhishana. Trijata. And Mandodari.

## The wise woman in the palace
Mandodari was the chief queen of Ravana, the daughter, in the tradition, of the architect Maya, and the mother of the warrior Indrajit. She is described as beautiful and learned. In the Valmiki text, she appears at the moments when Ravana is most in danger, to warn him.

When news reached Lanka that Hanuman had come, set the city on fire and returned, she spoke to her husband. In Yuddha Kanda 7, she begged him to let go of Sita:

In the text she says, in effect: *return her, my lord, and make peace with Rama. He is no ordinary man. The one who burned Lanka is only his servant.*

Her argument was not sentimental. It was strategic and moral at the same time. Rama, she said, was not a man to be defeated. And holding another man's wife is *adharma*, which eats away at a king's life.

Ravana did not listen. This was not the first time he had refused wise advice. His brother Vibhishana had left. His minister Malyavan had spoken. His son Prahasta had hesitated. Nobody could get through.

## The night after
When Rama's army was camped across the sea, she tried again. And again, as Indrajit and Kumbhakarna fell, she urged him to stop.

By the end she was not arguing any longer. She was simply there.

## The lament
When Ravana was killed by Rama, the epic gives Mandodari one of its most moving passages (Yuddha Kanda 111). She comes to the battlefield and finds her husband on the ground. She says what she has long known. Her words are not spiteful. They are not triumphant. They are the words of a wife who loved a great man who destroyed himself.

She says, in effect:

*You could conquer the gods, and you could not conquer your own desire. I told you many times. You did not listen. Now you lie here, and I cannot hate you, and I cannot say I was not right.*

There is an old saying that grief is the price of love, and this scene is a lesson in it. She is not made to disown her husband, or to curse Rama. She simply weeps, and the poet lets her.

Rama, who has watched this, tells Vibhishana to perform the funeral rites for Ravana with full honour. *Enmity ends with death*, he says. *He is as dear to me as he is to you.* (Yuddha Kanda 112.)

## A woman remembered every morning
The tradition lists five women as the *Panchakanya*, whose names, it says, destroy sin when remembered: Ahalya, Draupadi, Sita, Tara and Mandodari. The list is interesting. They are not all calm or conventional. They are women who faced very hard situations with courage, or sorrow, or both. Mandodari belongs there because of her moral clarity in a house that had lost its own.

## What does she teach?
- **Wise advice can be given and not taken.** Mandodari did all she could. That is also a kind of success.
- **You can love someone and tell them they are wrong.** She never stopped being his wife, and she never stopped being honest.
- **Do not mistake the enemy for a monster.** The epic gives Ravana a wife who loves him, a brother who warns him, a son who dies for him.
- **Grief does not have to take sides.** She mourned a husband who had done wrong, and the poet does not call her wrong.

## The verse that keeps her name alive
Families who know the Panchakanya say it as a morning verse: *ahalya draupadi sita tara mandodari tatha, panchakanyah smaren nityam maha-pataka-nashanam.* "Ahalya, Draupadi, Sita, Tara and Mandodari: let one remember these five maidens each day, for they destroy great sins." Scholars have long asked why women with such complicated stories are held up as models. One answer is that the tradition values *how a person faces a hard situation* more than whether her life was tidy. Another is that it knows virtue is not the same as luck.

## A note on the later stories
In some later retellings, after the war Vibhishana married Mandodari and she lived on in Lanka. Others say she went into mourning for good. The Valmiki text only says that Vibhishana took care of the queens, and the poet is silent about the rest of her life. We do not need to fill that silence.

## A closing thought
Ravana is one of the most powerful figures in Indian literature. And behind him, there is a woman whose advice, if heard, would have saved him. Many families, many companies and even countries have a Mandodari who was not heard. If you are one, you are in good company. If you have one near you, listen.
