Maybe someone in your family has been waiting for a wedding that keeps slipping. The horoscopes matched, the families met, and then something fell through. Then a relative says, quietly, as if passing on a secret: "Go to Mangadu. Take lemons."

That is how many people first hear of this vow. It is one of the best-known practices at the Kamakshi Amman temple in Mangadu, on the western edge of Chennai. Here is what devotees describe, where the legend comes from, and what you should honestly keep in mind before you start.

## Why Mangadu?

The name suggests a mango forest, and the temple's tradition places the goddess here before she went to Kanchipuram. The story, as the temple tells it, is that Kamakshi first performed a fierce penance at Mangadu, standing on one foot amid five fires (the *panchagni* tapas). Only afterwards, tradition says, did she go to Kanchipuram, where she worshipped Shiva in the form of a sand lingam under a mango tree and was joined with him. You can read that second story in our piece on [Kamakshi of Kanchipuram](/kamakshi-of-kanchipuram-the-goddess-who-embraced-the-sand-lingam/).

Local tradition also says the heat of the goddess's penance was so great that it needed to be cooled, and some accounts credit Adi Shankaracharya with installing a Sri Chakra here for that purpose. No text prescribes this, so it is best taken as the temple's own telling, not as established history.

Seen that way, the lemon, a cooling, sour fruit, is offered to a goddess who stood in fire. That is the idea devotees give for the vow. It is an explanation from the tradition, not a rule written in a scripture.

## How the six-week vow is described

Accounts agree on the outline, and differ on small points. This is the version devotees most often describe:

- You choose a day of the week and keep to it for **six weeks in a row**. Fridays, Sundays and Mondays are the days most often named, and some accounts mention Tuesdays for people praying about work.
- On the first visit you bring **two fresh lemons**, with the usual offerings of coconut, flowers and betel leaves.
- The priest offers the lemons to the goddess, keeps one at her feet, and gives **one blessed lemon** back to you. You take it home and keep it at your prayer altar.
- On each following visit you bring **the blessed lemon from last week and two new ones**. The priest takes them, offers the two new ones, and returns one fresh blessed lemon.
- On the **sixth visit** you bring your last blessed lemon and two fresh ones, and, as the vow is described, no lemon is returned. That marks the end of the vow.
- Many people then return once more to offer milk or a sweet to the goddess as thanks, and share it as prasadam.

A magazine report in *India Today* in 1983 already described people travelling to Mangadu for this, which tells you the practice is at least four decades old and likely older.

## What varies, and why you should ask

Please treat the list above as "how it is commonly told," not as a rule I can vouch for. Practices at a living temple change, and different priests explain it differently. Ask these at the counter or the priest before you begin:

- Is it six weeks now, and does the vow have to be on one fixed weekday?
- How should the lemons be brought, and how many?
- What do they advise if you fall ill, are travelling, or have a monthly cycle that week? Families differ a lot on this, and the temple's own guidance is the one that counts.
- Is there any fee for the lemon offering? It should be the price of the lemons and the usual archana, nothing more. If anyone offers a "special" paid ritual with a guaranteed result, walk away.

## What the lemon at home is for

The blessed lemon you keep at the altar is a reminder, week by week, that you have taken up a vow. Keep it clean and in a place of its own. If it dries naturally that is fine, as families commonly say, but it should not be left to rot. Many people say a short prayer to it each morning: no more than your name and your wish.

## An honest word

Faith is a strength, and a vow like this can give a long, anxious wait a shape: a day to look forward to, a promise you are keeping. That is real, and it is worth something.

It is not a guarantee, and nobody can promise you a result. Please do not let it replace the ordinary steps: talking openly with your family, meeting people, widening the search. No one should be pressed into marriage, or made to feel their worth waits on a lemon. If the delay is weighing on you, speak to someone you trust as well.

## A question to carry

When you take up a vow, what are you really asking for: the outcome, or the steadiness to wait for it?

## Where this comes from

The legend of the goddess's penance at Mangadu is the temple's own sthala tradition, and Kanchipuram's mango tree story is told in the Puranic tradition of that city. The lemon vow comes from devotees' practice, described in the 1983 *India Today* report and in many temple guides since; there is no scripture that prescribes it.
