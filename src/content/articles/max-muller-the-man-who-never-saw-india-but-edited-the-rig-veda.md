Few scholars have been loved and criticised in India as much as Friedrich Max Muller. He is the man who gave the Rig Veda to the printing press, and the man whose early letters make many readers wince. Both are true.

## A German in Oxford
He was born in 1823 in Dessau, Germany, and studied Sanskrit in Leipzig and Berlin. He moved to Paris and then to England, where he settled in Oxford and spent the rest of his life. He never went to India, though he often said that he wanted to.

## The Rig Veda
In 1847 the East India Company agreed to fund his plan to edit the Rig Veda along with the medieval commentary of Sayana, using manuscripts from India and Europe. The task took more than twenty years. The six volumes appeared between 1849 and 1874. It was the first printed edition of the oldest Hindu text, and scholars everywhere began to use it.

## Sacred Books of the East
In 1879 he began a series that would eventually run to 50 volumes, the *Sacred Books of the East*, translating texts from Hindu, Buddhist, Jain, Zoroastrian, Chinese and Islamic traditions, with the help of other scholars. It was completed in 1910, after his death. He himself translated the Upanishads for it.

## His early view
It is important to be honest about the less comfortable part. In a letter of 1866 to a friend, Max Muller wrote that the ancient religion of India was doomed, and that if Christianity did not step in, it would be somebody's fault. His employers, the East India Company and later the missionary circle in Britain, had their own interest in how the Vedas were read, and he knew it.

## How it changed
Over the years, his tone shifted. He wrote with growing respect of Indian philosophy, and spoke up against those who regarded it as inferior. He corresponded with Indian scholars and reformers, such as the Brahmo Samaj leaders, and in 1896 he met Swami Vivekananda at Oxford. In 1898 he published a biography of Ramakrishna, titled *Ramakrishna: His Life and Sayings*, which brought the saint to a wider audience. He is also said to have remarked that the Upanishads were a source of consolation to him in life.

## The criticism
He is still the subject of arguments. Some scholars say his dating of the Vedas was speculative. Some Indian writers say that his work helped to shape the colonial idea of Indian history, such as the Aryan invasion theory. Others point out that he did not invent that theory and that he later cautioned against taking the idea of race too far. The debate goes on.

## What remains
He died in Oxford in 1900. His library and papers are in the Bodleian. In Delhi and elsewhere, there are institutions named after him, Max Mueller Bhavan, the German cultural centre, though the name has often been debated.
