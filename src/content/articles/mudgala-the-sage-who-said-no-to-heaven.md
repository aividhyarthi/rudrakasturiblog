**Source:** Mahabharata, Vana Parva (the Mudgala episode, told to Yudhishthira).

Almost everyone is raised to hope for heaven. In the Mahabharata there is a man who was offered it directly and asked a very practical question: *what is the catch?*

## A life on gleanings
Mudgala was a sage who lived at Kurukshetra on very little. He practised *unchha-vritti*, collecting the grains left in a harvested field. He had a rule: he would keep a certain measure and cook it twice a month, on the full-moon and new-moon days, and give most of it to any guest who came.

This was no empty vow. The Mahabharata tells us that the sage Durvasa, known for his temper, once tested him. Durvasa arrived, naked and shouting, on each of several festival days. Each time Mudgala fed him the entire meal, and went hungry himself for the next fortnight. Durvasa found no trace of anger, ill-feeling or complaint in him. The old sage was moved, and blessed him.

## The messenger from heaven
Soon after, a divine chariot arrived, with a messenger of the gods to take him to heaven in his own body. It was an honour that few received. The messenger described heaven: its gardens, its music, its beauty.

Mudgala did not climb aboard. He asked: *What are the merits of heaven, and what are its faults?*

## The honest answer
The messenger, surprised, answered truthfully. In heaven, he said, there is great happiness, but one is there only as long as one's merit lasts. When it is used up, one falls back to earth. Even in heaven one sees others higher than oneself, which causes pain, and one sees the fall of others and knows one's own is coming.

Mudgala said, *then I do not want it.* He sent the chariot away.

## What he did instead
The text says that he turned from works to knowledge, and set himself to realise the Self. He lived simply, neither longing for nor fearing anything, and in time attained *moksha*, the freedom that has no fall at the end.

## Why this story matters
Many people think of religion as a way to get something: better health, a better rebirth, a pleasant afterlife. The Upanishads and the Gita both ask us to look further. The Gita says (8.16) that all worlds up to the world of Brahma are subject to return, but whoever reaches the Supreme does not return. Mudgala's story is a narrative of that verse.

## What it teaches
- **Read the small print.** Even a very good offer can have a fall at the end.
- **Generosity is not for reward.** Mudgala gave everything away before heaven was ever mentioned.
- **Look for what does not run out.** The tradition keeps pointing to it.
- **Contentment is a form of wealth.** He lived on gleanings and was never poor.

## A question for you
What would you have done? Many of us would have taken the chariot and thought about the rest later. The value of the story is that it makes you ask.
