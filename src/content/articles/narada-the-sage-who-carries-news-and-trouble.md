Have you noticed that in many of our old stories, just when everything seems calm, a sage walks in, says "Narayana, Narayana," and within a few minutes somebody is in trouble? That sage is Narada (నారదుడు). The question worth asking is not whether he is a troublemaker, but why the tradition kept him that way.

## The wanderer with the veena

Narada is a devarshi, a sage among the gods, who travels freely through all the worlds. He carries a veena, sometimes called Mahati, and sings the name of Narayana. He is a messenger, a teacher, a gossip, a counsellor, and in some stories a student. Kings give him a seat; gods fear his arrival and welcome it.

He does not own a place. That may be why he is trusted. A person who is attached to no court can say what courts do not wish to hear.

## Narada and Valmiki

The Ramayana begins with Narada. The Bala Kanda says that Valmiki asked him: who today is a man of every good quality? Narada gave a short answer, the story of Rama. This became the seed of the epic. If you read [the story of Valmiki](/valmiki-the-hunter-who-became-the-first-poet/), you will see how this question, and then the krauncha bird's cry, together made the first poem.

It is worth noticing that Narada gives the story to a poet and then steps aside. He starts things and does not stay for the credit.

## Narada and the unborn Prahlada

The Bhagavata tradition has one of its most tender Narada scenes. When the demon king Hiranyakashipu went away to perform austerities, Indra took the pregnant queen Kayadhu, intending to harm the child. Narada stopped him and took Kayadhu to his ashram. There, tradition says, he taught her about Vishnu and devotion, and the child in her womb heard it. That child was Prahlada.

Years later the boy told his classmates about this: the teaching had come to him before birth. The Bhagavata, in its seventh skandha, tells this. Think about how a few sentences, said to a frightened woman, reached a child who would not bow even to his father the king.

## The Bhakti Sutras

A short text called the Narada Bhakti Sutra, a set of aphorisms on love of God, is attributed to him. It speaks of bhakti as intense love for the Lord, and says that this love is its own fruit. Scholars are unsure of its date and authorship, and treat it as a later work in his name. Even so, many readers love it for how plainly it defines devotion without insisting on scholarship or status.

## Why his mischief ends in dharma

Narada's reputation for stirring trouble is old. In the Bhagavata Purana (Skandha 10), he visits Dwaraka and is amazed to find Krishna present, at the same time, in every one of his many wives' homes. He goes away convinced about who Krishna truly is. In the Krishna tellings he is also said to have warned Kamsa about the gods being born among the Yadavas, which hastened Kamsa's fall, though the details differ between texts. In other tellings he instructs the young Dhruva in the forest, and he turns away the sons of Daksha from producing creation by showing them the vastness of the world. Daksha's anger at this is part of the same Puranic story.

Even Narada has flaws in some tellings. Puranas say he once grew proud of his devotion, and Vishnu humbled him. Tradition does not hide this.

Look at the pattern. A quarrel exposes a hidden wrong, or makes someone grow up, or pushes a plan that the gods could not start themselves. Does he do it knowing the end? Tradition says yes, and that his mischief is an outer face of compassion. You do not have to take it as a rule. You can also read it as a reminder that peace that depends on a lie is not peace.

## What the stories ask of us

It is easy to enjoy Narada as a character and miss the discomfort he is designed to cause. He never settles for a polite surface. When a king is comfortable in a wrong, or a household is quiet because everyone is afraid, he arrives. Our grandmothers sometimes call a person who stirs up an uncomfortable truth a Narada, half in joke. The joke has some respect hidden in it.

At the same time, the stories do not praise gossip. Narada's words always carry something true, and he is never cruel for its own sake. That is a hard line to walk, and most of us fall off it daily.

## Temples and memory

Few temples are dedicated solely to Narada. You will see him instead, veena in hand, carved in the pillars and walls of Vishnu temples, and as a storyteller in harikatha and kirtan performances across Andhra, Tamil Nadu and Karnataka. Narada Jayanti is observed in some regions, and the dates differ by calendar. For the tradition of temples and festivals, see [Temples](/temples/).

## Where this comes from

Narada's meeting with Valmiki is in the Bala Kanda of the Valmiki Ramayana. The Kayadhu and Prahlada story is in the Bhagavata Purana (Skandha 7), and the Dwaraka scene is in Skandha 10. The Bhakti Sutras are a separate text of uncertain date. Other tellings, including the Mahabharata and different Puranas, add their own Narada stories, and details vary.
