*నరక చతుర్దశి నాడు తెల్లవారకముందే నువ్వుల నూనెతో స్నానం చేసే ఆచారం ఉంది; ఇది శ్రీకృష్ణుడు నరకాసురుని సంహరించిన కథతో ముడిపడి ఉంది. భాగవతం కృష్ణునికి విజయం ఇస్తుంది, సత్యభామ పాత్ర ప్రాంతీయ సంప్రదాయం అని ఈ వ్యాసం నిజాయితీగా చెబుతుంది.*

Somewhere in your house, someone is up in the dark. A lamp is lit, warm oil is being rubbed into the scalp, and an elder says, "Get up, it is already late." Outside it is still night. This is Naraka Chaturdashi (నరక చతుర్దశి), the day before the main Deepavali night in many homes, and in Tamil Nadu it is Deepavali morning itself.

Why are we bathing before sunrise, and who was Naraka?

## The story, as the texts tell it

Naraka, also called Bhauma or Bhaumasura, ruled Pragjyotisha, a city usually linked with the Assam region. The name Bhauma means son of Bhumi, the Earth. By tradition he was born of the Earth goddess and Vishnu in his Varaha form, and he grew proud and cruel.

The Bhagavata Purana (Book 10) says he took the earrings of Aditi, mother of the devas, and held about sixteen thousand one hundred princesses captive. Indra came to Krishna for help. Krishna rode Garuda to Pragjyotisha, broke through its fortifications, and killed Naraka with his discus. He then freed the princesses. They asked for his protection, and he accepted them with honour. The Vishnu Purana and the Harivamsha also tell this story, with differences of detail.

Notice what the text actually stresses. The victory is Krishna's. The rescue of the captives is the heart of it.

## Where Satyabhama comes in

Here honesty matters. In the Bhagavata, Satyabhama travels with Krishna on Garuda. That much is in the text. But the popular version, that she struck the killing blow, is a regional tradition, strong in Telugu, Tamil and Kannada homes.

Tradition holds that Naraka had a boon that only his mother could kill him, and that Satyabhama is Bhumi's own form. Krishna, wounded or stunned in the fight, lets her take up the bow. This is retold in later Puranic and regional versions rather than in the central Bhagavata telling. It also lives in Kuchipudi, where *Bhama Kalapam* keeps Satyabhama at the centre of the stage.

Neither telling cancels the other. One is the text, the other is what a people did with it. When you tell your children, you can say both.

## The oil bath before dawn

The custom is the *abhyanga snana*, a bath with sesame oil, taken before sunrise. Many families call it Ganga Snanam (గంగా స్నానం). In Tamil homes the greeting that morning is often "Gangā snānam āchā?", which means "Have you had your Ganga bath?" Tradition holds that on this day the Ganga is present in the water and Lakshmi in the oil.

How families keep it, in simple steps:

1. Get up while it is still dark, before the stars fade.
2. Light a lamp, and put a little warm sesame oil on the head and body.
3. Wait a few minutes, then bathe with warm water, often with a little shikakai or gram flour.
4. Put on new or clean clothes, light the lamps, and have the sweets.

No one needs a river. A bucket of water will do. What the day asks for is the waking up.

## Why before dawn?

Ask three elders and you may get three answers. One will say that the dark hour before sunrise is the quiet, clear hour, the brahma muhurta, and a day begun then feels longer. Another will say the bath washes off the night, just as Krishna's victory ended a long night for the captives. A third will simply say, "We have always done it." All three are honest answers. The text does not command the oil bath in the story; the custom grew around the story, as customs do.

There is also a gentle practical side. Deepavali is a heavy few days of cooking, visitors and crackers. A calm bath with warm oil, taken slowly while the house is still, gives you one unhurried hour before the noise begins.

## What about the 16,000?

The number is large and the texts say plainly that it is large. Many teachers read it as a way of speaking about every person who was shut away from society and was brought back with dignity. Whether you read it as history or as a symbol, the point is the same: the king who frees them does not leave them in shame. Do you know anyone in your own circle who needs to be welcomed back in the same way?

## Yama tarpana and the lamps

On this day, some families also offer water mixed with sesame to Yama and to the departed, called Yama tarpana. In others, a lamp is placed facing south at dusk. This belongs to the same cluster of customs as [Yama Deepam](/yama-deepam-the-prince-who-was-to-die-on-the-fourth-night/). It is not universal, so follow what your elders do.

## Where this comes from

The Narakasura story is in the Bhagavata Purana (Book 10), the Vishnu Purana (Book 5), and the Harivamsha. Satyabhama's role as the one who strikes is not in the Bhagavata's account; it comes from regional retellings and stage tradition. The oil-bath custom is traditional and is described in later dharmashastra and panchangam writing, not in the Narakasura story itself. Tellings differ by region, and there is no single correct version.

## A question to carry

Is there someone, or some part of you, that has been shut away for a long time, waiting for a morning to be called back?
