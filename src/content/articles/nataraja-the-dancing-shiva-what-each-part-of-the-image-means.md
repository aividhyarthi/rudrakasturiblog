Stand in front of a Nataraja bronze, even a small one in a museum or on a friend's shelf, and your eye starts to travel. The drum, the flame, the lifted foot, the flowing hair, the circle of fire around him. Nothing is there by accident. Do you know what each part is saying?

Nataraja means the lord of dance, Shiva as the dancer at the centre of all things. In Tamil he is Koothan, and at his great temple in Chidambaram he is called Sabhapati, lord of the hall. In Telugu he is నటరాజు.

## The damaru, the drum

In his upper right hand Shiva holds a small hourglass drum, the damaru. Tradition reads its sound as the first sound, the vibration from which creation arises. Its shape, two triangles meeting at a point, is taken to stand for the balance of opposites. When Nataraja sounds it, the world begins.

## Agni, the flame

In the upper left hand he holds a flame. Where the drum begins, the fire ends: it is the fire of dissolution, which burns the world down so that it can be made new. The two hands, drum and fire, face each other like creation and destruction in balance.

## The raised hand and the lifted foot

His lower right hand is lifted in abhaya mudra, the gesture that says do not fear. His lower left hand points across the body to his raised left foot. Tradition reads this as pointing to refuge: the lifted foot is where a seeker can take shelter. Many a devotee has heard the verse that says, in effect, this foot is the way out of the cycle of rebirth.

## Apasmara, the dwarf beneath

Under his right foot is a small figure, Apasmara or Muyalakan in Tamil. He stands for ignorance, forgetfulness of the Self. Notice that Shiva does not destroy him. The foot rests on him, keeping him in place, because ignorance in this reading is not killed but ruled over. Apasmara looks up, often holding a cobra, calm and unafraid. Some readings see him as the ego that must be placed at the feet of the divine.

## The prabha, the ring of fire

The circle of flames around the dancer is the prabhamandala. It represents the universe, the whole field of movement and change, in which the dance goes on. At its base are small tongues of flame, and the arc rises above his head. The dance is not separate from the world; the world is the dance.

## The hair, the river and the crescent

If you look closely, the locks of his hair fly out in the dance. On them sits a crescent moon, and often a small figure of Ganga, the river. Sometimes a skull and a datura flower are shown too. These are the same signs of Shiva you meet in other images, brought into the dancing form.

## Chidambaram and the Chola bronzes

The great home of Nataraja is Chidambaram. Tradition holds that the Lord danced here, the Ananda Tandava, the dance of bliss, before the sages. The sanctum has no ordinary lingam for its main worship but the space itself, the akasha, behind a curtain. If you want to learn more about the temple, read our article on [Chidambaram, the temple of space](/chidambaram-nataraja-temple-the-temple-of-space/).

The familiar bronze Nataraja, with ring of fire and the lifted foot, is most associated with the Chola period of south India. Scholars usually place the flourishing of this art between roughly the tenth and the twelfth century, though exact dates for particular bronzes are debated. The Cholas cast these images by the lost-wax method, and many still travel in festival processions. The skill of the Chola artists made the image so well-known that it has become a symbol of Indian art itself.

## The Tamil voice

In Tamil Shaiva tradition, the saints of the Tevaram sang of the Lord who dances at Thillai, and the poet-saint Tirumular and later teachers wrote of the dance as an inner reality as much as an outer image. The hall where he dances is called the Chit Sabha, the hall of consciousness, and some teachers say it is also within the heart of the devotee. That is why a visit to Chidambaram is often described less as seeing an idol and more as being in the presence of space itself.

## The five acts

The dance, in the Shaiva tradition, is the pancha-krityas, the five acts of Shiva:

1. **Srishti**, creation, shown by the drum.
2. **Sthiti**, preservation, shown by the hand held in blessing.
3. **Samhara**, destruction or dissolution, shown by the flame.
4. **Tirobhava**, concealment, the veiling of the Self, shown by the foot on Apasmara.
5. **Anugraha**, grace, the release, shown by the lifted foot.

Notice that the last two are the unusual ones. Many gods are shown as creators and destroyers. Here the tradition adds that Shiva hides the truth, and that he himself lifts the veil.

## What you might take from it

Why would the Supreme be shown as a dancer? One answer teachers give is that the world is not a mistake to be escaped but a movement to be joined, with balance, rhythm and a free foot raised in grace.

You can bring Nataraja into daily worship simply. A small bronze on your puja shelf, a lamp lit before him on Monday, the Panchakshari repeated slowly. For the day he is worshipped especially, see our page on [Pradosham](/pradosham/).
