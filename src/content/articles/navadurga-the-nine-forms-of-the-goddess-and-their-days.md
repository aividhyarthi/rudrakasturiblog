Navaratri comes, and one family hears Shailaputri, Brahmacharini on the first two days, while next door the temple has dressed the Goddess as Bala Tripura Sundari and then Gayatri. Are they different goddesses? Who is right?

The short answer is that both are right, and both are the same Mother. Let us see how.

## The nine forms

In the North Indian order, which is also found in the Devi Kavacha and in many texts on Navaratri, the Goddess is worshipped in nine forms, one per night. Tradition gives each a meaning:

1. **Shailaputri** (day one). Daughter of the mountain Himalaya, she rides a bull and holds a trident and lotus. She is the beginning, the root.
2. **Brahmacharini** (day two). The Goddess in her penance, walking barefoot, holding a rosary and a water pot. She stands for discipline and steady effort.
3. **Chandraghanta** (day three). She wears a crescent shaped like a bell on her forehead. She is said to give courage and drive away fear.
4. **Kushmanda** (day four). Tradition says she smiled the universe into being. She is the creative energy.
5. **Skandamata** (day five). Mother of Skanda, Kartikeya, whom she holds on her lap. The tender mother.
6. **Katyayani** (day six). Born to the sage Katyayana, she is the warrior who slew Mahishasura, in one telling.
7. **Kalaratri** (day seven). Dark and fierce, the night that ends the night. Frightening in form, she is held to be kind to her devotees.
8. **Mahagauri** (day eight). Radiantly fair, washed clean by her penance. Peace and purity.
9. **Siddhidatri** (day nine). The giver of accomplishments, seated on a lotus.

Some families add that Shailaputri is the Goddess's first birth as the daughter of the mountain after she, as Sati, left her body. Others tell of different parentage. Accounts differ, and no one tradition has a monopoly.

## The colours

Many families wear a colour for each day: red, royal blue, yellow, green and so on through nine colours, often posted on social media each year. We should be honest: there is no single ancient list. The order is usually set by the weekday on which Navaratri begins, and it changes from year to year. If your family follows a colour, use the one your temple or local calendar gives. If you do not, no loss.

## What the South does

In Andhra, Telangana, Tamil Nadu and Karnataka, a different custom is better known. Temples dress the Goddess in a series of alankarams, each form drawn from the Puranas and the Lalita tradition. A commonly seen list includes Bala Tripura Sundari, Gayatri, Annapurna, Lalita Tripura Sundari, Saraswati, Mahalakshmi, Durga, Mahishasuramardini and Rajarajeshwari. You may see Kanaka Durga in Vijayawada dressed like this over the nine days; see the story of [Kanaka Durga](/kanaka-durga-of-vijayawada-the-goddess-on-the-hill-of-indra/).

Alankaram names vary by temple, and the order is not fixed. A temple in Madurai may follow one list, one in Vijayawada another. That is not an error; it is the long local life of devotion.

At home, many South Indian families do not name Navadurga at all, but arrange Golu, the display of dolls on steps, and worship Lakshmi, Saraswati and Durga in the three sets of three nights. This is also a very old pattern: the first three nights to Durga, the next three to Lakshmi, the last three to Saraswati.

## How to keep the nine nights

You do not need to do everything. A simple practice:

- Light a lamp each evening and place a flower before the Goddess.
- Read a small stanza or recite a name. The Lalita Sahasranama, the Devi Mahatmya (see [Durga Saptashati](/durga-saptashati-what-it-is-and-how-to-read-it/)) and the Devi stotras are all good.
- Offer a simple sweet and share it.
- On the eighth or ninth day, many families call girls, kanyas, and feed them with respect.

## A simple plan for each night

If you want something small to hold on to for the nine days, this works in many homes:

- Draw a rangoli or kolam at the door each morning, a little different each day.
- In the evening, light a lamp and say the name of that day's form nine times, or once with attention.
- Offer a flower, and the colour of the day if you like, but never make colour a source of worry. If red hibiscus is all you have, it is enough.
- Make one simple prasadam, such as sundal, payasam or fruit, and share it with a neighbour.

Some families also keep a kalasha, a pot of water with mango leaves and a coconut, in the puja room for the nine days, and it is left undisturbed until Vijayadashami. If you do this, ask an elder about the customs for your home. Practices for the kalasha and the sowing of grain vary a great deal, and no single rule fits all.

## Fierce and gentle

Notice how the nine move from the mountain girl to the ascetic, to the warrior, the dark night, and finally the giver of fulfilment. The tradition reads this as the soul's own journey. There is a place for fear (Kalaratri) and a place for peace (Mahagauri). If you are drawn to the dark form, our piece on [Kali](/kali-the-goddess-people-fear-and-the-mother-they-pray-to/) may help.

## One Mother

When someone asks which list is correct, perhaps the best reply is that Navaratri is not a list at all. It is nine nights set aside to sit with the Mother. Whether you call her by nine names or by one, she hears.
