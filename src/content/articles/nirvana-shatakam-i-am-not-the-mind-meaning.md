This is a hymn that has become popular on playlists and in yoga classes, but its story is older than any of that.

## The story
When young Shankara reached the Narmada and met Govinda Bhagavatpada, the teacher asked him, *Ko bhavan?* Who are you? The boy, so the tradition says, replied with six verses. They became the Nirvana Shatakam, also called the Atma Shatakam, the six verses on the self.

## The method
The hymn is built on a simple method that is called *neti neti*, not this, not this. Instead of saying what the self is, it says what it is not. You take away the things you normally identify with, one by one, and see what is left.

## The refrain
Each verse ends with the same line:
*Chidananda rupah Shivoham Shivoham.*
I am the form of pure consciousness and bliss, I am Shiva.

## The six verses, in plain words
**Verse 1.** *Mano buddhi ahankara chittani naaham.* I am not the mind, the intellect, the ego or the memory. I am not the ears, the tongue, the nose or the eyes. I am not space, earth, fire or air.

**Verse 2.** I am not the life-breath, nor the five vital airs, nor the seven body elements, nor the five sheaths. I am not the organs of speech, hands or feet.

**Verse 3.** I have no hatred or desire, no greed, no delusion, no pride or envy. I do not seek wealth, pleasure, duty or even liberation.

**Verse 4.** I am not merit or sin, pleasure or pain. I am not the mantras, the holy places, the Vedas or the sacrifices. I am not the experience, the one who experiences or the thing experienced.

**Verse 5.** I have no death, no fear, no caste. I have no father, mother, birth, friend, teacher or student.

**Verse 6.** I have no form and no changes. I am everywhere, in all the senses. I am not bound, nor free.

## What the verses are really doing
Some people read them and feel uneasy: if I have no father or mother, am I denying my family? That is not what it means. The hymn is not telling you to drop your roles. It is telling you that the awareness which watches all of them is not any one of them.

## Why it appeals
Part of its charm is that it does not ask you to believe anything. It asks you to try something: look at your thoughts, and then ask who is looking.

## Listening and reading
There are many recordings, and people often learn it by ear first. When you read the Sanskrit, note how the rhythm carries you. When you read the meaning, let it be slow.
