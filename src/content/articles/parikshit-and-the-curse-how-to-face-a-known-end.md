Parikshit was the grandson of Arjuna and the king who ruled after the Pandavas. His story appears in the Mahabharata and, in greater depth, in the Bhagavata Purana.

## The moment of anger
Hunting in the forest, the king grew tired and thirsty and came upon the sage Shamika, who was sitting in deep meditation. Parikshit asked for water and received no answer, because the sage was absorbed in silence. Offended, he picked up a dead snake with his bow and placed it round the sage's neck, and rode away.

## The curse
Shamika's young son Shringi heard of it and, in anger, cursed that the king would die within seven days of a bite from Takshaka, the king of snakes. The sage himself was dismayed by his son's haste and sent word to the king. Parikshit accepted the news without protest.

## The seven days
Parikshit placed his son Janamejaya on the throne, went to the banks of the Ganga, and sat down to fast. The sage Shuka came and, over seven days, told him the Bhagavata Purana. At the end of the seventh day Takshaka came, and the king left the world at peace.

## What the story teaches
- **A single moment of anger can start a chain.** Parikshit's offence was small, and Shringi's response was larger. Both were products of haste.
- **Karma is not always proportionate.** The tradition does not say the curse was fair. It says the king met it with grace.
- **We all have a known end, even if we do not know the date.** Parikshit's week is a picture of how to spend the time we have: letting go of what does not matter and listening to what does.

## Who Parikshit was
Parikshit was the son of Abhimanyu and Uttara. He was born after the great war, and the story goes that he was nearly lost before birth, when Ashvatthama aimed a terrible weapon at the unborn child and Krishna protected him. The Bhagavata Purana explains his name by saying that, after he was born, he looked at every face around him, searching for the one he had seen in the womb. The word suggests one who examines or tests. That is tradition's explanation, and it is a lovely one.

He took the throne when the Pandavas retired from the world. In the first canto of the Bhagavata Purana, he is shown as a just king who, when he finds the age of Kali trying to settle in his land, does not kill it, but confines it to a few places. He was no tyrant. That is why his one flash of anger with the sage seems so out of character, and why the story matters.

## The question he asked
This is the part many people miss. When the curse came, Parikshit did not rage, bargain or hunt for a way out. He went to the river and sat down. Many sages gathered round him, and the king asked them one plain question: what should a person do who is about to die?

None of them could agree, until Shuka, the young son of Vyasa, arrived. The Bhagavata Purana is presented as his answer. The whole text, with its stories of Vishnu and his devotees, comes from that one honest question asked by a man who knew his time was short.

## The week of listening
Because of this, the tradition of the *Bhagavata Saptaha*, a seven-day recitation of the Bhagavata, is still kept in many towns and villages. Families sponsor it for peace in the home, and neighbours come to listen. The idea is that each of us, in a way, has Parikshit's week. Seven days of attention, set apart, can change how a person lives, even if no curse is hanging over them.

The Mahabharata tells the end a little differently, with Takshaka coming to the king. Later in the epic, his son Janamejaya performs a great sacrifice against the snakes in revenge, which is the story of [Astika and the snake sacrifice](/astika-and-the-snake-sacrifice-how-a-boy-saved-the-nagas/).

## Reading it with modern eyes
Most of us do not get a date. We carry on as if there will be endless time, and put off the things that matter: the call to a parent, the apology, the quiet hour. Parikshit's story takes away that cushion for a moment.

Try asking yourself:
- Whom would I want to speak to this week?
- What am I carrying that I could put down?
- What would I want to hear or read, if I could only choose one thing?

You do not have to change your life. Even one honest answer is a good beginning.

## Questions people ask
**Was the curse fair?**
The tradition does not claim so. Shringi was a young man acting in anger, and even his father regretted it. The story is about how to meet an unfair thing with dignity, not about proving that it was deserved.

**Does the story say we should give up our work when we know the end is near?**
It shows one way, not a rule. Parikshit handed the kingdom to a capable son, and then spent his time on what mattered most to him.

**What if someone I love is facing a serious illness?**
Be with them, listen, and let them lead. This is not medical advice, and medical care should always come first. For a quiet companion text in hard times, you might read [Gita on grief](/gita-on-grief-what-krishna-says-to-someone-who-has-lost-someone/).
