By the late 1920s, many Western writers were telling stories about India's holy men. Most of them were about magic and miracles. Then a British journalist arrived in the south, and the story changed.

## A journalist's search
Paul Brunton, born Raphael Hurst in London in 1898, was a writer on spiritual subjects. In 1930, he travelled around India looking for genuine teachers, and found a lot of what he thought was show. In the end, he was advised to go to Tiruvannamalai.

## Meeting the sage
He found Ramana Maharshi sitting quietly in a hall at the foot of Arunachala, with devotees around him. He was, Brunton wrote, struck by the stillness. He stayed for several days, asked questions and sat in silence. Ramana's answer to his questions was simple: look into the question, *Who am I?*, and find where the sense of I comes from.

Brunton wrote about the experience in *A Search in Secret India* in 1934. The book was a bestseller, and it was the first time that many Western readers heard of Ramana. After it appeared, visitors from Europe and America began to arrive at the ashram. Brunton later wrote on a more personal path, which he called the philosophical path, and moved away from any single teacher.

## Arthur Osborne
Another Englishman, Arthur Osborne, came for a different reason. He had been working as a journalist and teacher in Poland and Bangkok, and in 1945 he came to India. He settled at Tiruvannamalai with his family, and became a devotee of Ramana in the last years of his life. After Ramana's death in 1950, he edited the ashram's journal, *The Mountain Path*, and wrote the first full-length account of the sage's life, *Ramana Maharshi and the Path of Self-Knowledge*, published in 1954. Many readers still find it the best introduction.

## What drew them
Both men, in different words, said the same thing. Ramana taught very little. He answered questions, sometimes with silence. What they remembered was not so much what he said, but the effect of sitting with him. Brunton described it as an atmosphere of peace that changed the way he thought.

## What Ramana taught
His method was called *atma vichara*, self-enquiry. When a thought arises, ask, to whom does it arise? The answer is, to me. Then ask, who am I? Follow it back. The teaching is much the same as in the [Nirvana Shatakam](/nirvana-shatakam-i-am-not-the-mind-meaning/) and [Advaita](/advaita-vedanta-in-plain-words/).

## Visiting
The Sri Ramanasramam at Tiruvannamalai is open to visitors and is a place of quiet. There is an old hall where Ramana sat, a library and a guesthouse. Many people spend a few days there and walk the Girivalam. See [our Arunachala guide](/arunachala-the-hill-that-is-shiva-and-the-walk-around-it/).

## What it shows
These two stories are not about conversion. They are about two men who went in search of something and found a place where the search could stop for a while.
