You wake up on the morning of Varalakshmi Vratam, Ugadi or Deepavali, and you realise that your period has started. Your stomach drops. The house is full of plans, your mother-in-law is already in the kitchen, and the first thing you feel is not cramps. It is guilt.

Let me say the first thing plainly. Nothing is wrong with you, and nobody planned this.

## Why does it keep happening on festival days?
There are two simple reasons.

**The calendar is crowded.** The Hindu calendar has a festival, a vratam or a special tithi on a large number of days. Add Ekadashi, Pournami, Amavasya, Sankashti, Fridays for Lakshmi and Tuesdays for Hanuman, and the chance that some important day lands on the first day of your period is high. It would be stranger if it never did.

**Festivals change your body clock.** Doctors say that stress, poor sleep, travel, fasting and sudden changes in routine can make a period come a few days early or late. Festival weeks have all of those. So the period does not come on a festival because of the festival's timing. The festival has shaken the timing of the period.

## What the traditions say
There is no single answer, and anyone who claims there is has not looked widely.

- In many families, especially in older generations, a menstruating woman does not enter the puja room, cook for rituals or visit a temple for three to five days. In Kerala this is often called *theettu*, and in other places other names.
- The reasoning varies. Some people say it was ritual purity. Many say it was a rest, in a time when women did hard work every day and there were no hygiene products. Others now say it was simply a social rule that carried a stigma.
- Not every tradition follows this. In the Shakta tradition, the goddess herself is honoured as a bleeding goddess. At the Kamakhya temple in Assam, the Ambubachi festival celebrates her annual cycle ([read more](/kamakhya-devi-the-shakti-peetha-of-assam/)). That alone shows that the tradition is not one voice.
- Many modern teachers and many families say that prayer from the heart cannot be blocked by a natural process of the body. Others prefer to keep the rules. Both exist today inside Hindu homes, sometimes in the same family.

## What you can do on the day
There is a lot of space between "ruin the festival" and "fight your family."

1. **Do not carry guilt.** A natural body function is not a sin. This is the most important point.
2. **Ask what your family expects, calmly, early.** If you know your cycle is due near a festival, tell your mother or mother-in-law a few days before, so that the plan is made without panic.
3. **Share the ritual work.** Someone else can do the formal puja while you help with preparations that your family allows, or you can sit nearby.
4. **Pray in your own way.** Many women say a silent prayer, listen to a stotra, read the Gita or chant a mantra in the mind. Most households accept that.
5. **Visit the temple later.** The festival day is crowded anyway, and the temple will be there a few days after.

## What about pills to delay the period?
Some women take hormonal pills to postpone a period. If you are thinking about it, speak to a doctor first. The pills have side effects, they do not suit everyone, and they should not be taken casually, especially for young girls.

## If you are a young girl reading this
A first period can come at any time, and sometimes it is during a festival. If someone makes you feel dirty or ashamed, that is about their view, not about you. Your body is healthy and is doing what it should.

## If you are the elder in the family
The best thing you can do is to make it easy to talk about. A daughter or daughter-in-law who can say "my period is due on Friday" without fear is a gift.

## A last thought
Many teachers say that God looks at the heart. If you go by that, a woman who sits quietly with her eyes closed and thinks of the Lord on a festival morning is doing what she needs to do. And if your family keeps a rule that you do not agree with, you can follow it for peace and still ask the questions gently.
