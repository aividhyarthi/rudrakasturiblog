If you ever sat in a children's music class, you will remember the first lessons: sa ri ga ma pa, up and down, again and again. Do you know whom we have to thank for that patient routine? Tradition gives the credit to a man who was once a banker, and who was, say the stories, a very hard man about money.

## The merchant of Purandaragada

Purandara Dasa (పురందర దాసు; Kannada ಪುರಂದರ ದಾಸ) was born, according to the commonly given dates, around 1484 and lived into the 1560s. His place of birth is itself debated: some say Purandaragada near Pune, others a place in Karnataka. His early name was Srinivasa Nayaka, and he is said to have been a very wealthy dealer in gems and gold, married to a woman called Saraswati Bai.

Tradition adds that he was so careful with money that people called him *navakoti Narayana*, the lord of nine crores. The detail is part of the legend and should be held as such.

## The change

The best-known story of his transformation goes like this. A poor Brahmin came to Srinivasa's shop asking for money for his son's thread ceremony. For days Srinivasa put him off, giving him a coin of little value. The Brahmin then went to the merchant's wife, who gave him her nose-ring. Srinivasa saw it later in his own shop, sold by the Brahmin himself, and he went home to confront his wife. She, in fear, prepared to end her life and found the nose-ring in her cup of water.

Tradition says the Brahmin was the Lord himself in disguise, and that Srinivasa gave away all he owned that day. There are other versions: some say it was a family loss, some say Vyasatirtha's teaching, and some do not tell the story at all. We cannot say with certainty which is true. What the traditions agree on is the result.

## Vyasatirtha and Hampi

Srinivasa became a disciple of Vyasatirtha, the great Madhva saint and royal guru at Vijayanagara, and took the name Purandara Dasa. The *dasa* tradition of Karnataka, with its roots in Madhva devotion, asked its followers to live simply, beg for their food, and sing the Lord's name in plain Kannada.

At Hampi, on the bank of the Tungabhadra, there is a mantapa still named for him. If you want to feel the world he sang in, read about [Hampi and the river of the Vijayanagara kings](/tungabhadra-hampi-and-the-river-of-the-vijayanagara-kings/).

## The songs

His signature is *Purandara Vittala*, the name of the Lord at Pandharpur. For the story of that deity, see [Vitthala of Pandharpur](/vitthala-of-pandharpur-the-god-who-waits-on-a-brick/). His songs, called *padas*, *suladis* and *ugabhogas*, are in everyday Kannada. They speak of money, pride, food, family and the deep fear of death, with an almost joking honesty. Many keep a simple structure: a refrain and a few verses.

Tradition says he composed 475,000 songs. That cannot be true in the sense of preserved texts, and likely refers to a symbolic large number. Today perhaps a thousand or so songs are known under his name, and scholars debate which are really his.

## The first lessons

The thing that makes him special for musicians is the teaching method. He is credited with arranging the graded exercises of Carnatic music: the *sarali varisai* (simple note patterns), *janta varisai* (paired notes), *alankaras* (patterns across the tala cycles), and *geethams* for the beginner. He is also credited with choosing the raga Mayamalavagowla as the one in which a beginner starts.

How much of this is truly his, and how much was later assigned to him, is something historians discuss. But the tradition is old and strong, and every teacher in the South still begins this way.

## Purandara Dasara Aradhane

Each year musicians gather to remember him on the day called Purandara Dasara Aradhane, usually in the month of Pushya (around January). At Hampi, in Bengaluru and in Chennai, students sing his songs in groups. If you have never been, it is a good place to see how a teaching tradition becomes a festival.

## How to listen to him

If you want to meet Purandara Dasa as a listener and not a student, start with a song you already half know. Many of his best-known pieces are short and spoken almost like proverbs: the Lord does not need our wealth; the mind is a monkey; a man's pride is a thin cloth. Notice how often he turns a joke on himself, the rich man now laughing at his old greed. Then listen to the same song in two voices, a child's and an experienced singer's, and see which one moves you more. Most people are surprised.

You can also sit for ten minutes with one verse in Kannada, if you can read it, and ask what he would say about your work week. He did not write for scholars. He wrote for the woman at the well, the farmer, the cook.

## Where this comes from

The earliest sources are the songs themselves and the works of dasa writers who came after him, such as Vijayadasa and Jagannathadasa, along with Madhva hagiographies. The story of the nose-ring appears in later accounts. His dates, his birthplace and the nine-crore story all vary. The credit for the music lessons is a long-standing tradition, supported by how music is taught, but not documented in a single early text.
