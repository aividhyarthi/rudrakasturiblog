You have probably seen the picture many times: Krishna with his flute, and beside him Radha, in a festival procession, on a calendar, on a temple wall. And perhaps you have wondered, quietly, where she comes from. If you open the Mahabharata to find her, she is not there. That is an honest place to begin.

## Where Radha is, and is not

The Mahabharata, the story of Krishna as friend and charioteer, does not name Radha. The Bhagavata Purana, the great text of his childhood in Vrindavan, does not give her name either, as far as the tradition's own scholars note. It does describe the rasa dance, and a moment when Krishna disappears from the circle of gopis, leaving with one of them whom he favours. Many devotees understand this as Radha, and commentators have long read the Bhagavata that way, while others say the text deliberately keeps her unnamed.

She appears clearly in later literature. The Gita Govinda, written by the poet Jayadeva in the twelfth century (so tradition and scholars say), puts Radha at the centre of a Sanskrit poem about longing, separation and reunion. The Brahma Vaivarta Purana, a text of uncertain date, tells her story at length and calls her the beloved of Krishna. After that, poets across India take her up: Vidyapati and Chandidas in the East, Surdas in the North, and many more.

So Radha is real in devotion, even though she is late in the written record. Many sacred ideas in India have lived first in song and worship, and reached the books afterward.

## What the name may mean

The name is often explained as coming from a root meaning to worship or to accomplish. Some explain it as the one who worships Krishna best. This is a pious play on words and not a settled derivation, so take it as a teaching rather than a fact of language.

## Radha as the soul's longing

For those who follow the path of bhakti, Radha is not only a woman in a village story. She is the soul that longs for God. In the poems, Radha waits, grows angry, forgives, searches in the dark. All of that is what a human heart does in the presence and absence of the divine. When you feel that God is near one day and far the next, you have understood something of Radha.

This is why the tradition treats these poems as prayer rather than romance. The language is the language of love because, the teachers say, no other human feeling comes so close to the intensity of longing for God.

## In Gaudiya theology

In the school founded by Chaitanya Mahaprabhu (sixteenth century, Bengal), the teaching goes further. Krishna, the supreme person, has a power called hladini shakti, the power of joy, and Radha is that power in person. She is not separate from him but his own delight, shown as a second form so that love can be given and received. Chaitanya himself, followers say, was Krishna in Radha's mood. Other schools of Vaishnavism, including the Sri Vaishnava tradition of the South, honour the Lord with different emphasis and different companions, and these differences are respected.

## Other voices of the same longing

Radha is not alone. The tradition has many human voices who speak as the beloved of God. In the South, Andal, the girl-saint of Srivilliputtur, sang in the Tiruppavai of rising before dawn with her friends to wake Krishna, and in the Nachiyar Tirumozhi she spoke openly of longing for the Lord. Her words are in Tamil, from the old land of the Alvars, yet the longing in them is the same as in Jayadeva's Sanskrit. The South also loves Nappinnai, the cowherd girl who in Andal's verses is among those Krishna is woken beside, and some families see her as a southern counterpart to Radha. Others keep the two apart, and both views are heard. Meera in Rajasthan sang to Krishna as her husband, leaving a palace for the road. Seen together, Radha, Andal and Meera are three faces of the same cry.

If you are a Tamil or Telugu family, you may know Andal first and Radha second, and that is perfectly in order. Both are valid ways in.

## How to approach Radha in worship

If you keep a Radha Krishna image at home, you need no special rule. Offer flowers, a lamp, a little butter or sugar candy, and sing a bhajan. On Janmashtami many homes include Radha in the decoration; see [how to celebrate Krishna Janmashtami at home](/krishna-janmashtami-how-to-celebrate-at-home/). To meet Krishna in a different mood, read [Krishna the cowherd, the king and the teacher](/krishna-the-cowherd-the-king-and-the-teacher/).

Be wary of retellings that turn the story into something cheap. The tradition's own teachers insist the theme is divine love, not the pastimes of ordinary people, and the great poets handle it with tenderness and restraint.

## Why the late arrival does not weaken her

Some readers feel uneasy that Radha is not in the oldest texts. It helps to remember how devotion grows. A village sings a name for centuries before a poet gives it a Sanskrit verse, and the poet's verse then shapes how the next generation sings. The tradition does not claim that every figure must be in the earliest books. It claims that love for God, once it appears, finds its own voice, and Radha is one of the loveliest it found.

## A question for you

Which voice speaks to you most: the waiting of Radha, the early morning of Andal, or the journey of Meera? There is no wrong answer, and many people find that it changes with the years.
