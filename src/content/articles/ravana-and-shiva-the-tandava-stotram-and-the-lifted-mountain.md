Have you ever met someone who prays sincerely every morning and is still unkind to the people at home? It confuses us. We expect devotion to make a person gentle. The story of Ravana (రావణుడు) and Shiva is the oldest and sharpest version of that confusion.

## The devotee before the villain

Most of us meet Ravana only as the ten-headed king who took Sita. But tradition has another picture of him, a man who sat in tapas for years, offered his heads one by one in some tellings, and won boons from Brahma. Many Shaiva families in the south honour him for his love of Shiva, and in a few places he is remembered with real respect.

That is worth holding beside the usual story. The Ramayana does not make its villain a fool. It makes him a giant.

## The mountain in the Uttara Kanda

The Uttara Kanda, the last book of Valmiki's Ramayana, tells how Ravana was flying in his chariot, Pushpaka, when his path was blocked by Kailasa. Nandi, Shiva's bull, stopped him and warned him that Shiva was at play there with Parvati. Ravana laughed at the warning, and in anger he decided to lift the mountain and move it out of his way.

He put his arms under the mountain and heaved. Kailasa shook. Parvati was startled. Shiva, without effort, pressed down with the tip of his toe, and the mountain settled back onto Ravana's arms. He was trapped, and he cried out so loudly that the worlds trembled. Tradition holds that the name Ravana, the one who roars, comes from this cry.

His ministers told him to praise Shiva. For a long time he did, with hymns and, in some tellings, by making a stringed instrument of his own arm and sinew. Shiva was pleased, released him, and gave him blessings, including a sword in many versions, with a warning to be humble.

## The hymn that carries his name

The Shiva Tandava Stotram is a fast, thundering hymn to Shiva dancing, with long compound words that seem to move like drums. Tradition attributes it to Ravana, and often says he composed it while pinned under Kailasa or after he was freed.

Please hold that carefully. The authorship is traditional. Scholars do not agree on when the hymn was composed, and the Valmiki text does not name it. What we can say is that for centuries the hymn has been loved because it sounds the way the dance of Shiva feels, and that the tradition has joined it to Ravana's name.

## How pride and devotion sit together

This is the question behind the story. Ravana's devotion was real in the telling, and his pride was real too. Neither cancelled the other.

Ask yourself a quiet question. Do I use my devotion to become smaller, or to feel bigger? A prayer can soften a person. It can also become another thing to be proud of. Ravana asked Shiva for power again and again, but there is no moment in the story where he asks to become gentler.

The Bhagavad Gita speaks to this in its own way. It warns that learning, austerity and gifts are of little use when ego is steering them. You may want to read [the Gita's teaching on the three gunas](/gita-three-gunas-why-you-feel-restless-dull-or-calm/) with Ravana in mind.

## Why the story is kept

Rama's war with Ravana is not a war between a god and a godless man. It is a war between dharma and a devotee who would not bend. That is why Rama is shown praying to Shiva at Rameswaram, and you can read more in [Rameswaram Ramanathaswamy and Rama's prayer to Shiva](/rameswaram-ramanathaswamy-temple-where-rama-prayed-to-shiva/). Both sides worship Shiva. Only one side is willing to be corrected.

## If you want to recite the Stotram

Some families recite it on Mondays or on [Maha Shivaratri](/maha-shivaratri-how-to-keep-the-fast-and-the-night-vigil/). A simple way: bathe, light a lamp before an image or lingam, say Om Namah Shivaya a few times, then read the hymn slowly from a printed copy in a language you can pronounce. Do not worry about speed. It is often recited fast, but a slow, correct reading is better than a fast, mistaken one. If a word is difficult, leave it and come back to it later.

## What the pinned giant teaches

Stay with the picture for a moment. A man with ten heads and twenty arms, who has defeated the guardians of the directions, is held down by the pressure of a single toe. Shiva does not strike or curse him. He simply does not move aside. The mountain is not Shiva's weapon; it is Shiva's rest.

Many teachers read this as a lesson about the limit of effort. You can lift a great deal in the world, but there are things you cannot lift by strength alone, and the attempt tells you where you stand. Ravana's pain was the beginning of his best prayer. It is common for people to pray most truthfully when they are pressed down, and the question is whether the prayer outlasts the release. In his case, tradition says it did not.

## Where this comes from

The lifting of Kailasa is in the Uttara Kanda of the Valmiki Ramayana, which many scholars regard as a later addition to the epic, though it is loved all the same. The same event appears in Puranic and Shaiva tellings, with differences in details such as the sword and the hymn. The Tandava Stotram's attribution to Ravana is a tradition passed down in recitation and popular books. Telugu, Tamil and Kannada versions of the Ramayana also differ in how much space they give to this episode.

Where in your own life do you pray with your hands folded and still keep your pride quietly in charge?
