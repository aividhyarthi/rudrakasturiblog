**Source:** later and folk tradition, not Valmiki. The details change from region to region, and we say where we are choosing one version over another.

Imagine a palace on an island, on the night a child is about to be born. A queen is in labour. Outside her room, her husband is not praying. He is checking the sky.

This is the scene that this story begins with. Ravana, king of Lanka, scholar, devotee of Shiva, conqueror of gods, is about to become a father. His wife Mandodari is expecting. And he has decided that the birth of this boy will not be left to chance.

## Who is telling this story, and who is not

Start with an honest note, because a good story deserves one.

You will not find this tale in Valmiki's Ramayana. Valmiki's Ravana is a great conqueror, learned in the Vedas, with ten heads and a boon from Brahma. In the Uttara Kanda we are told how his son Meghanada earned the name *Indrajit*, "conqueror of Indra", and how Brahma gave him boons. But the picture of the nine planets lying in chains is a later one. It is told by village storytellers, in *harikathas*, in temple lore, and in the astrology-minded traditions of the South. Different regions put the details in different places.

We have chosen the most widely told version and noted where others differ. If your grandmother told it another way, please write and tell us. We would like to hear it.

## Ravana, the king who read the sky

Why would a king think of the planets at all? Because tradition says Ravana knew them well. Our piece on [Ravana the scholar](/ravana-the-scholar-what-tradition-says-he-knew/) explains that later tradition gives him learning in the Vedas, music, medicine and astrology. A work called the *Ravana Samhita* is attributed to him, though scholars are careful about who really wrote it.

In the story, this is the point. Ravana did not simply fear fate. He believed it could be arranged.

He had defeated the guardians of the directions. He had made Kubera give up his flying chariot. He had made Yama, the lord of death, retreat. If the gods could be defeated, why not the stars?

## The astrologers' advice

When Mandodari's time came near, Ravana called together his court astrologers. His question was a father's question, asked with a king's ambition: *what would it take for my son to be unbeatable, and never die?*

The astrologers answered in the language of the birth chart.

A chart is a map of the sky at the moment of birth, divided into twelve *bhavas*, or houses. Each house stands for an area of life. The **11th house**, *Labha Bhava*, is the house of gain: wishes fulfilled, friends, income, success. In the old texts the 11th is one of those houses where even the harsher planets are said to do well.

So the advice was simple, and enormous. If the child were born at a moment when all nine planets stood together in the 11th house, he would carry the sky's whole blessing. He would be strong, victorious and long-lived beyond any man.

The nine are the Navagrahas. If you want to meet them properly, our guide to [the nine planets in Vedic astrology](/navagraha-the-nine-planets-in-vedic-astrology/) is the place to start:

- **Surya**, the Sun
- **Chandra**, the Moon
- **Mangala**, Mars
- **Budha**, Mercury
- **Brihaspati (Guru)**, Jupiter
- **Shukra**, Venus
- **Shani**, Saturn
- **Rahu** and **Ketu**, the two lunar nodes

The problem is plain. The planets do not move because a king asks. Each has its own path and its own time. Saturn alone spends about two and a half years in one sign.

So Ravana did the only thing a man of his pride could think of. He went and got them.

## How he took the planets

Here the story becomes a tale of power. Ravana had earned his strength by years of hard tapas. He had pleased Shiva and Brahma, and received boons that no god, demon or spirit could defeat him in the usual way. With that strength, the telling goes, he marched through the heavens and took hold of the nine.

The Sun, the Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, Saturn and the two nodes were seized and brought to Lanka. They were put in chains, or in prison cells, and made to stand in the order Ravana demanded.

Some tellings say they were kept at the foot of his throne. Some say they were made to lie face down on the steps, so that he would walk over them. Some say he used the chains to make his astrologers' chart come true. This detail is the same in all of them: for once, the planets did what a human wanted.

Think about what a strange picture this is. The nine who rule over every human life, who decide the good days and the bad, are bound and quiet in a court on an island. A king has put the sky in a cage.

## The night of the birth

Now the hour comes. The child is about to be born. Ravana's astrologers watch the clock. Every planet is in place, and each one has been forced into the 11th house.

All but one is calm. Shani, Saturn, is in a different mood.

Shani is the planet of time and consequence, of justice and hard lessons. He does not bow easily. And he knew what the plan meant. A child who could not lose and could not die would be a gift to a king who had already done great harm. Shani, the story says, was not willing to be used for that.

Here the tellings go in slightly different directions:

- In the most common one, Shani, who had been held in the 11th house, **shifted into the 12th house** at the very moment of birth. Some say he moved his foot, some say he turned his gaze.
- In another, Ravana could not keep Shani fully in position, and Shani's eyes strayed towards the 12th.
- In a third, Shani simply refused to stand where he was told, and looked away.

The 12th house is the house of endings. It is the house of loss, of far places, of the end of the journey. When Shani looked there, he did something very small and very large. The boy's chart was no longer perfect.

## What Ravana's son became

The child was born, and he cried so loudly that the sound was like thunder. That is why, as tradition says, he was named **Meghanada**: the one whose voice is like the clouds.

He grew up to be one of the great warriors of the Ramayana. Valmiki tells us that he won the name *Indrajit* by defeating Indra, king of the gods, and that Brahma gave him boons. He knew illusion and divine weapons. He fought Rama and Lakshmana so fiercely that for a time the brothers lay wounded on the battlefield.

You can read about him, and about his wife, in our page on [Indrajit and Sulochana](/indrajit-and-sulochana-the-warrior-and-his-wife/).

But he was not immortal. In the end he was killed by Lakshmana at the sacrifice he was performing at Nikumbhila. The planet that had looked toward the 12th house, the story says, had made sure that the perfect chart did not come to be.

So in this tradition Indrajit is a man almost made into a god, and kept human by a single glance.

## What Ravana did to Shani

Ravana's reaction is told with the same colour as the rest. He was furious. His son was great, but he was not what he should have been. And the reason was plain.

In the common telling, Ravana attacked Shani. Some versions say he struck him with his mace on the leg. This is the reason given in some places for the idea that Shani walks slowly or has a lame foot. Others say he had Shani thrown down, or kept him in chains longer than the rest.

A side note. The word *Shanaishchara* really does mean "the one who moves slowly". Saturn takes nearly thirty years to go around the sky, the slowest of the visible planets. So the story of the injured leg looks like a story built to explain a name. That is how a good deal of folk tradition works. People notice something true, and a story grows up around it.

## How the planets were freed

For years, the telling goes, the nine stayed in prison. Then came Hanuman.

When Hanuman crossed the ocean to find Sita, he searched the whole of Lanka. In the popular versions, he found the Navagrahas in their cells, broke the doors open and let them go.

Shani, grateful and humbled, made a promise. Those who pray to Hanuman, he said, will not be troubled by his heavy gaze. That is why so many families go to a Hanuman temple on Saturday evening. We tell this part in more detail in [Hanuman and Shani](/hanuman-and-shani-why-hanuman-devotees-are-said-to-be-safe/) and in our guide to [Saturday worship](/saturday-venkateswara-shani-hanuman-how-to-worship/).

You can see how the stories join. One tale explains why Indrajit was strong but mortal. A second explains why Shani is slow. A third explains why Saturday belongs to Hanuman. They are three different threads, woven into one cloth.

## What the story is really saying

Whether you take it as history or as poetry, the tale has a clear idea in it, and it is worth saying slowly.

**1. You cannot cage time.** Ravana could defeat armies, kingdoms and gods. He could not make the sky obey. The story says that even the one who seemed to control everything could not arrange his own child's fate.

**2. Shani is the planet of justice.** In every version, Shani is the one who stands up. He is not the cruel planet in this story. He is the one who refuses to help power escape consequence. When people say Shani is stern but fair, this is the picture they have in mind.

**3. One small act can change everything.** The planet did not fight. He moved a foot, or turned his eyes. The plan fell apart. We often think that only big actions change big outcomes. The story says that sometimes it is the quiet refusal.

**4. Ambition without restraint ends in loss.** Ravana had knowledge, strength and boons, and used them to try to remove death from his family's future. Our page on [Mandodari](/mandodari-the-queen-who-warned-ravana-and-mourned-him/) shows what this meant for the person nearest him: a queen who warned him and was not heard.

## A word for those who worry about Shani

If you are reading this because you are in Sade Sati, or someone told you Shani is against you, take a breath. The same story that shows Shani as a hard planet also shows him as the one who kept a tyrant from getting his way.

Planets are not villains in this telling. If you want the practical side, our [Sade Sati guide](/sade-sati-2026-which-rashis-are-affected/) explains who is affected now and what the traditions suggest. And be careful of anyone who sells fear or an expensive "remedy". The old practices cost almost nothing: a lamp, a few verses, a kind act, honest work.

## Where to see the Navagrahas

If the story makes you want to see the nine in stone, the **Navagraha temples near Kumbakonam** in Tamil Nadu are the classic circuit. We have listed them in order in [Navagraha temples near Kumbakonam](/navagraha-temples-near-kumbakonam-the-nine-in-order/).

For Shani in particular, **Thirunallar**, near Karaikal, has the best-known shrine. **Mandapalli** in the Godavari region of Andhra Pradesh is another much-visited Shani temple.

## What we do not know

We have said this before, and it matters here.

We do not know who first told this story. We do not know whether it is old or comes from a later age. We do not know if there is one written source or many. The version with the mace and the leg is common in some places and unknown in others. The "11th house" detail is told in the astrology-minded tellings and is not always mentioned in the others.

What we can say is that it has been told for a long time, by many people, and that it has stayed because it says something people feel to be true.

## Questions people ask

**Was Indrajit's birth really timed by the stars?**
Valmiki does not say so. In his text Indrajit is a great warrior with boons from Brahma. The birth-chart story is a later addition.

**Why 11th house and not the 1st?**
The 11th is the house of gains and the fulfilment of wishes. In Vedic astrology it is one of the houses where harsh planets are said to give good results. That is why, in the story, it was the place a father would choose.

**Does this mean Shani is a bad planet?**
No. In most readings of the story he is the one who refuses to be bent to a tyrant's will. Most thoughtful teachers describe Shani as strict but fair.

**Is it all right to believe the story and also not believe in astrology?**
Of course. You can enjoy it as a story about pride and consequence, and keep your decisions in your own hands. That is what most careful readers do.

## How to read a story like this

Folk stories are not footnotes to the epics. They are the way ordinary people carried the epics home and made them useful. This one gives you a Ravana who is brilliant and wrong, a Shani who is slow and just, and a Hanuman who opens locked doors. It tells you that no one is large enough to own the sky, and that patience, in the end, outlasts power.

Hold the story lightly. Let it make you think. And the next time you pass a small shrine to the nine planets on a temple wall, look at them again, standing quietly in their row, free.
