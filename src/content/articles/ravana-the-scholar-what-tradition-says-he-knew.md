*వాల్మీకి రామాయణం రావణుని వేదాలు తెలిసిన పండితునిగా చూపుతుంది, సంప్రదాయం సంగీతం, వైద్యం, జ్యోతిషం గ్రంథాలను కూడా ఆయన పేరున చెబుతుంది. అయితే జ్ఞానం ఉన్నా నడవడి సరిగా లేకపోతే ఏం జరుగుతుందో ఈ కథ చూపిస్తుంది.*

You probably know someone very clever who made a very poor decision. The cleverness did not help. It may even have made the poor decision sound reasonable. That is a good way to start thinking about Ravana (రావణుడు) the scholar.

## What Valmiki actually says

The oldest description we have is in Valmiki's Ramayana. In the Sundara Kanda, Hanuman enters Lanka at night and sees Ravana asleep. He is struck by the king's splendour, and the text lets Hanuman say something that surprises many readers: that Ravana has such qualities that, if he were not led by adharma, he could have been a protector even of the gods. Elsewhere Ravana is spoken of as learned in the Vedas and the other sacred branches of knowledge, and the city of Lanka is shown with the sound of recitation in some houses.

So this is not a later softening of a villain. Valmiki himself insists that this was a great man gone wrong, because that is what makes the lesson hard.

## The music

Tradition is clear that Ravana loved music. The story in which he pleased Shiva by making an instrument of his own arm and singing, belongs to this memory. Many musicians and families keep the tradition that he was a master of the veena (వీణ), and a simple bowed instrument, the ravanahatha, still played in parts of western India, is linked to his name in folk memory.

Please hold these as tradition. Whether the instrument really goes back to a king of Lanka, nobody can show. What is true is that Ravana is one of the few villains in Indian literature who is remembered kindly by musicians.

## Works attributed to him

Over the centuries several texts have been placed under Ravana's name. The best known is the Ravana Samhita, a work on astrology and related subjects, also said to include medicine. Other titles on medicine, magic and statecraft are also attributed to him in manuscripts and popular books.

Here I must be honest: scholars generally treat these attributions with caution. Authors often placed a famous name on a work to lend it authority, and the texts we have are much later than the age of the Ramayana. Tradition holds that he knew these subjects. The books we hold today may or may not go back to him. If you see a book sold as the words of Ravana, ask where its manuscript tradition comes from before you rely on it for anything, and do not use any such book in place of a doctor or a qualified teacher.

## The gap between learning and character

Now the real question. If he knew so much, why did he do what he did?

The Ramayana does not answer by saying he was ignorant. It says he knew and did not act on it. His brother Vibhishana, his wife Mandodari and his brother Kumbhakarna all told him the truth. You can read how each of them did so in [Vibhishana, who chose dharma over his brother](/vibhishana-the-brother-who-chose-dharma-over-his-brother/) and [Mandodari, who warned Ravana and mourned him](/mandodari-the-queen-who-warned-ravana-and-mourned-him/). He listened as a scholar listens, with interest, and then did as he wished.

This is what Adi Shankara returns to in so many verses: knowledge by itself, if it never reaches how you live, is only information.

## Valmiki versus later tellings

Later tellings split in two directions. Some make Ravana even more of a monster, a creature of pure cruelty. Others, especially in some regional and modern retellings, make him a tragic scholar or a hero of his people. Valmiki stands between them. His Ravana is great and clearly in the wrong.

If you read a version that says Ravana was entirely evil, or entirely noble, it is worth asking which text it is drawing from and why it needed him to be one thing.

## How to study this yourself

You can read the Sundara Kanda description of Ravana in a good translation, and note down three lines about his qualities and three about his actions. Put them side by side. Do this slowly over an evening. You will find the gap in his life drawn very plainly, and it is a kind of mirror.

## A note on reading villains

There is a habit worth correcting in ourselves. When we dislike a figure, we tend to strip away everything admirable about him, so that the story becomes easy. But the Ramayana does not take that shortcut. Its great enemies, Ravana, Kumbhakarna and Indrajit, are all given real strength and some real feeling.

Why would the poet do this? One answer is that a victory over a weak enemy teaches nothing. A victory over a learned, powerful and devout enemy teaches that none of those things can stand in for dharma. If Ravana had been ignorant, we could comfort ourselves that we are not like him. Because he was learned, we have to ask more carefully about ourselves.

## Where this comes from

Hanuman's view of Ravana is in the Sundara Kanda of Valmiki. The scholarly and musical reputation is carried by Valmiki and by Puranic and regional tradition. The Ravana Samhita and similar works are attributions with uncertain authorship. Kamba Ramayanam, the Telugu Ranganatha Ramayanam and other regional versions each add or remove details, and I have not tried to settle those here.
