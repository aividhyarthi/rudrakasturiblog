You are on your way to an important meeting when the phone rings with bad news. Or a message arrives with praise you did not expect. In either case, something in you lurches. A few seconds later you are in a different mood from the one you started in, and you may not even remember deciding to be.

The second chapter of the Gita has a verse about exactly this.

## The question behind it
Arjuna, in the second chapter, has just been given the first teachings of Krishna. He asks a very human question (2.54): *what is the person of steady wisdom like? How does he speak, sit, walk?* He wants to be able to recognise such a person, and presumably become one. The rest of the chapter is Krishna's answer, and the first part of it is this verse.

## The line
> **duḥkheṣv anudvigna-manāḥ sukheṣu vigata-spṛhaḥ**
> **vīta-rāga-bhaya-krodhaḥ sthita-dhīr munir ucyate**
> (Bhagavad Gita 2.56)

## Word by word
| Sanskrit | Meaning |
|---|---|
| duḥkheṣu | in sorrows, in pain |
| anudvigna-manāḥ | whose mind is not agitated |
| sukheṣu | in pleasures |
| vigata-spṛhaḥ | free of craving, not hungry |
| vīta | gone, free of |
| rāga | attachment, clinging |
| bhaya | fear |
| krodhaḥ | anger |
| sthita-dhīḥ | of steady mind |
| muniḥ | a sage, a thinker |
| ucyate | is called |

## In plain words
*The one whose mind is not shaken by suffering, who does not crave pleasure, who is free of attachment, fear and anger: that person is called a sage of steady mind.*

## What it asks, and what it does not
It is easy to read this as asking us to feel nothing. That is not what the words say. Look at the first phrase: *anudvigna-manāḥ*, "a mind that is not agitated". The verse does not say the sorrow is absent. It says that in sorrow the mind is not thrown about. And for pleasure it says not that the person never enjoys, but that he is *vigata-spṛhaḥ*, free from craving: he enjoys what comes, and does not chase it.

Notice the list of three: attachment, fear and anger. They are the three that keep us unsteady. We cling to what we have, we fear to lose it, and we are angry when it is threatened. They are, as it were, one thing in three forms.

The Gita itself shows this person. Krishna is moved by Arjuna's grief. He is not cold. But he is not swept away by it either.

## Where it applies
- **At work,** when a criticism lands or a promotion does not come.
- **At home,** when someone says something unkind.
- **In illness,** when the news is worse than you hoped.
- **In success,** when praise makes you forget yourself.

The verse is not asking for a different life. It asks for a different footing in the same life.

## A practice for the next time
Next time you feel a strong mood coming, whether joy or anger, try three small steps.
1. **Name it.** Say to yourself, quietly, *this is anger* or *this is excitement*. Naming does something: it makes you a witness.
2. **Breathe out slowly,** three times.
3. **Wait** before you reply or decide. Even thirty seconds.

That is the beginning of steadiness. It will not make you a sage in a week, but over a year it changes how you live.

## What comes next in the chapter
Verse 2.57 adds: *he who is not attached in any direction, who neither rejoices nor hates when he gets what is good or bad, his wisdom is fixed.* And verse 2.58 says he can draw in his senses like a tortoise draws in its limbs. The tortoise is a fine image: not an absence of limbs, but the ability to withdraw them when needed.

## A word of care
If you find yourself unable to feel, if numbness, not steadiness, is what you have, please do not mistake that for the Gita's ideal. It may be tiredness or low mood, and it is worth speaking to a doctor or counsellor. In India, Tele-MANAS (14416) is free, day and night.

## A thought
The quiet word in the verse is *muni*, "one who thinks". The person of steady mind is not an ascetic on a hill. He is a *thinker*, who has learned to notice. That is available to anyone.
