*How to use this page:* read the Sanskrit slowly, even if only in the English letters. Then read the word-by-word, then the meaning. Chanting and listening carry the rhythm of a verse, and that has its own power. Reading the words carries the meaning, and you can stop, go back and ask what each word is doing. Do both, but do not skip the reading.

## The line
> **patraṁ puṣpaṁ phalaṁ toyaṁ yo me bhaktyā prayacchati**
> **tad ahaṁ bhakty-upahṛtam aśnāmi prayatātmanaḥ**
> (Bhagavad Gita 9.26)

| phalam | a fruit |
| toyam | water |

| me | to me |
| bhaktyā | with devotion |
| prayacchati | offers |
| tat | that |

| bhakti-upahṛtam | brought with devotion |
| aśnāmi | I accept, I eat |
| prayata-ātmanaḥ | of the one whose heart is pure and offered |

## In plain words
*Whoever offers me a leaf, a flower, a fruit or water with devotion, I accept that offering of the pure-hearted one.*

## The surprise in the verse
Think of what Krishna could have listed. Gold. Elephants. A great sacrifice. He lists the four cheapest things in an Indian village: something green, something that blooms, something that grows, and water from the well. Almost anyone can offer these.

The key word is *bhaktyā*, "with devotion". It appears twice, once as *bhaktyā* and once in *bhakty-upahṛtam*, "brought with devotion". Krishna is repeating it so that we do not miss it. Take that out of the verse, and nothing is left. Put it back, and a leaf is enough.

## What it changed
For many Indians this verse is the answer to a quiet worry: *I am poor, I do not know the Sanskrit, I cannot afford the grand puja. Does it count?* The Gita answers, plainly: yes.

It is why the tulasi leaf on a plate, a single flower from the garden, or a glass of water in front of the picture is a complete act of worship in a village home.

## Common misreading
It does not say that nothing else matters, or that one should offer a leaf instead of acting rightly. In the very next verse (9.27) Krishna says: *whatever you do, whatever you eat, whatever you offer, whatever you give, whatever austerity you perform, do it as an offering to me.* The leaf is the beginning, not the whole.

## One thing to try
Tomorrow morning, offer one thing: a flower, a leaf, a sip of water. Before you do, say aloud in your own language what you are offering, and why. See how it feels to give something small with your whole attention.

## A closer look at the word "prayatātmanaḥ"
The last word of the verse is easy to pass over, but it matters. It is usually translated "of the pure-hearted" or "of one whose self is offered". The idea is not that you must be a saint before you can worship. It points to the way you come: without pretending, without bargaining, with your attention actually present.

A flower thrown at a picture while your mind is elsewhere and a flower placed slowly while you say a few honest words are very different acts, though the flower is the same. The verse asks about the second kind.

## A story that fits the verse
The Bhagavata Purana tells of Sudama, a poor Brahmin and boyhood friend of Krishna, who visits him in Dvaraka carrying only a small bundle of beaten rice, which he is almost too embarrassed to give. In the telling, Krishna takes it with delight and eats it. This comes from the Purana, not the Gita, but many people read the two together: the verse states the principle, the story shows it.

## Questions people ask
**Do I need to say a mantra when I offer something?**
No. The verse names no mantra. If you know one, use it. If you do not, a few words in your own language, such as "please accept this", are enough.

**Can I offer food and water?**
Yes. Many homes offer a little of the day's cooked food before eating. Simple, clean food made with care is what the tradition asks for.

**What if I forget, or I am travelling?**
Then offer in the mind. Picture the flower or the water and offer it. Many teachers say this is a real offering.

**Does the offering have to be fresh?**
Tradition prefers clean, fresh things, and flowers picked from the plant rather than taken from the ground. If you cannot manage that, do what you can with a clean heart.

## Taking it into the day
The verse works well as a habit of mind. Before you cook, before you begin work, before you speak to someone difficult, you can quietly think: *I offer this.* Nothing outside changes, but you may find you do the task with more care.
