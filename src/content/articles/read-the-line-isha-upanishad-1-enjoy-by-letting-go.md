*How to use this page:* read the Sanskrit slowly, even if only in the English letters. Then read the word-by-word, then the meaning. Chanting and listening carry the rhythm of a verse, and that has its own power. Reading the words carries the meaning, and you can stop, go back and ask what each word is doing. Do both, but do not skip the reading.

## The line
> **īśāvāsyam idaṁ sarvaṁ yat kiñca jagatyāṁ jagat**
> **tena tyaktena bhuñjīthā mā gṛdhaḥ kasya svid dhanam**
> (Isha Upanishad 1)

## Word by word
| Sanskrit | Meaning |

| īśā | by the Lord |
| āvāsyam | to be covered, pervaded |
| idam | this |
| sarvam | all |
| yat kiñca | whatever |
| jagatyām | in the world |
| jagat | moving, changing world |
| tena | by that |
| tyaktena | by renunciation, by what is given up |
| bhuñjīthāḥ | may you enjoy, protect |
| mā gṛdhaḥ | do not covet |
| kasya svit | of anyone |
| dhanam | wealth |

## In plain words
*All this, whatever moves in this moving world, is to be covered by the Lord. Enjoy it by letting go. Do not covet anyone's wealth.*

## Two halves
**First half: the world belongs to God.** *īśāvāsyam* has been read in two ways. One: the world is *covered* by the Lord, as a wall is covered by plaster, so that behind everything there is something sacred. Another: the world is *to be covered* by the Lord in our minds, that is, we should see it as God's. Either way, the world is not a mere object to be used.

**Second half: so, enjoy by renouncing.** *Tena tyaktena bhuñjīthāḥ*. This is the surprising phrase. Enjoy, but by letting go. The teachers explain it in several ways. Do not cling to the thing. Take what you need and leave the rest. Treat what you have as given to you.

*Mā gṛdhaḥ*, "do not covet", closes the verse. The word *gṛdhra* means a vulture. Do not be a vulture over another man's wealth.

## A word that cuts two ways
The Upanishad does not say *renounce the world*, nor *grab the world*. It asks for a third thing: *use the world without owning it.* That is a very practical teaching. It means that you can have a house and not be made small by losing it, earn money and not be ruled by it.

## An echo in the epics
The sage Mudgala, in the Mahabharata, was offered heaven and said no. He lived on gleanings and gave away what he had. He is the Isha Upanishad as a life.

## Common misreading
- **"Give everything up."** The verse says *enjoy*. It is not an argument for poverty, but for non-clinging.
- **"So wealth does not matter."** It matters, but it does not belong to you the way you think it does.

## One thing to try
Choose one possession or privilege that you tend to grip: a title, a gadget, a seat at the table. Hold it for a day as if it were on loan. Notice how that changes the way you feel when it is not there.

## Where this verse sits
The Isha Upanishad is short. It has eighteen verses and forms the last chapter, the fortieth, of the Vajasaneyi Samhita of the Shukla Yajurveda. Most Upanishads sit in a section of explanation attached to the Vedas. This one is part of the Samhita itself, which is one reason it is treated with such respect.

It begins with the peace invocation many people know: *pūrṇam adaḥ pūrṇam idam*, "that is full, this is full." Then, straight away, comes the verse we are reading. It is as if the teacher says: here is the whole lesson, and the rest is explanation.

## The very next verse
The second verse is a useful companion. It says that a person should go on doing their work and wish to live a hundred years, and that this way, and no other, the action does not cling to you.

So the Upanishad does not ask you to leave your job, your family or your duties. It asks you to carry them lightly. Verse 1 tells you how to think about what you have. Verse 2 tells you how to act.

## Questions people ask
**Is "tyaktena" about giving things away?**
Teachers take it in more than one way: giving up the sense of "mine", or enjoying what has been given to you. Either way, the emphasis is on the inner grip, not only on the outer object.

**Can a householder follow this?**
Yes, and the verse seems written for exactly that person. A hermit with nothing has little to cling to. A householder has to learn it while the things are still in the room.

**Does "do not covet" only mean other people's money?**
The word is about wealth, but you can stretch it. Many of us envy another person's praise, health or peace of mind. The same lesson applies.

## A small daily practice
At the end of the day, think of three things you used or enjoyed: a meal, a conversation, a warm bed. Say quietly, *this was given.* You are not trying to feel guilty or to feel holy. You are only practising the first half of the verse, that things come to us, and that we do not make them.

Over time many people find they enjoy things more, not less, because they are no longer afraid of losing them.
