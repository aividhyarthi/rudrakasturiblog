*How to use this page:* read the Sanskrit slowly, even if only in the English letters. Then read the word-by-word, then the meaning. Chanting and listening carry the rhythm of a verse, and that has its own power. Reading the words carries the meaning, and you can stop, go back and ask what each word is doing. Do both, but do not skip the reading.

## The lines
> **uttiṣṭhata jāgrata prāpya varān nibodhata**
> **kṣurasya dhārā niśitā duratyayā durgaṁ pathas tat kavayo vadanti**
> (Katha Upanishad 1.3.14)

## In plain words
*Arise, awake. Having reached the excellent teachers, understand. The wise say that the path is as hard to cross as the sharp edge of a razor.*

## Who is speaking
This verse comes at the end of a conversation. A boy named Nachiketa has gone to the house of Yama, the god of death, and waited three days at the door. Yama, ashamed to have kept a guest waiting, offers him three boons. For the third, Nachiketa asks what happens after death. Yama tries to talk him out of it, offering wealth, long life, pleasures. The boy refuses. Finally, Yama teaches him.

This line is Yama's call to everyone who is listening, at the close of a chapter.

## What the verse really says
You may know the Swami Vivekananda version: *Arise, awake, and stop not till the goal is reached.* It is a fine, rousing line, and he made it famous. But it is a paraphrase. The original is different in an important way:

- **Uttiṣṭhata jāgrata**: arise, awake. This is the call.
- **Prāpya varān nibodhata**: having approached the best, understand. The word *varān* is read by teachers as *excellent teachers* or as *boons*. Either way, it speaks of finding a guide and understanding what they say.

So the verse is not only "keep going". It is "wake up, find a real teacher, and *understand*". Effort alone is not enough. You also have to listen.

## The razor's edge
The second line is often dropped when the verse is quoted. It says the path is like a razor's edge, sharp and hard to cross. It is honest. The path to understanding the Self is not a comfortable slope. It demands care and attention, and a guide who has walked it.

W. Somerset Maugham took *The Razor's Edge* as the title of a novel from this verse.

## Common misreading
- **"It is a motivational slogan."** In its setting it is an invitation to spiritual awakening, not to working harder.
- **"Any teacher will do."** *Varān* implies the best. See our piece on how to find a real guru.

Ask yourself: *what am I half-asleep about?* A habit, a relationship, a question I keep putting off. Then ask: *who could I learn from?* Write down one name, and contact them this week.
