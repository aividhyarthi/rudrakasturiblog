If you have ever held an Indian coin, a banknote or a government paper, you have seen three words in Devanagari under the lions: *Satyameva Jayate*. We say them so often that we have stopped hearing them.

They are the first words of a longer verse, and the rest is worth reading.

## The verse
> **satyam eva jayate nānṛtaṁ satyena panthā vitato deva-yānaḥ**
> **yenākramanty ṛṣayo hy āpta-kāmā yatra tat satyasya paramaṁ nidhānam**
> (Mundaka Upanishad 3.1.6)

## Word by word
| Sanskrit | Meaning |
|---|---|
| satyam | truth |
| eva | alone, only |
| jayate | wins, triumphs |
| na | not |
| anṛtam | falsehood (that which is not rta, not in order) |
| satyena | by truth |
| panthāḥ | path |
| vitataḥ | laid out, spread wide |
| deva-yānaḥ | the way of the gods |
| yena | by which |
| ākramanti | they ascend, they walk |
| ṛṣayaḥ | the sages |
| āpta-kāmāḥ | those whose desires are fulfilled |
| yatra | where |
| tat | that |
| satyasya | of truth |
| paramam | supreme |
| nidhānam | treasure, resting place |

## In plain words
*Truth alone wins, not falsehood. By truth the path to the gods is laid out, the path along which the sages, whose desires are fulfilled, climb to where the supreme treasure of truth lies.*

## What is the verse actually saying?
It is not, in its setting, a statement about courtrooms or elections. The Mundaka Upanishad is about knowing the Self. In this verse it says that the road to the highest is a road of truth, and that the people on it are those who have no unmet longing.

A few things stand out.
- **"Not falsehood."** The word *anrita* does not simply mean a lie. It means what is out of step with *rita*, the order of things. A lie is one form of it; so is living in a way that denies what is real.
- **"The path is laid out."** The verb is *vitata*, spread wide. The path is not a narrow secret. It is open.
- **"Whose desires are fulfilled."** The sages on the path are *apta-kama*, which can mean those who have attained what they want, or those who have *no remaining want*. It is a person who is whole.
- **"The supreme treasure of truth."** Truth is not only a rule. It is a destination, a place where you rest.

## How it became the motto
In the early twentieth century, Madan Mohan Malaviya, who founded Banaras Hindu University, urged the use of *Satyameva Jayate* as a national motto. When the Emblem of India was adopted in 1950, the words were placed under the lion capital of Ashoka. It is one of the few places where a verse of the Upanishads appears on the money of a modern state.

## Why it matters that we read the rest
When the verse is shortened to three words, it can sound like a promise: *the truth will always win.* We know that is not always true in the short run. People lie and prosper, and the honest are cheated. The full verse is more careful. It does not say truth wins every time. It says that truth is the *path*, the one that leads where a human life is meant to go. And it says that the people on it are free.

That is a different promise, and a more believable one.

## One way to use it
Pick one small area where you tell yourself a little fib: how much you have really done, or what you really think about a person, or how you really feel about a decision. For one week, say the plain thing, kindly. Notice what happens to your mind. Many people find that the first benefit of truth is that you stop carrying two stories.

## The Upanishad around the verse
The verse sits in the third Mundaka, among a famous passage about two birds on a tree, one who eats and one who watches. Just before it, the Upanishad says that the Self cannot be reached by the weak, nor by one who is careless, nor by austerities without insight. Just after, it says that the Self is revealed to those who are quiet and pure. The verse on truth, then, is part of a picture: truth is one of the supports of the path, along with effort, attention and a clear mind.

You can also read it as practical advice. A person who lies has to remember each lie. The one who tells the truth can walk with an empty hand and a free mind.

## A thought
The old teachers say that truthfulness is the root of all virtue. The Taittiriya Upanishad opens its advice to students with it: *satyam vada*, speak the truth ([Read the line: Taittiriya Upanishad](/read-the-line-taittiriya-upanishad-speak-the-truth-do-your-duty/)). The Mundaka takes it one step further and makes it a road.
