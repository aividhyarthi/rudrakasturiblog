**Source:** Puranic story of Renuka and Jamadagni (Mahabharata, Vana Parva, and Puranas), and regional temple and folk traditions. The links between the goddesses vary by region.

Some goddesses have temples. Others are in every village. Renuka, whom the South knows by names such as Yellamma and Mariamman, is both.

## A woman of great power
In the story, Renuka was the wife of the sage Jamadagni, and the mother of five sons, the youngest of whom was Parashurama. She was said to be so pure in thought that she could carry water in a pot made of unbaked clay, held together by the power of her chastity.

One day, at the river, she saw a gandharva king, Chitraratha, playing in the water with his queens. For a moment, her mind wandered. The pot melted in her hands.

## The terrible order
Jamadagni sensed what had happened and was furious. He ordered his sons, one after another, to kill their mother. The four elder sons refused, and were cursed. Parashurama obeyed, and cut off her head.

The sage, pleased with his obedient son, offered him a boon. Parashurama asked that his mother be restored to life, and his brothers too. Jamadagni agreed, and told him to join the head to the body.

## The confused head
Here the story branches. In many South Indian versions, Parashurama, in his haste, joined his mother's head to the body of a woman of a lower caste who had been killed nearby. The result was a goddess who was both a brahmin's wife and an outcaste woman, and who, in the folk reading, was worshipped by all communities. The pot and the tradition of carrying a pot on the head in many festivals are linked to this.

## Many names
- **Yellamma** is the form in Karnataka, Maharashtra and parts of Andhra Pradesh. The great temple is at Saundatti, in Belagavi district.
- **Mariamman** is the Tamil goddess of rain, cooling and healing, believed to protect from fever, measles and pox. Samayapuram, near Tiruchirappalli, is her most famous seat. Worshippers offer neem leaves, porridge made of ragi, and fire pots.
- **Renukamba, Ellamma, Mahur Renuka** and others in Maharashtra and Andhra are all variations on the story.

In many traditions, she is a mother of the village, who must be fed, honoured and kept cool in the hot months, since a displeased goddess may send heat, fevers and disease.

## A caution
The devadasi system, in which girls were dedicated to Yellamma and often exploited, is illegal in India and has caused great harm. The goddess's worship does not require it, and families who still do it should be helped to stop. Most devotees pray to Yellamma as a protector and healer, and offer simple food and neem.

## What the story says
- **Even the most devout can be human.** The pot melted for a moment's thought, and the tradition does not condemn her.
- **Obedience has a price.** The story is hard on both father and son, and many retellings are critical of Jamadagni.
- **The goddess belongs to everyone.** The mixed body is read as a sign that she is for all castes.
- **Healing and rain are of the same mother.** The village needs her for both.

## Visiting
Samayapuram is a major pilgrimage and best visited on weekdays. Saundatti holds large fairs on full-moon days. Follow the local customs about offerings, and avoid any practice that harms people.
