At a Shiva temple, on a Monday or during Karthika, you may have stood as priests poured water and milk over the lingam while a deep chanting rose around you. It is steady, hypnotic, full of the word *namo*. If you asked what it was, the answer is likely: *Rudram*.

What does it say? Let us look.

## Where it comes from
The Sri Rudram is in the *Taittiriya Samhita* of the Krishna Yajurveda (the fourth book, fifth and seventh chapters). It has two parts.
- **The Namakam** (4.5), eleven sections, each full of *namah*, salutations.
- **The Chamakam** (4.7), eleven sections, each full of *cha me*, "and for me", a long list of requests.

Together they are chanted for the Rudrabhishekam, the ritual bath of Shiva's lingam.

## The Namakam: a list of salutations
It opens with *namaste rudra manyava utota ishave namah*: "Salutations to your anger, O Rudra, and to your arrow." It starts with the fearsome Rudra, who is feared. Then something changes. The hymn turns the same Rudra into *Shiva*, the auspicious: *ya te rudra shiva tanuh*, "that form of yours, O Rudra, which is peaceful and kind."

After that comes the long list, and here is where it becomes remarkable. Rudra is saluted as:
- the lord of the forest and the one in the mountains,
- the physician, the one who heals,
- the one who is in the green leaf and in the dewdrop,
- the carpenter, the chariot-maker, the potter, the blacksmith,
- the cook and the hunter,
- *and* the thieves and robbers, the one at the crossroads.

It is a hymn that refuses to leave anyone out. Every craft, every place and even the dangerous are shown to be Shiva. It is a vision in which the divine is the whole of life, not just the temple.

## The Panchakshari inside
Near the middle of the Namakam appears *namah shivaya cha shivataraya cha*, "salutations to the auspicious and to the more auspicious." This is the source of the five-syllable mantra *Om Namah Shivaya*, which millions say every day. When you say it, you are using a line from the Rudram.

## The Chamakam: what we ask
The Chamakam is the other half. Each section lists things to ask for: food, strength, health, a long life, cattle, learning, cooperation, a good mind and, remarkably, the things we usually leave out, like a good teacher, kind neighbours and freedom from fear. *Annam ca me*, "and food for me", *ayushcha me*, "and long life for me." It is a prayer that asks for everything at once, and yet, at the very end, it is asking for the ability to receive it well.

## How it is chanted
Traditionally the Rudram is learned by ear from a teacher over years, in a specific tune, and chanted with the *Laghunyasa* and the *Maha Nyasa* before it. It is not casual. If you want to try it, find a teacher or a recording by an authentic chanter, and follow the words in a book. It is perfectly acceptable to listen and read along.

## What happens at the abhishekam
At a Rudrabhishekam, the priests pour water, milk, curd, honey, ghee and other substances over the lingam, one after another, between rounds of the Rudram. The idea is that Shiva is cooled and honoured. At the end, the lingam is dried, anointed and decorated. Many families sponsor a Rudrabhishekam on a Monday, in Karthika, or on a family occasion.

## What it can teach on an ordinary Monday
The hymn says to us: *the one you are looking for is not far away.* The next time you stand at a bus stop or a cook's counter or the office, remember that the Namakam would salute even there. It is a very large view of God.

## From storm god to Shiva
In the oldest Vedic hymns, Rudra is a frightening deity of storms, the lord of wild places, a god you pray to in order to be spared. The Rig Veda has only a few hymns to him. Over time the tradition began to call him by a gentler name, Shiva, "the auspicious one", and the Rudram is the great bridge between the two. It begins by saluting his anger and arrow, and ends by asking for his mercy and care. In that single hymn you can see how a god changed in the minds of the people.

## A listening practice
If you want to begin, try this. Find a recording of the Namakam by a reliable chanter and listen for ten minutes, once a week, on Monday evening. Do not worry about understanding. Just let the rhythm settle you. After a few weeks, start following the English meaning in a book. The tradition itself says that hearing is the first step in learning.
