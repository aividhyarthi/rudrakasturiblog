*రుక్మి రుక్మిణి అన్న, కృష్ణునికి బావమరిది. కురుక్షేత్రానికి సైన్యంతో వచ్చి మొదట పాండవులకు, తరువాత కౌరవులకు సహాయం చేస్తానన్నాడు, గర్వం వల్ల ఇద్దరూ తిరస్కరించారు.*

Have you ever offered help in a way that made people not want it? A favour with a lecture inside it, a gift that comes with a reminder of who is giving? Rukmi (రుక్మి) is the king in the Mahabharata who did exactly that, on the biggest stage of all.

## Rukmini's brother

To understand Rukmi you have to go back to a wedding that did not happen. Rukmi was the eldest son of Bhishmaka, the king of Vidarbha, whose capital is traditionally called Kundina. His sister was Rukmini (రుక్మిణి), who loved Krishna by hearsay and sent him a letter, according to the Bhagavata. Rukmi, however, had decided that she should marry Shishupala, the king of Chedi, an ally he admired.

Krishna carried Rukmini away from her wedding procession, as the Bhagavata and Harivamsha tell it. Rukmi chased him with an army, and was defeated. Krishna was about to kill him, but Rukmini begged for her brother's life. Krishna spared him, and in the tellings that follow the Bhagavata he disfigured Rukmi by shaving part of his head and moustache as a sign of shame. Rukmi took a vow, the story says, never to go back to Kundina until he had killed Krishna. He founded a new city, traditionally called Bhojakata, and lived there, nursing his wound.

## At the edge of the war

Years later, as the armies gathered, the Mahabharata (Udyoga Parva) says Rukmi came to the Pandavas with an akshauhini, a vast army, and offered his help. He was proud of his skill, being a student, it is said, of the great teacher Parashurama or Drona (the sources vary), and he held a famous bow.

Arjuna refused him. The reasons given are mixed. In the telling most quoted, Rukmi spoke boastfully, saying he would take on whatever part of the fight they wanted, and Arjuna would not accept help given as a favour. He did not need it, and Krishna was already by his side. Rukmi, offended, went to Duryodhana.

Duryodhana, too, turned him down. In tellings the reasoning is that he had already been refused by the Pandavas and was a second-best choice; that he arrived with insult and arrogance; or that Duryodhana did not want a man who owed him nothing and would demand much. We cannot be certain which reason the text gives, and it may be a mixture. What we can say is that this one king, with a full army, stood at the door of both camps, and neither opened it.

## What the pride story asks

It is tempting to see this as only a comic scene: a vain man looking for a stage. But consider his position. He had lost a sister to Krishna, a war to Krishna and part of his dignity to Krishna. He could not go home, could not fight his brother-in-law, and could not see himself as other than the injured party. Pride had turned every offer into a bargain for respect.

Think about how this appears in everyday life. The relative who helps and keeps the account. The friend who is generous but needs to be thanked every time. We have all met Rukmi, and sometimes we have been him. Pride in the epic is not just a fault; it is what removes a person from the story. He has an army and no place to stand.

Notice what the refusal says about Arjuna and Duryodhana as well. The Pandavas were careful about whose help they took, because help brings a claim. Even Duryodhana, who gathered every ally he could, was unwilling to take this one. An ally whose pride is larger than his loyalty is a weight, not a weapon. Why did the text keep this scene at all? Perhaps to show that numbers do not decide a war, and that character is weighed before strength.

## The end at Dwaraka

Rukmi's later fate is told in the Bhagavata, and it is not gentle. At the wedding of his granddaughter and Aniruddha, Krishna's grandson, the family played dice in the festivities. Rukmi quarrelled with Balarama about a throw, mocked him, and Balarama struck him dead. The scene connects with [Balarama's own story of being the brother who stayed out of the war](/balarama-the-brother-who-stayed-out-of-the-war/), and it shows that the elder brother's gentleness had an edge when he was insulted.

If you want to see how another character handled a family tie that pulled him away from Krishna's side, read [the Kaurava who crossed the line before the war](/yuyutsu-the-kaurava-who-crossed-the-line-before-the-war/). Yuyutsu moved to the other side; Rukmi, who could not, stayed nowhere.

## Where this comes from

The refusal by both sides is in the Udyoga Parva of Vyasa's Mahabharata. The abduction of Rukmini and Rukmi's humiliation, as well as his death at the dice game, come from the Bhagavata Purana (tenth skandha) and the Harivamsha; the Mahabharata mentions the abduction only briefly. Tellings differ on his teachers, on the exact reason Duryodhana refused him and on the exact details of the dice quarrel. Folk and temple traditions in the south often tell the Rukmini story with more attention to her and less to him.

## A question to carry

When you help someone, is it for them, or for the picture of yourself as the helper, and what would you do if the help were refused?
