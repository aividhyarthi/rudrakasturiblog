**Source:** Mahabharata, Bhishma Parva (the opening chapters), and Shalya Parva for his capture.

When the great war began, there was no television, no news and no reporter on the field. But there was one man who saw all of it, minute by minute, from the king's palace a long way away.

## An offer and a refusal
On the eve of the war, the sage Vyasa came to Dhritarashtra, the blind king of Hastinapura, whose sons were about to fight. He said that he could give the king the gift of sight, so that he might watch the war with his own eyes.

Dhritarashtra said no. He wanted to know how the war went, he said, but he could not bear to *see* the death of his own family. The text does not say if this was love or fear, and we are probably meant to wonder.

Vyasa then turned to Sanjaya, the king's charioteer and counsellor, and gave him *divya-drishti*, divine sight. With it, Sanjaya could see all that happened on the battlefield, by day or night, near or far, and even know the thoughts of the warriors. He could not be wounded and could not tire.

## The reporter
On the tenth day, Sanjaya comes to the king and says, *Bhishma has fallen.* After that, he narrates the war day by day. And what a narrator he is. He does not pick sides. He does not soften what the king does not want to hear. When the king asks, again and again, *and then, who won?*, Sanjaya tells him the truth, even about his own sons.

In the Bhishma Parva, when Dhritarashtra asks him to tell the whole story from the beginning, Sanjaya repeats what Krishna said to Arjuna on the field. That is the Bhagavad Gita. It reaches us through him: *Sanjaya uvacha*. The Gita's final verse (18.78) is his: wherever there are Krishna and Arjuna, there will be prosperity, victory and wise policy, and so I think.

## Honest witness
There is something deeply moving in a man who sees everything, says it plainly, and is not corrupted by the powerful man in front of him. Sanjaya had every reason to tell Dhritarashtra only what pleased him. Instead he repeatedly warned the king, before the war, that his sons were wrong. He is one of the unsung heroes of the epic.

Sanjaya, in the end, lost his gift. Near the end of the war he was captured by the Pandava warrior Satyaki and was about to be killed when Vyasa arrived and ordered his release. The text says that with the ending of the war, his divine sight was gone. His job was over.

## What it teaches
- **To see clearly is a gift and a burden.** Dhritarashtra refused the burden and kept the blindness.
- **Tell the truth, even to the powerful.** Sanjaya's honesty is the model for the best kind of advisor.
- **The Gita is a message passed through a witness.** We have it because someone listened carefully and repeated it exactly.
- **A good witness does not take sides.** He reports.

## Where to connect
Some scholars call Sanjaya the first war correspondent in world literature. A famous verse, the last of the Gita, ends with his own conclusion, and it is chanted at the close of many recitals.
