Sita's days in Ashoka Vatika are filled with fear, pressure and uncertainty. Around her are people who want her to surrender to Ravana. Yet the Ramayana tradition also remembers a woman in Lanka who offers her kindness.

Her name is Sarama.

## A different voice in Lanka

Sarama is remembered in some tellings as a woman connected to Vibhishana's household and as someone who treats Sita with compassion. While Sita is held captive, Sarama offers comfort and helps her understand events taking place in Lanka.

Her role is not that of a warrior. She does not defeat Ravana or lead an army. Her service is quieter: she brings information, reassurance and human kindness to someone living through an unbearable situation.

## Why her presence matters

Sita refuses Ravana's demands and remains firm in her faith in Lord Rama. But courage does not mean that a person never feels fear or loneliness. A kind word can matter greatly when someone is isolated.

Sarama's presence reminds us that goodness does not always follow political boundaries. A person living in Lanka can still choose compassion, even when the ruler of that land acts unjustly.

## Different tellings of the Ramayana

The details of Sarama's role differ across Ramayana traditions. Some versions give her a more prominent place in the episodes involving Sita. A Southwala article should identify the specific text or regional retelling being followed rather than combine every version into one account.

This distinction is important because the Ramayana has travelled through many languages and communities. Its central devotion remains powerful, while details can vary.

## What her story teaches

Sarama's story honours the courage to do what is right even when the surrounding environment makes it difficult. She reminds us that support does not always need to be dramatic. Sometimes it is a message, a warning or a few words that help another person hold on.

## The deeper meaning

The story of Sita in Lanka is often told through the strength of Sita, the devotion of Hanuman and the arrival of Rama. Sarama adds another dimension: the quiet compassion of someone who chooses humanity in a place ruled by fear.
