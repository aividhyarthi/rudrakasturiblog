Stand at the Triveni Sangam in Prayagraj, where two rivers meet, one pale and one dark. Every pilgrim is told there are three. The third, the Saraswati, is said to flow underground. You cannot see her. But you are told she is there, and many people believe it as firmly as they believe in the other two.

The strange thing is that this is not just a pious legend. Behind it is one of the great puzzles of Indian history.

## A river in the Rig Veda
In the Rig Veda, the oldest text of the tradition, there is a great river called the Saraswati. A famous hymn (2.41.16) calls her *ambitame, naditame, devitame*: the best of mothers, the best of rivers, the best of goddesses. Other hymns, such as the *Nadistuti*, the hymn of the rivers (10.75), place her between the Yamuna and the Sutlej, and speak of her as a mighty stream flowing from the mountains to the sea, "swift and wide".

Then, in later texts, she changes. The Brahmanas and the Mahabharata speak of a river that is shrinking and disappearing. In the Mahabharata (Shalya Parva), the pilgrim Balarama follows her course westward and finds the place where she vanishes into the sand, a place called *Vinashana*, "the place of disappearance". She is, by then, a river that no longer reaches the sea.

## What happened?
Here the question moves from the text to the land. Researchers using satellite images, geology and archaeology have noticed that in north-west India, there is a dried-up channel, wide and flat, running through the plains of Haryana, Rajasthan and Gujarat. It is called, in different stretches, the *Ghaggar* and the *Hakra*. Along its banks are hundreds of settlements of the Harappan civilisation, including Kalibangan and Rakhigarhi. Many scholars believe that this was once a large, seasonal river, and that its drying, as the rivers that fed it changed course, may have been part of the story of the end of Harappan cities.

Many connect this channel with the Vedic Saraswati. Others think the identification is not certain. Some argue that the Saraswati of the Rig Veda may be a river in Afghanistan, the Helmand, because a river with a similar name appears in the Avesta. The truth is that scholars still disagree, and a careful reader should be wary of anyone who claims to have settled it.

## From river to goddess
What is certain is what the *tradition* did with her. In the Vedas, the river is already addressed as a goddess. She is associated with *Vac*, speech, and with inspiration. As the physical river faded, her presence in the imagination grew. By the time of the Puranas, Saraswati is the goddess of speech, music and learning, seated on a white lotus, with a veena in her hands. She is the one before whom a child writes the first letter. See [Saraswati and the first letter](/saraswati-and-the-first-letter-aksharabhyasam-at-home/).

It is one of the loveliest transformations in Hindu thought: a river that dried up and became *the source of understanding*.

## The river that is unseen
At Prayag, tradition says, the Saraswati joins the Ganga and Yamuna underground. Pilgrims bathe in the confluence, the *sangam*, and call it a union of three. There is no geological proof that a river flows there, but the idea has a lasting power: *that which is hidden is not absent.* A culture that remembers a vanished river, and gives it a place at every great gathering, has a fine sense of the value of what has passed.

## Other places that remember her
- **Pushkar,** in Rajasthan, and **Kurukshetra** in Haryana, have tanks and temples linked with her.
- **Somnath,** in Gujarat, is said to lie near where she meets the sea in some traditions.
- **Basar,** on the Godavari in Telangana, is a Saraswati temple, among the few in India dedicated to her.
- **Kashmir:** the *Sharada* temple tradition honours her as the goddess of learning.

## A balanced view
It is good to hold two things at once. The first is that the Vedic Saraswati was probably a real river, whose memory is preserved in our oldest texts. The second is that exactly which river it was, and when it dried, are questions that researchers continue to work on. Faith and scholarship need not quarrel. The first tells us what the river meant to people. The second tells us, slowly, what it was.

## A thought
The next time you say *Saraswati namastubhyam* as you begin a book or a task, you are addressing a river that nourished a civilisation. A student before an exam is, in a sense, kneeling on a dry riverbed and asking for water.
