A kind man gets a serious illness. A woman who gave her life to her family is left alone. A corrupt official is promoted. And someone says, quietly, "It is karma." Another person, listening, thinks: if that is karma, it is not fair.

This is one of the oldest questions in Indian thought, and the answer is not simple.

## What the Gita admits
In the fourth chapter, verse 17, Krishna says: the nature of action is hard to understand. One must understand action, wrong action and inaction. The way of karma is deep.

That is an honest thing to say. The Gita does not offer a neat ledger. It says: be humble. We do not see all the causes.

## What the epics show
Look at the stories we have told in the [karma series](/karma-stories/). Rama is exiled. Yudhishthira loses his kingdom at dice. Bhishma lies on the arrows. Even good people suffer, and nobody in the epics says that they deserved it in a simple way. What the stories give is a picture of how actions travel, in ways that are not always visible.

## The riddle of the wonder
In the Mahabharata, in the forest, the Pandavas are thirsty and come to a lake guarded by a Yaksha. Four brothers drink without answering his questions and fall dead. Yudhishthira answers.

The Yaksha asks: what is the greatest wonder? Yudhishthira replies: every day, creatures go to the house of death, and yet those who remain wish to live forever. What could be more wonderful than that?

It is a way of saying that the real puzzle is not why bad things happen. It is how we carry on as if they never would.

## Three ways to use karma, and one way not to
**Use it on yourself.** Karma is most useful as a question you ask about your own actions: what am I putting out? It gives you a reason to act well, not for a reward but because actions shape you.

**Use it for acceptance.** What has already happened cannot be undone. [The three kinds of karma](/three-kinds-of-karma-sanchita-prarabdha-kriyamana/) say the arrow that has left the bow has left. You can accept it and still decide what to do next.

**Use it for compassion.** If you cannot see the whole picture, you cannot judge anyone. Treat the suffering person with kindness.

**Do not use it to blame.** "You must have done something in a past life" is a cruel thing to say to someone who has lost a child or been abused. Teachers who know the texts say this plainly. Karma is not an excuse for cruelty, for ignoring injustice or for staying in a harmful situation. The same tradition that speaks of karma tells you to help the one who is suffering.

## What about the cheat who prospers?
The tradition says that results do not always show in the same life or in the way we expect. It also says that wealth and power are not the same as happiness or peace. Many a rich person is miserable, and many a poor person is content. We cannot see another person's inner life.

But the tradition does not ask you to wait for justice in some other life. It asks you to work for justice here, within your reach.

## When you are the one suffering
It may help to know that:
- You are not being punished.
- It is natural to ask why.
- Meaning can sometimes be made from the suffering, not found in it.
- Help is a part of dharma, and asking for it is not weak.

## The short version
The universe may well be fair in a long run that we cannot see. What we can see is our own next action. That is the piece we hold. See [karma and free will](/karma-and-free-will-what-the-gita-says/).
