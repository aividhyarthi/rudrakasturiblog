**Source:** Valmiki Ramayana, Aranyakanda (chapter 74). The berry-tasting and the nine forms of devotion are from later retellings, and are marked below.

She is one of the most loved figures in the Ramayana, and she appears in a single chapter.

## The hermitage of Matanga
After Kabandha showed Rama the way, the brothers walked toward Lake Pampa. On the way they came to the hermitage of the sage Matanga. His disciples had gone to the higher worlds long ago, and one old woman remained. Her name was Shabari. Valmiki calls her a *shramani*, a woman ascetic, and says she was far advanced in her practice.

She had waited there for a long time. The text says that her teachers, before they left, told her that Rama would come to this hermitage, and that she should remain until she saw him. So she stayed.

## The meeting
When she saw Rama and Lakshmana, she welcomed them with the simple rites a hermitage can offer: water for the feet, a seat, fruits and roots that she had collected from the forest. Rama asked about her austerities and whether she had overcome the obstacles of the practice, and she answered that all was well, now that she had seen him.

She then showed them the forest around: the places where the sages had made their offerings, and the wonders that the power of her teachers had left behind. When she had finished, she asked Rama's permission to leave the body and join her teachers. He agreed. Shabari lit a fire, entered it, and rose in a radiant form to the world of her gurus (Aranyakanda 74).

That is the whole of it in Valmiki. She is there, she waits, she serves, she goes.

## What later retellings added
It is the later versions that give us the image most people remember. In popular retellings and many folk songs, Shabari tastes each berry before offering it, to be sure that only the sweetest are given to Rama. Lakshmana frowns, but Rama accepts the half-eaten berries with joy, because he sees the love in them. It is not in Valmiki, but it has stayed in the hearts of readers for centuries because it says something true about love: that it cares more about giving the best than about the rules.

In Tulsidas's *Ramcharitmanas* and the *Adhyatma Ramayana*, Rama also teaches her the *navavidha bhakti*, the nine forms of devotion. The lists differ from text to text. Tulsidas's begins with the company of the holy, love of the stories of Rama and service of the teacher. It is a beautiful teaching, and it is from these later works, not from Valmiki.

## Why Matanga's hermitage matters
There is also a story from Valmiki (Kishkindha Kanda) that connects to this hermitage. Vali, the monkey king, was cursed by the sage Matanga after throwing a buffalo's body into his hermitage. As a result, Vali could not enter the region around Matanga's hill, which is why his brother Sugriva could hide at Rishyamukha nearby. So the hermitage of the old teacher is quietly part of the plot: its protection is what lets Sugriva survive and, later, meet Rama.

## What it teaches
- **A life of waiting can be a life of service.** She was not idle. She kept the hermitage.
- **The gift is the heart, not the thing.** Valmiki's fruits and the later berries both say it.
- **Teachers' promises are kept.** Her gurus said Rama would come, and he did.
- **The last people to meet a hero may be the most prepared.** Shabari was ready.

## Where to see it
Hampi in Karnataka is traditionally identified with the Kishkindha of the Ramayana. Matanga Hill, Lake Pampa and the Anjanadri hill are within a short distance, and many pilgrims visit them every year.
