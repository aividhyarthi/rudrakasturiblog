Think of the one person in your family who is always at the edge of every quarrel. Not the loudest, not the eldest, but the one who seems to know exactly which word will start it. Most of us have met such a person. The Mahabharata has one too, and his name is Shakuni (శకుని).

## A prince from the north-west

Shakuni was the son of Subala, king of Gandhara, a land in what is now Afghanistan and north-west Pakistan, far from the Kuru heartland. His sister was Gandhari. The epic does not describe a quiet courtly alliance. Tradition holds that Bhishma, looking for a bride for the blind prince Dhritarashtra, sent word to Gandhara that the match was wanted, and a small kingdom did not find it easy to refuse the great house of Hastinapura.

Subala agreed. Shakuni brought his sister to Hastinapura, and the epic says he stayed on in the Kuru court afterwards, close to his nephews.

Pause on that. A proud young man delivers his sister to a household where her husband cannot see, and she herself binds her eyes. Whether he swallowed resentment at that moment, the text does not tell us in so many words.

## What the epic shows

In Vyasa's account Shakuni is, above all, an adviser to Duryodhana. When Duryodhana burns with envy at the Pandavas' prosperity at Indraprastha, Shakuni offers a plan: Yudhishthira likes dice but does not play well; Shakuni plays very well. He will play on Duryodhana's behalf. The dice game in the Sabha Parva follows.

Notice what is and is not said. The text gives him cleverness and loyalty to his nephew. It does not say clearly that he has spent years planning to destroy the Kuru line. That motive is largely something later tellers and modern readers have added, because we like our villains to have a wound.

## The folk image

The popular story goes like this. Bhishma, after the marriage, imprisoned Subala and his sons and gave the family a single handful of rice a day. Subala, seeing his sons weaken, asked them to leave the food for the one who was most clever. Shakuni survived. The dying father asked that his bones be made into dice that would always obey Shakuni, and that he would one day ruin the Kurus.

I am telling you this as a story you will hear, not as Vyasa's text. Scholars who compare manuscripts generally treat this as a later folk and literary tradition, and regional versions differ in the details. It is moving, and it explains him neatly. That neatness is itself a reason to be careful.

## Why the grievance matters even if it is later

Why did people feel the need to add it? Because the epic asks a hard question: can one person's injury become a whole family's ruin? A sibling who feels overlooked, a relative whose family was humbled for another's convenience: such hurt can sit for years and come out as advice, a joke, a "harmless" game.

Yet notice the epic's own judgement. Even if Shakuni had a grievance, it did not make the dice game right. Draupadi's questions in that hall, and the silence that answered them, are in another piece: [Draupadi's questions in the court](/draupadis-questions-in-the-court-and-the-answer-she-never-got/). Shakuni's pain does not excuse what Duryodhana and the court did with it.

## What happens to him

On the last days of the war Shakuni's own Gandharan troops were destroyed, and the epic says Sahadeva killed him. Gandhari, his sister, lost almost everything; her sorrow and her curse come later, as in [Gandhari's curse and Krishna](/gandharis-curse-and-krishna-karma-of-the-yadavas/).

## Is he only a villain?

Look at what Shakuni actually does in the text. He does not raise a weapon against the Pandavas in the dice hall; he rolls the dice and watches the others. The cruelty of that evening is carried out by the court, by Duryodhana and Dushasana, with Shakuni as the quiet engine. That is exactly why he is so unsettling. He is the person who makes something terrible possible and keeps his own hands tidy.

How often do we meet this in daily life? The colleague who suggests, never decides. The relative who only mentions what the neighbours said. Shakuni is the epic's portrait of advice with no responsibility attached.

He also shows what happens when a young man is surrounded by someone who tells him what he wants to hear. Duryodhana had elders who warned him and an uncle who flattered him. He listened to the second. A good adviser is not the one who agrees with you fastest, and this is a lesson that does not need a war to be understood.

## A practical way to read him

If you are studying the Mahabharata with children or with elders at home, you might try this. Read the dice episode aloud, then ask two questions. Who played the game? Who allowed it? The answers are different, and the gap between them is where most of the epic's moral weight sits. Do it in a few quiet minutes, and let the answers be different for each person.

## Where this comes from

The dice game and Shakuni's role are in the Sabha Parva of Vyasa's Mahabharata, and his death is in the war books. The story of the bone dice and the starved family is not in the critical edition as far as I am aware; it appears in later popular retellings, stage and television adaptations, and some regional folk tradition. Tellings differ on whether Bhishma or someone else imprisoned the family, so please treat all such details as tradition, not text.
