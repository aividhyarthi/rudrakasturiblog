The Puranas tell the story in several ways. In the commonly told version, Brahma had five heads, and the fifth spoke with arrogance, claiming that he alone was supreme. Shiva, in his form of Bhairava, cut that head off.

## The skull that would not fall
The act brought the weight of Brahmahatya, the sin of killing a Brahmin, and the skull of Brahma stuck to Shiva's hand. He wandered the world as Bhikshatana, a naked beggar with the skull as his bowl. In some tellings the food placed in it did not fill it, and his wandering continued through holy places and forests.

At last he came to Kashi, where the skull fell from his hand at a place still called Kapala Mochana (the release of the skull), and the burden lifted.

## What the story teaches
- **Anger acts first and answers later.** The head spoke in pride, and the answer was swift, but the cost followed.
- **Not even the lord of destruction is exempt.** Whatever one thinks of the act, the story insists that it left a mark which had to be carried.
- **Penance is a journey, not a moment.** Shiva does not talk himself out of it. He walks.
- **Release comes when the work is done.** The skull falls only at the end, and in a place of surrender.

Versions of this story differ among the Shiva Purana, Skanda Purana and regional traditions. The details change, but the teaching is the same.

## For today
If you have lost your temper and caused harm, the first step is to carry it honestly instead of denying it. Apologise, make it right in a practical way, and give it time. Release comes slowly, and it comes.

## What is Brahmahatya?
The word means the killing of a Brahmin. In the old law books it stands for the gravest kind of wrong, one that stains the doer until he makes it right. For the story, you do not need to dwell on caste. What matters is the idea behind it: some acts leave a weight that does not wash off by being sorry for a moment. It has to be carried, and then paid down over time.

## Bhikshatana, the wandering beggar
In temples of the South you will sometimes see a figure called Bhikshatanamurti: Shiva as the wandering beggar, shown young and handsome, often unclothed, holding a bowl, with a small deer by his side and sometimes followed by admiring onlookers. Many readers connect this form with the wanderings that followed the beheading of Brahma. The form asks us to imagine the Lord of all things standing at a door with an empty bowl. It is a picture of humility, and a reminder that nothing and no one is above the law of action and result.

## Bhairava and Kashi
Bhairava, the fierce form of Shiva who is said to have cut off the head, is honoured as the guardian of Kashi. Pilgrims there are traditionally told to take his blessing for permission. You can read more in [Kalabhairava, the guardian of time and Kashi](/kalabhairava-the-guardian-of-time-and-kashi/). Notice how the story ties together: the one who bears the guilt becomes the one who guards the holy city.

## A later echo in the Ramayana tradition
In later tradition, Rama, after the war with Ravana, is said to have sought purification for the killing of Ravana, who was born a brahmin, and to have worshipped Shiva at Rameswaram. This is not from Valmiki's main story, but it shows the same idea at work: even the righteous warrior feels that a great death asks for a pause. You can read more in [Rameswaram, where Rama prayed to Shiva](/rameswaram-ramanathaswamy-temple-where-rama-prayed-to-shiva/).

## Questions people ask

**Did Shiva really do wrong?**
Different tellings answer differently. In some, Brahma's pride is the fault; in others, Shiva's anger. The tradition holds both thoughts together without hurrying to settle them.

**Does Brahma have temples?**
Very few. The best known is at Pushkar in Rajasthan. Many people connect this rarity with stories like this one, but the Puranas give several reasons, so treat it as tradition, not settled fact.

**Why would a god need to repent?**
The story is not about guilt as a feeling. It is about the order of the world, in which every deed leaves a mark. It tells us that if even the Lord walks through this, we should not expect to skip it.

**What should I do if I carry a heavy mistake?**
Name it honestly, make amends in practical ways, and ask for help from a trusted elder, friend or counsellor. Prayer is a support, not a substitute. If guilt is weighing on your sleep and health, please speak to a doctor or counsellor.
