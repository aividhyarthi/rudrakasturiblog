Have you ever stood in front of a plain, dark stone in a temple and wondered why a god who has such a rich story is shown only as a smooth, rounded shape? That quiet question is where understanding the Shiva lingam begins.

## What the word means

Linga in Sanskrit means a sign, a mark or a symbol. The Shiva lingam is the sign of Shiva, the one who cannot be fully pictured. Tradition says the lingam is formless: it has no face, no arms, no ornament. It points to the reality that is before and beyond every form, which is also what Shiva means in the Upanishadic tradition. In Telugu it is శివలింగం, in Tamil Sivalingam.

If you want a wider introduction to Shiva himself, our article on [who is Shiva, five things most people get wrong](/who-is-shiva-five-things-most-people-get-wrong/) is a good companion to this one.

## The story of Lingodbhava

The Linga Purana, and other texts, tell of a time when Brahma and Vishnu were arguing over which of them was supreme. Tradition holds that a vast column of fire appeared between them, with no beginning and no end. They agreed to search for its ends: Brahma flew upward as a swan, Vishnu dug downward as a boar. Neither could find an end. Vishnu came back and admitted he could not. Brahma, in some tellings, claimed falsely to have seen the top, and was rebuked. From the fire Shiva appeared, and both bowed.

This is called Lingodbhava, the birth from the linga. You will find the image carved on the back wall of the sanctum in many Shiva temples, particularly in Tamil Nadu. It is the story that explains why the lingam is a column, a pillar of light that has no limit. Tellings differ in detail, so take this as the traditional outline.

## The parts

A lingam stands in three parts, though not all are always visible:

- **The base, the pitha.** The lower part, often square, said in some readings to be Brahma's.
- **The middle, the yoni or avudai (the lower base).** The round or oval base with a spout for the water to drain. In tradition this is understood as the shakti, the divine feminine power in which the lingam is established. Teachers speak of it with great reverence, as the union of the supporting power and the supported Self, and not as anything to be treated lightly.
- **The top, the linga itself.** The rounded cylinder, said to be Shiva's. Some traditions assign the three parts to Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva.

The water that flows over the lingam passes along the spout. Many families take care not to step across it, and some touch the water to their eyes as a blessing.

## Simple worship at home

You need not have a large lingam or a priest. Many homes keep a small stone lingam, or a Narmada lingam, in the puja corner. A simple order:

1. Wash your hands and feet. Light a lamp.
2. Pour water over the lingam slowly, chanting Om Namah Shivaya or the Panchakshari.
3. If your family does so, pour a little milk, then water again. Some families add curd, honey and ghee; those are more elaborate and not required.
4. Place a few bilva leaves, with the stalk toward you and the smooth side down on the lingam, as tradition suggests. Odd numbers, especially three, are common.
5. Offer white flowers if you have them, then show a lamp and incense.
6. Apply vibhuti, sacred ash, to your forehead and sit in silence for a few minutes.

That is it. Elaborate abhisheka, like Rudram chanting, can follow when you are ready. Our article on [Rudram and Namakam](/rudram-and-namakam-what-the-great-hymn-to-shiva-says/) explains the great hymn used for such worship.

## Kinds of lingam

Not every lingam is the same. A swayambhu lingam is one believed to have arisen on its own, as at some ancient temples. A banalinga is a smooth stone from the Narmada river, often kept at home. Some lingams are made of sand, as the Prithvi lingam at Kanchipuram, or of clay, which devotees make and immerse on certain days. The tradition accepts all of them, because the worship is directed at Shiva and not at the stone's material. A small lingam you can hold is as welcome as one in a great temple.

## What not to offer

Tradition lists a few things to keep away from Shiva worship. Tulasi is associated with Vishnu and is not offered to Shiva. Ketaki, the screwpine flower, is excluded, and the stories behind this vary; one well-known telling links it with Brahma's false claim in the Lingodbhava story. Turmeric (haldi) is usually not applied to the lingam, since it is associated with Shakti and with auspicious marriage rites. Families differ on all of this, so ask your elders if a custom matters to your home.

## Mondays and Pradosham

Monday, Somavara, is Shiva's day, and many devotees fast or offer water and bilva to the lingam that morning. Read more about the day in [Monday Shiva worship and the Panchakshari](/monday-shiva-worship-panchakshari-and-pournami/).

Pradosham, the twilight on the thirteenth lunar day of each fortnight, is considered a specially graceful time to worship Shiva. Many families visit a temple or do a simple puja at home at dusk. See our page on [Pradosham](/pradosham/) for dates.

## A quiet thought

The lingam asks you to do something odd: to look at a plain shape and drop the need for a face. For some that is hard. For others it is the first relief they have felt in prayer. Which is it for you?
