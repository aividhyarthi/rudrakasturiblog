It is hard now to remember how low the reputation of Tantra stood a hundred years ago. In respectable circles in Calcutta and London, it was a word for superstition and worse. A British judge decided to read the texts instead.

## A lawyer in Calcutta
John George Woodroffe was born in Calcutta in 1865, the son of the Advocate-General of Bengal. He studied at Oxford, trained as a barrister and returned to Calcutta to practise. In 1904, he was appointed a judge of the High Court of Calcutta, and he served for almost twenty years.

## The first reading
It was his work in the courts that led him toward the texts. Cases involving Hindu law and customs were referred to him, and he found that many of them depended on traditions that he could not follow without reading the original Sanskrit. He began to study with Indian scholars, and his interest widened from law to philosophy.

Among the people who helped him was the pandit Shivachandra Vidyarnava, who had edited a Tantric text and who guided Woodroffe through the field.

## Arthur Avalon
From 1913 on, Woodroffe published a series of translations and studies under the pen name Arthur Avalon. He had good reasons to be careful. As a sitting judge he did not wish his scholarship to be mixed up with his rulings, and the subject was controversial. His books included *Hymns to the Goddess*, *Shakti and Shakta* and, most famously, *The Serpent Power* in 1919. He also began the Tantrik Texts series, which published Sanskrit originals with notes.

## What he argued
Woodroffe's case was that Tantra was a serious spiritual and philosophical tradition and that much of what was said against it came from ignorance, or from reading the wildest parts of a literature that has many layers. He explained the idea of Shakti, the divine power, and of kundalini, the energy said to lie at the base of the spine, as a system with a long history and its own logic. He also stressed that Tantric practice should be learned from a qualified teacher.

## The criticism
Not everyone agreed. Some Western writers thought he took the subject too seriously, and some Indian scholars felt that he idealised it. Later researchers have added more detail and have sometimes corrected his readings. Still, they credit him with opening the subject for serious study.

## After the court
He retired in 1922 and taught Indian law at Oxford from 1923 to 1930. He died in France in 1936.

## What it shows
It is easy to read the past as a story of Europeans looking down on India. Woodroffe is a reminder that there were also those who looked more closely, and that the best of them did so with the help of Indian teachers.

## A closer look at The Serpent Power
Of all his books, *The Serpent Power* is the one most readers have heard of. It is not a work Woodroffe wrote from scratch. It is a translation, with a long introduction and commentary, of two Sanskrit texts about the subtle centres of the body, called *chakras*: the *Shat-chakra-nirupana* and a shorter companion text. The introduction is where Woodroffe tried to explain, in careful English, what Indian readers had long taken for granted: that the chakras and the rising of kundalini are described as a map of inner practice, not a physical anatomy lesson.

This matters because many popular books today repeat the words without the context. If you want to understand what the old writers meant, it helps to know that they wrote for students who had a teacher beside them.

## Questions people ask

**Was Woodroffe the first Westerner to study Sanskrit?**
No. Others came before him, such as [Sir William Jones](/sir-william-jones-the-judge-who-fell-for-sanskrit/), who was also a judge in Calcutta, and [Max Muller](/max-muller-the-man-who-never-saw-india-but-edited-the-rig-veda/), who edited the Rig Veda in Oxford. Woodroffe's special field was Tantra, which many scholars of his time avoided.

**Why did he use a pen name?**
As a sitting High Court judge, he did not want his private scholarship tied to his public office. The name Arthur Avalon appeared on his books for many years.

**Is it safe to try the practices he describes?**
Woodroffe himself said that such practices should be learned from a qualified teacher. Reading about them is one thing. Doing breathing and concentration exercises that you do not understand can be unsettling. If you are curious, begin with gentle and well-known practices such as [pranayama for beginners](/pranayama-for-beginners-three-safe-breathing-practices/).

## Why his story still matters
There is a lesson here that has nothing to do with Tantra. Woodroffe changed his mind because he did the work of reading. He did not need to agree with everything, but he refused to judge a tradition he had not studied. Many of us carry opinions about other people's beliefs that we have never examined. His example is a small, steady invitation to look first.
