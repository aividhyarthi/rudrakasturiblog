When the East India Company's ships brought Englishmen to Calcutta in the 1700s, most came to trade or fight. William Jones came to judge. And somewhere between the courtroom and the library, he became one of the first Europeans to take Indian thought seriously.

## A judge with a gift for languages
Jones was born in London in 1746 and was already known as a prodigy of languages. He is said to have mastered more than a dozen, including Persian and Arabic, before he turned thirty. In 1783 he was appointed a judge of the Supreme Court in Calcutta and sailed for India.

## Why he learned Sanskrit
As a judge, he had to rule on disputes under Hindu and Muslim law. The British at that time relied on local scholars to interpret the texts, and Jones was uneasy about trusting translations. So he decided to learn Sanskrit himself. He found a teacher among the pandits of Bengal, and read the texts in the original.

He soon realised he had found something more than a legal resource.

## The Asiatic Society
In 1784, with a group of friends, Jones founded the Asiatic Society in Calcutta, to study the history, languages and culture of Asia. It became one of the first scholarly societies of its kind, and its journal published a flood of new studies on India. The society still exists, as the Asiatic Society in Kolkata.

## The speech that changed linguistics
In 1786 he gave a famous talk in which he said that Sanskrit, "whatever be its antiquity, is of a wonderful structure; more perfect than the Greek, more copious than the Latin, and more exquisitely refined than either." He added that the three languages had a common source, which perhaps no longer existed. It is often seen as the beginning of the study of Indo-European languages.

## A hymn to Narayana
Jones was not only an academic. He wrote English hymns to Hindu deities. His *Hymn to Narayena*, published in 1785, speaks of Vishnu as the spirit that moved on the waters. It was an unusual thing for an Englishman and a judge to write. He wrote others to Lakshmi, Saraswati and Ganga.

## Translations
He translated the play *Shakuntala* by Kalidasa in 1789, which was soon read all over Europe and admired by Goethe. He also translated the *Gita Govinda* and, just before his death, the laws of Manu, called *Institutes of Hindu Law*, for use by the courts.

## Did he become a Hindu?
No. Jones remained a Christian all his life, and he did not believe that the Hindu traditions were equal to his own religion. But he did believe that they were worth understanding on their own terms, and said that the Hindu texts should be known by the British because they would not otherwise be able to rule fairly. That is where, as always, historians point to the mixed motives in this story: the same knowledge helped the Company govern.

## The end
He died in Calcutta in April 1794, at the age of 47. His grave is in the South Park Street Cemetery, where it can still be seen.
