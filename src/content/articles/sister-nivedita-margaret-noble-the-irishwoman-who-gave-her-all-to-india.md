Margaret Noble did not set out to be a saint, a revolutionary or a writer. She was a young Irish schoolteacher with questions she could not answer. Her story is one of the best examples of what a single conversation can change.

## Before India
She was born on 28 October 1867 in Dungannon, Ireland, into a family of Protestant clergymen and teachers. She became a teacher herself and opened a school in London. She was active in the education reform movement and in writing circles. But in religion she was restless. She had grown away from the strict Christianity of her childhood, and nothing satisfied her.

## November 1895
In London she attended a talk by an Indian monk, Swami Vivekananda, who had been invited to speak at a friend's house. She was struck by his words, though she did not agree with all of them. She went to listen again and again, arguing with him, asking hard questions. In time she was convinced that what he taught about the divine in every person could change lives.

When Vivekananda said that India needed women to educate other women, he asked her whether she would come.

## Coming to India
She sailed to India and reached Calcutta in January 1898. Vivekananda gave her the name Nivedita, meaning the dedicated one, in March of that year. It was not easy at first. She learned Bengali and lived in simple surroundings, and she was warned that she would not be welcomed in all families. She became close to Sarada Devi, the wife of Ramakrishna, who treated her like a daughter.

## The school and the plague
In November 1898, she opened a school for girls in Baghbazar, in north Calcutta, which Sarada Devi blessed. Girls from conservative families came, often to learn to read. In 1899 an outbreak of plague hit Calcutta. Nivedita and her volunteers cleaned the streets and drains and nursed the sick, sometimes going door to door.

## A voice for India
In the following years, she wrote on Indian art, myths and customs, in books like *The Web of Indian Life* and *Kali the Mother*. She became a close friend of the scientist Jagadish Chandra Bose and the painter Abanindranath Tagore, and supported the young nationalists, including those of the Swadeshi movement. She was clear that India must find its own way. Her association with politics led her to separate from the Ramakrishna Mission formally, though she remained devoted to her guru.

## The end
Nivedita died in Darjeeling on 13 October 1911, at the age of 43. Her memorial there carries the words: *Here lies Sister Nivedita who gave her all to India.*

## Why she matters
She is often remembered in India as an example of the way a foreigner could be a true friend. She did not arrive as an expert. She came as a student, and she stayed as a worker. Many people who have read her letters say that what stands out is her humility.
