It is Monday morning, the day after Deepavali. The lamps from last night are still on the steps, and the house smells of oil and smoke and sweets. And yet something else is in the air as well: a quiet pull to be still. This is Amavasya, the night with no moon, and it has landed on Shiva's own weekday.

That is why people call today **Somavati Amavasya** (సోమవతి అమావాస్య). Here is what that means, when exactly it begins and ends, what families do, and what the old story says.

## Amavasya on 8 or 9 November?

You may have seen both dates, and both are correct. They answer different questions.

- **Sunday evening, 8 November.** The Amavasya tithi is already running at dusk. That is the evening of Deepavali and Lakshmi Puja, and it is why many calendars name 8 November for Amavasya.
- **Monday morning, 9 November.** The tithi is still running at sunrise, and it ends at 12:32 PM. The morning bath, the tarpanam for the ancestors and the Somavati observances are done today, within those hours.

We list 8 November as the Amavasya date on our [Amavasya dates page](/amavasya/november-2026/), because we take the day when the tithi is present in the afternoon. Many families and temples count the sunrise tithi instead, which makes it Monday. If your family follows a fixed rule, keep to it, and if you are unsure, ask your priest.

## What Somavati Amavasya means

*Soma* means the Moon. It is also a name used for Shiva, who wears the crescent. *Vara* means day. Monday is *Somavara*, the day of the Moon and of Shiva.

Amavasya, the new moon, is the day that tradition keeps for the ancestors. So a Monday Amavasya brings two things together: worship of Shiva, and remembrance of the family that came before. It is rare, which is why people plan for it.

It also has a gentle mood. Many teachers say the new moon is not unlucky in itself. It is a day for turning inward, for prayer and silence rather than celebration.

## Who keeps the vrat

In North India, Somavati Amavasya is best known as a vrat for married women, who fast and worship the peepal tree for the long life and health of their husbands and family. It is close in spirit to Vat Savitri, where the banyan is worshipped for the same prayer.

In many families, other people keep the day too: unmarried girls, men and elders, for Shiva and for the ancestors. In the Telugu and Tamil country the peepal custom is less common, and the day is more often kept with Shiva worship, a bath, tarpanam and charity. Neither way is more correct than the other.

## The story of Somavati Amavasya

This is a folk story, told with small differences from village to village. Some tellings say it was told by Bhishma to Yudhishthira after the war. We have not found a standard text for that, so we mention it only as the way the story is often introduced.

In the usual telling, a young woman from a respected family was told by a sage that her fate held no marriage, or in some versions that she would be widowed. Troubled, she went to a poor washerwoman called Soma, and began to serve her in secret. Every morning, before the household woke, the girl did the old woman's chores for her.

When Soma discovered who had been helping her, she was deeply moved, and she gave the girl her blessing as a married woman. In one telling the blessing cost Soma her own husband. With nothing else to offer, she went to the peepal tree she worshipped every day and walked around it 108 times, and her husband came back to life. Married women repeat her parikrama on this day.

Whatever you make of the details, the heart of the story is clear. The girl served without being asked and without being seen. The washerwoman gave what she had. In both, devotion and kindness do what power cannot.

## How to do the puja at home

Follow your family's custom first. This is a simple, common outline.

1. **Bathe before sunrise.** Bathe in a river if you can, or at home with a few drops of Ganga water or a little sesame in the water.
2. **Offer water to the rising Sun.** Stand facing the east and pour water from a copper or brass lota.
3. **Take a sankalp.** At your home shrine, say your name and your prayer for the day: for the family, for someone unwell, or for the peace of the ancestors.
4. **Worship Shiva.** Offer water or milk and bilva leaves to a Shivalinga at home or at a temple, and chant *Om Namah Shivaya*. Monday is his day.
5. **Light a lamp.** A ghee or sesame oil lamp at the shrine, and another at the doorstep at dusk.
6. **Worship the peepal tree if you can.** At the foot of the tree offer water, a little raw milk, kumkum, sandalwood, rice and flowers. Walk around it, winding a cotton thread or red and yellow moli round the trunk. The custom is 108 rounds. If that is too many, do what you can, with devotion.
7. **Remember the ancestors.** If your father is no longer living, offer water with black sesame (tarpanam) in the way your family does it. Many families set aside some food for crows before they eat.
8. **Give.** Share food, grain or clothes with someone in need, or give to a temple kitchen. Married women often give bangles and bindi to other women.
9. **Break the fast.** After the puja, with fruit or a simple vegetarian meal. Children, the elderly, pregnant women and anyone unwell should eat normally and keep the day through prayer and charity.

For the ancestors' side of the day, see [how to honour ancestors](/how-to-honour-ancestors-pitru-mantras-and-family-peace/).

## What to keep ready (samagri)

- A copper or brass lota and clean water
- A few drops of Ganga water, if you have it
- Milk, kumkum and sandalwood paste
- Akshat (unbroken rice) and flowers
- Black sesame (til)
- Raw cotton thread or red and yellow moli
- Fruits, flowers or sweets, if you will do the peepal rounds
- A ghee or oil lamp and wicks, with incense
- Bilva leaves for Shiva
- Bangles and bindi, if you will give suhaag daan
- Grain, clothes or food for charity

## The peepal tree

The peepal, or *ashvattha*, is one of the most honoured trees in India. In the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna says "of all trees I am the ashvattha" (10.26), and in chapter 15 he uses the picture of an ashvattha with its roots above and branches below to describe the world. Many people worship it as a dwelling of the three great gods.

If you go to a peepal tree today, choose one at a temple or a clean public place, and go in the morning. Pour the water and milk at the root. Light your lamp on the ground, away from the bark and dry leaves. As you walk, let the thread unwind round the trunk, round by round. To keep count, set aside one grain or flower at each round.

## How the day is kept in different places

- **Haridwar, Varanasi and Prayag.** Large crowds bathe in the Ganga from the early morning, and offer tarpan and charity on the bank.
- **North India generally.** Married women gather at a neighbourhood peepal or a temple courtyard, wind thread round the trunk and listen to the katha together.
- **The Telugu and Tamil country.** Monday is kept for Shiva with abhishekam and bilva leaves. Many families offer tarpanam and give food. Tarpanam is often done at a river, a tank or the sea. On this particular Amavasya many people also go to the temple after the bath.
- **Tomorrow and after.** Karthika Masam begins on 10 November. Its four Mondays, called Karthika Somavaram, are among the most important Shiva days of the year, so today is a natural start.

## What many families avoid on Amavasya

- Starting a new business, a wedding or a house-warming
- Cutting hair, shaving or cutting nails
- Non-vegetarian food
- Lending or borrowing money
- Quarrels and harsh words at home

These are traditional customs and they vary by family. Most teachers add that the new moon is not a day to fear. It is a day to slow down.

## Questions people ask

**Is it rare?**
It comes about once or twice in most years. This year it comes on the day after Deepavali, which makes it feel even more special.

**Can men observe it?**
Yes. Many men keep the day for Shiva and the ancestors, and do tarpanam.

**Can I do the puja without a peepal tree?**
Yes. Worship Shiva and Parvati at home or at a temple, and keep the prayer in mind.

**What should I give in charity?**
Food, grain, clothes and sesame are the traditional gifts. Feeding someone who is hungry is the simplest.

**Should I fast?**
Only if it suits your health. A fast until the puja, ended with fruit, is common. A strict fast is not needed.
