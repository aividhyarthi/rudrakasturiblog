Imagine an English teenager in 1916, flying a fragile aircraft over the trenches of France. Now imagine the same man, ten years later, in a simple robe, serving at a small Krishna temple in the mountains of India. That is the story of Ronald Nixon.

## A war and a question
Ronald Henry Nixon was born in England in 1898. He joined the Royal Flying Corps as a pilot in the First World War, and survived. Like many young men of his generation, he came out of the war with questions that his upbringing did not answer. He went to Cambridge to study, with an interest in philosophy and religion, and was drawn to Buddhist and Eastern texts.

## To India
In 1921 he was offered a post as lecturer in English at the University of Lucknow, and he went to India. It was meant to be a job. In Lucknow he became friends with the family of the Vice-Chancellor, Dr. Gyanendra Nath Chakravarti, and in particular with his wife, Monica Devi, a woman who had turned to a life of devotion to Krishna.

## Yashoda Ma
Monica Devi, who came to be called Yashoda Ma, became his guru. Nixon had read about devotion, but she showed it to him, as a daily practice of prayer and care. In 1928 he was formally initiated into the Vaishnava tradition, and took the name Sri Krishna Prem, Krishna's love. He was 30.

## Mirtola
In the early 1930s, the two moved to Mirtola, a village in the hills near Almora, in what is now Uttarakhand. They built a small ashram around a Radha-Krishna temple, and lived simply, with prayer, study and service. When Yashoda Ma died in 1944, Krishna Prem became the head of the ashram. He was joined by another Englishman, Madhava Ashish, who was Scottish by birth.

## What he taught
Krishna Prem did not preach. He wrote, and spoke to those who came. His books, *The Yoga of the Bhagavat Gita* and *The Yoga of the Kathopanishad*, are admired for their clarity. Where some Western writers treated Indian texts as a curiosity, he wrote from inside the tradition. He taught that devotion and knowledge are not opposed, and that the way to the Gita is through actually living it.

## Visitors
People who met him, among them the Dalai Lama's circle and well-known writers such as Alan Watts, remembered a quiet and humorous man, and never a performer. He treated his Indian visitors with the same courtesy as his European ones.

## The end
He lived at Mirtola until his death on 14 November 1965. His ashram still stands there.

## What stands out
Many people who come to the Gita do so after a shock. In Nixon's case it was a war. What is striking is that he did not turn to ideas alone. He turned to a teacher who was a Bengali woman and devotee of Krishna, and he took her seriously enough to change his life.

## Questions people ask
**Was he the only Westerner to take this path?**
No. The 20th century saw several Europeans and Americans turn to Indian traditions. You can read about others on Southwala, such as [Sister Nivedita](/sister-nivedita-margaret-noble-the-irishwoman-who-gave-her-all-to-india/) and [Annie Besant](/annie-besant-from-atheist-to-the-gita-and-home-rule/). Each came by a different door.

**Did he stop being English?**
He took a new name and a new way of life, but nothing in his story suggests he hated where he came from. What changed was what he wanted to spend his days doing.

**Why did a woman become his teacher?**
In the Vaishnava tradition, a guru is chosen by the quality of devotion and understanding, not by age, sex or nationality. Monica Devi had both, and Nixon was honest enough to see it.

## Why a pilot, and why Krishna?
It is tempting to say that war pushed him towards faith, and perhaps it did. But look at the shape of his later life. He did not run from the world. He became a teacher and a writer, and he lived in a community of prayer where ordinary villagers came and went.

Devotion to Krishna in the Vaishnava way is not only about feeling. It is about service, daily worship and a quiet discipline. A man who had been trained to be alert and precise in the air brought some of that to the lamp, the flowers and the book.

## Mirtola today
Mirtola lies in the hills of Kumaon, near Almora in Uttarakhand. It is a quiet place, and the ashram there is centred on a Radha-Krishna temple. If you ever plan to go, ask locally about visiting hours and manners first, because it is a place of prayer and not a tourist stop.

## What you can take from his life
- **It is fine to ask big questions late.** He came to India in his early twenties with no plan to become a monk.
- **A good teacher may not look like you expect.** He listened to someone the world might have overlooked.
- **Read the scripture and live it.** His books are valued because they come from practice.
- **Change need not be loud.** Much of his life was small daily acts in a mountain village.
