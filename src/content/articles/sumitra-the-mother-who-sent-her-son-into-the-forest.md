**Source:** Valmiki Ramayana, Ayodhya Kanda (chapter 40 and the chapters that follow), and Bala Kanda (chapter 16) for the sharing of the payasam.

There is a moment in the Ramayana that is easy to read past. A young man comes to his mother's room late at night, shaking with anger and grief, to tell her that he is leaving. He expects tears. He expects to be told to stay. What he gets is a few short, steady sentences, and a blessing.

Most readers remember Kaushalya's weeping and Kaikeyi's demand. The third queen is easy to overlook, and that, as it turns out, is the beauty of her.

## The quiet queen
Sumitra was the third wife of Dasharatha. When the king performed the sacrifice for sons, the divine *payasam* that came from the fire was divided among the queens. Kaushalya received half, Sumitra a quarter, and then, after Kaikeyi took her share, Sumitra was given a second portion (Bala Kanda 16). That is why she became the mother of twins, Lakshmana and Shatrughna, and the story says that each of them was drawn to one of the elder brothers: Lakshmana to Rama, Shatrughna to Bharata.

From then on, Sumitra rarely speaks in the epic. She is not in the quarrels, not in the politics, not in the speeches. Yet when she does speak, everyone stops.

## The night Lakshmana came to say goodbye
When Rama was ordered to the forest, Lakshmana decided at once that he would follow. He came to his mother to ask for her blessing. In the Valmiki text (Ayodhya Kanda 40), Sumitra does not stop him. She says something that has been quoted for generations:

> **rāmaṁ daśarathaṁ viddhi māṁ viddhi janakātmajām**
> **ayodhyām aṭavīṁ viddhi gaccha tāta yathā-sukham**

*Think of Rama as Dasharatha, think of Sita as me, think of the forest as Ayodhya. Go, my son, and go happily.*

Look at what she does with those four lines. She gives away her place as mother: Sita is now to be seen as her. She gives away the king's place: Rama is to be seen as the father. She gives away the idea of home: the forest is to be seen as Ayodhya. A person who has done that has given away everything that could make her son hesitate.

## What she does not say
She does not scold Kaikeyi. She does not weep. In the text she says only that she is glad her son has a chance to serve his brother. Some readers find this hard, even cold. Others see in it a deep trust: she knows her son, and she knows what he must do.

## The second test
Sumitra's strength is tested again. After Rama, Sita and Lakshmana have gone and Dasharatha has died, it is Sumitra who comforts the broken Kaushalya (Ayodhya Kanda, soon after). She tells her that Rama is a man of dharma, who will return, and that no one who walks in dharma comes to ruin. Kaushalya, who has been weeping for days, is calmed. There are many quiet heroes in the Ramayana, and she is one of them.

## Why this still moves us
We are a country of families in which sons and daughters leave: for jobs, for studies, for marriages, for other countries. Many of us have been on one side of that doorway or the other. Sumitra's few lines say what is perhaps the hardest thing a parent can say: *go, and be well.* She did not use her love to hold him. She used it to send him.

There is also something in her advice for those who stay. She did not tell Lakshmana to be safe. She told him to serve, to treat Rama and Sita as family. She knew that what keeps a person steady in a hard place is not caution but *belonging*.

## A few lessons from Sumitra
- **Love can be a blessing for departure.** The best proof of it is that it does not hold on.
- **Quiet people can be the strongest.** Sumitra rarely speaks, and when she does, it matters.
- **Give people a way of seeing their situation.** She gave Lakshmana a way to see the forest as home.
- **Comfort others when you are also in pain.** She helped Kaushalya when she herself had lost her son's company.

## Reading it with modern eyes
It would be too easy to read this scene only as a lesson in sacrifice. Many readers today notice that the Ramayana asks its women to bear loss in silence, and they wonder what Sumitra felt that the poem does not say. That is a fair question. The poem gives her few words, and what it keeps from us may also be part of what it is telling us.

What we can say is this: the scene shows a mother in command of herself at the worst moment, and gives her son something he could carry into fourteen years in a forest. It is a gift of *meaning*, not a command. If you have ever had to say goodbye to a child, or been the child saying it, those four lines may carry more than their weight.
