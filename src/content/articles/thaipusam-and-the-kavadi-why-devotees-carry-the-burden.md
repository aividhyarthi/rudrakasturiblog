Imagine a long road before dawn, filled with people walking slowly. Some carry brass pots of milk on their heads. Some carry a bright arch of peacock feathers on their shoulders. The drums keep time, and the air is full of one word, "Vel, Vel." That is Thaipusam, and what it is asking of each walker is a question worth pausing over: what are you carrying, and for whom?

## When is Thaipusam?

The festival belongs to the Tamil month of Thai, which falls around January and February. Thaipusam is the day when the Pusam star, known in Sanskrit as Pushya, rules the sky, usually close to the full moon. The exact date changes yearly, so please look at [our festivals page](/festivals/) for the current one.

## The vel from Parvati

Tradition holds that Parvati gave her son Murugan the vel, a spear of divine power, so that he could overcome the demon Surapadma. The vel is not only a weapon. Many devotees see it as knowledge, sharp and bright, that cuts through ignorance. This is why Thaipusam is also called the day of the vel, and why the vel itself is worshipped in many Murugan temples. For the wider story of Murugan, see [Murugan, Kartikeya, Subramanya: one god, many names](/murugan-kartikeya-subramanya-one-god-many-names/).

## Idumban and the two hills

The kavadi has its own story. Tradition tells it like this. The sage Agastya asked his disciple Idumban to bring two hills, Sivagiri and Saktigiri, to the south. Idumban tied them on either end of a pole and set out, carrying them on his shoulders.

On the way, near the place now known as Palani, he put the load down to rest. When he tried to lift it, he found the hills would not move. A young boy stood on top of one hill. Idumban, angry, challenged him, and was defeated. In the end he recognised the boy as Murugan and asked to stay as his devoted servant. Murugan granted that anyone who came to him carrying a burden, as Idumban had, would be blessed.

Details differ between tellers, and some versions speak of other sages and other hills. But the core idea is consistent: the kavadi is a pole with a load at each end, and it is carried as an offering and a vow.

## What is a kavadi?

The word kavadi is Tamil and refers to the carrying pole itself. Over time it has come to mean any burden carried to Murugan. Some common forms:

- Paal kavadi (milk kavadi). Pots of milk are carried, then poured over the deity in abhishekam.
- Pushpa kavadi. A kavadi decorated with flowers.
- Alagu kavadi. A heavy, ornate kavadi, often with peacock feathers and a vel.
- Other regional forms, some with fruits, sugar, or small pots of different offerings.

Each one means the same thing: I bring what I have, and I carry it myself.

## Fasting and vows

In many families, those who take a kavadi observe a period of discipline before the day. This usually includes simple vegetarian food, a bath at dawn, and a plain life. The length varies, and in many places it is forty-eight days, but practices differ by temple and community, and it is best to ask the priest or elders at the shrine you will visit.

A vow is often made privately: for a recovery, for a child, for gratitude. Whether such prayers are answered is not something anyone can promise. The vow is the devotee's own promise, and the walk is the keeping of it.

## Palani and Batu Caves

Palani, one of the six great abodes of Murugan, is the most famous place for kavadi in Tamil Nadu. You can read about it in [Arupadai Veedu, the six abodes of Murugan](/arupadai-veedu-the-six-abodes-of-murugan/). Many pilgrims walk from their own towns, some over several days.

Outside India, Batu Caves near Kuala Lumpur in Malaysia holds one of the largest Thaipusam celebrations in the world, with a tall statue of Murugan standing at the foot of the steps and hundreds of thousands of people taking part. It shows how deeply Tamil devotion has travelled.

## What about the piercings?

Some devotees, in states of deep vow, take on severe practices such as piercing the skin with hooks or skewers. These are done by a small number, usually under guidance in certain communities. We will not describe or encourage them, and we would not want anyone to feel that devotion requires pain. Murugan is not pleased by harm to the body. Many teachers remind us that the body is a gift to be respected.

## What the burden means

Why a burden at all? Think of how often we carry things that no one sees: a debt, a sick parent, a fear for a child. The kavadi makes that hidden weight visible and lays it at the feet of the god. The walker does not pretend the load is light. He or she carries it, step by step, and arrives. Many devotees say the lesson is simply that grace meets effort halfway. The tradition does not say the burden vanishes, only that it is shared.

There is also humility in it. Idumban was strong enough to carry two hills and still had to bow to a child. Strength, in this story, ends in surrender.

## The simple form is enough

If you are wondering whether you can take part, here is the plain answer. Yes.

1. Bathe, wear clean clothes, and keep the day simple.
2. Fill a small pot with milk. Decorate it with a flower or turmeric if you like.
3. Carry it to the temple with a quiet mind. Say "Vel Vel, Vetri Vel" or just Murugan's name.
4. Hand it to the priest for abhishekam.

That is a kavadi, in its heart. It is not smaller than the grand ones.
