**Source:** Valmiki Ramayana, Yuddha Kanda (chapter 22) for the bridge. The squirrel is from folk and later tradition, not Valmiki.

Ask almost anyone in South India why the squirrel has three stripes on its back, and they will tell you the same story. It is told at bedtime, in schools, in discourses. It is also, strictly speaking, not in the Ramayana of Valmiki. That does not make it less worth telling. It makes it more interesting.

## What Valmiki actually says
When Rama's army reached the shore, the sea stood between them and Lanka. Rama waited for three days and nights on a bed of grass, praying to the ocean god for a passage. When the god did not appear, Rama prepared to dry up the sea with his arrows, and the ocean came to him with advice: let a bridge be built, and the vanara Nala, a son of Vishwakarma, the divine architect, will build it (Yuddha Kanda 22).

So Nala supervised the work. The vanaras tore up trees and mountains and threw them in. According to the text, the bridge was built over five days: fourteen yojanas on the first day, twenty on the second, twenty-one, twenty-two, and twenty-three on the fifth. A hundred yojanas of bridge, and the vanaras are said to have shouted with joy at each stage.

There is no squirrel in Valmiki's account.

## The squirrel story
In the folk tradition, a tiny squirrel sees the great vanaras carrying huge boulders. It wants to help. It rolls in the sand on the shore and then shakes the sand off onto the bridge, again and again. The vanaras laugh, or tell it to move out of the way. One almost steps on it.

Rama sees it. He picks the squirrel up and strokes its back with three fingers, saying that its effort is as valuable as the largest rock. The marks of his fingers remain on the squirrel's back, as three stripes, to this day.

## Why do we keep telling it?
The story has no authority as scripture, and it needs none. It carries a feeling that the Ramayana's grand scale can miss: that the smallest, most sincere effort counts. In Telugu there is a phrase for this, *udata bhakti*, the squirrel's devotion. It is used for someone who does whatever little they can, with all their heart.

A story like this may have been added by countless tellers over centuries, because people who have no great power wanted to feel included in the epic. That, in itself, is a very beautiful thing about how the Ramayana has lived in India: it keeps growing space for people like us.

## How to tell the difference
As a reader, it is useful to be able to separate three layers:
- **Valmiki:** the oldest, and the main source, in Sanskrit.
- **Later regional Ramayanas:** Kamban in Tamil, Ranganatha and Molla in Telugu, Tulsidas in Awadhi, each of which adds scenes.
- **Folk tradition:** stories like the squirrel that live in songs and storytelling.

None is "wrong". But it helps to know which one you are reading, and to be honest about it when you retell it. That is the approach we try to follow on this site.

## The little animal behind the story
The squirrel in the tale is the Indian palm squirrel, a common creature in gardens and temple compounds across the South. It has pale stripes running along its back, usually three, which makes the story easy to retell: a child only has to look at the animal to see the evidence. That is one reason the story has lasted. It sits in the everyday world, not in a far-off forest.

## The bridge in the real world
Today, the chain of shoals between Pamban island near Rameswaram and the island of Mannar off Sri Lanka is known as Rama Setu, or Adam's Bridge on many maps. People in India regard it with deep reverence, and the ideas people hold about how it formed differ. We will not argue that here. What can be said simply is that Rameswaram and Dhanushkodi are places where the bridge story is remembered with great feeling. ## How to tell the story to a child
- Begin with the child's own experience: "Have you ever wanted to help, but felt too small?"
- Tell the squirrel's part gently, with the sand and the shaking.
- Pause where Rama picks up the squirrel. Let the child imagine how it felt.
- Say honestly that this is a story people have told for a very long time, and that it is not in Valmiki's text. Children handle that well.
- Ask at the end: "What is a small thing you can do today?"

## Questions people ask
**Is the squirrel story in any written Ramayana?**
It appears in later retellings and folk tradition. Some regional versions give it more detail than others. Valmiki himself does not mention it.

**Why do so many people believe it?**
Because it feels true to the heart. Even without a text, the idea that a sincere small effort counts is one the whole tradition agrees with.

**Does it mean small efforts always change the outcome?**
Not necessarily. The story's claim is gentler: the effort is seen and valued. The squirrel did not build the bridge. It took part.

## What it teaches
- **Do what you can.** The squirrel did not wait to be a vanara.
- **A small effort, offered sincerely, is seen.**
- **Do not mock the small.** The vanaras laughed, and the story corrects them.
