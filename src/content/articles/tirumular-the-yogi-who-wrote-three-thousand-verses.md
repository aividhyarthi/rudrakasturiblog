*తిరుమూలర్ తమిళ శైవ సిద్ధుడు; ఆయన రాసిన తిరుమంత్రంలో తొమ్మిది తంత్రాలలో సుమారు మూడు వేల పద్యాలు ఉన్నాయి. చనిపోయిన గోపాలకుని దేహంలోకి ప్రవేశించి ఆయన దీన్ని రాశాడని సంప్రదాయం; ప్రేమే శివుడనే మాట ఇందులో ప్రసిద్ధం.*

Have you ever heard someone say "love is God" and wondered whether it was just a pleasant thing to say at a wedding? A Tamil yogi said it some fifteen centuries ago, or perhaps more recently, and then wrote three thousand verses to explain what he meant.

## Who was Tirumular?

Tirumular (திருமூலர்) is named in the *Periya Puranam* as one of the 63 Nayanmars, and in the Siddha tradition as one of the great teachers. We know little about the man himself. Dates given by scholars range widely, from about the fifth to the ninth century, so it is wise to leave the date open.

What survives is the *Tirumantiram* (திருமந்திரம்), "the sacred mantra", which forms the tenth book of the Tirumurai, the Tamil Shaiva canon.

## The cowherd Mulan

The traditional account, found in the Periya Puranam, goes like this. A yogi from Kailash, said to be a disciple of Nandi, was travelling south to meet the sage Agastya at the Podigai hills. On the way, near Tiruvavaduturai on the Kaveri, he saw a herd of cows crying around the body of their keeper, a cowherd called Mulan. Moved by pity, the yogi decided to bring the cowherd back to life, so that the cows might have their master.

He left his own body hidden in a hollow, entered Mulan's body, and Mulan rose. The cows were happy. But when the yogi returned to look for his own body, it was gone. Tradition says Shiva had hidden it, so that the yogi might remain as Mulan and give the world the *Tirumantiram*. He then sat under a peepul tree in Tiruvavaduturai, and each year, goes the story, he composed one verse over three thousand years.

Whether we treat this as history, as symbol of a teacher taking on the human condition out of compassion, or as both, is for each reader to decide. It is a tradition and no more.

## Nine tantras

The *Tirumantiram* has nine sections, called *tantiram* (tantras), each of different length. They cover:

- The nature of the self, the body and breath
- Yoga practices, including the eight limbs
- The Shaiva doctrine of Pati (the Lord), Pasu (the soul) and Pasam (the bond)
- Worship, mantra and the mystery of the letters
- Ethics, charity and the company of the good

It is a technical text in places, and it can be hard to read. Yet scattered through it are lines of remarkable plainness.

## Love as God

In the *Tirumantiram* there is a line that speaks of love and Shiva as one: the foolish say that love and God are different, but no one knows that love itself is God. In Tamil it is often quoted as *Anbe Sivam*. I give it only as a line in the Tirumantiram and leave the verse number to the printed editions.

Think about what it is saying. It is not asking you to feel warm toward everyone. It says that the force pulling you toward a child, a friend, an old teacher, a poor stranger at the door, is the same force as the one that turns you toward God. If so, then ignoring a person in need is not a small lapse beside worship; it is a kind of turning away.

## A voice for ordinary life

The *Tirumantiram* also says that the body is a temple, and that one should not despise it, since it is the house in which the Lord lives. It speaks against empty quarrels between religions and castes, and it values the guru and the company of the wise. A famous verse in Tamil tradition speaks of one family of humankind and one God; the exact lines differ, so seek a reliable edition.

The Siddhas were a wide stream of yogis in Tamil land who valued experience over rule. If you want to see how the Shaiva idea of the formless in form is visualised, read [what the Shiva lingam means](/shiva-lingam-what-it-means-and-how-to-worship-it/).

## How to read it

You can start with a short selection, not the whole book. Choose a translation with notes, read five verses a day, and stop when a line unsettles you. Ask what it wants you to do today, not in some far future.

## The Siddha way

The Siddhas of Tamil country, a line that includes Tirumular, did not found a school with a building and a seal. They moved among villages, wrote in plain Tamil, and mixed yoga, medicine and worship. Tradition lists eighteen principal Siddhas, though the list differs between sources. For many, Tirumular stands at the head because his work gives the Siddha path a Shaiva shape.

Their common message is that the divine can be known in this body, in this life, through steady practice, kindness and breath. That does not mean quick results; the tradition itself speaks of long years. ## Where this comes from

The story of Mulan comes from the *Periya Puranam* by Sekkizhar, and later hagiographies, and is told differently in Siddha lore. The Tirumantiram is the primary text, but scholars note that its nine tantras may have grown over time and may include later additions. The count of "three thousand" refers to the traditional total.
