**Source:** Local legend of the Tatayyagunta Gangamma temple, Tirupati, and the Gangamma Jatara. The details differ between tellers.

Most people who come to Tirupati go straight to the hill. But down in the town, in the older streets, there is a goddess who was there before the crowds, and who is still visited by those who live there.

## The goddess of the town
Gangamma, or Tatayyagunta Gangamma, is regarded as the *grama devata*, the guardian goddess, of Tirupati. In the oral tradition, she is the sister of Lord Venkateswara. Families visit her before a wedding, before a journey, and when a child is sick.

## The legend
According to the local story, many centuries ago the area around Tirupati was ruled by a petty chieftain, the Palegadu, a poligar. He was cruel and lustful, and any woman who caught his eye was taken. The people had no relief.

Gangamma came to the town as a young woman. The Palegadu saw her and set out to capture her. She escaped, and for days she hid, changing her disguises to avoid him: a bairagi, an ascetic; a Matangi; and others. Each time, the Palegadu hunted her down, and each time she slipped away.

At last she stopped running. She took on her terrible form, called him out, and slew him. The town was freed.

## The jatara
Every year, usually in May, Tirupati holds Gangamma Jatara, a week of celebration. The central feature is the *vesham*, the taking of disguises. Men dress as women, as bandits, as ascetics, as Matangi, as bairagis, in many different disguises. They smear themselves with turmeric and vermilion, wear garlands of neem and dance through the streets. On the last day, the goddess is taken in a procession and the town is cleaned and decorated.

The drama is a *remembering*. Every disguise recalls her flight, and the final day recalls her victory.

## Other interpretations
Some historians suggest that the festival may have developed from earlier agrarian rites, and that the Palegadu is a reflection of the local struggles of the Vijayanagara period. Such readings are plausible, and do not displace the devotion of the people.

## A reminder about the vesham
The disguises are acts of worship. Visitors to the jatara should be respectful: ask before photographing, and do not treat the vesham as a spectacle.

## What the story says
- **Protection from the vulnerable.** The village goddess is a woman who was herself hunted.
- **Cleverness is also strength.** She did not fight at first. She disguised herself, and waited.
- **The goddess is close.** She is the sister, not a distant queen.
- **Festivals keep memory.** The disguises are a way of keeping a story alive.

## Visiting
The temple is in the Tatayyagunta area of Tirupati, a short ride from the railway station. It is open daily, and especially busy on Fridays and during the jatara.
