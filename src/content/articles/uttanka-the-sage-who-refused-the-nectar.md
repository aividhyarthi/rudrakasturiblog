**Source:** Mahabharata, Ashvamedhika Parva (the section after the war, in the Anugita context).

After the war, when everyone else was grieving, one sage was angry. He had a very simple charge: *you could have stopped it.*

## The sage and the charioteer
Uttanka was a sage known for his devoted service to his teacher. After the battle of Kurukshetra, Krishna was returning to Dwaraka when he met Uttanka on the road. Uttanka asked whether the Kurus were well. Krishna told him that the Kurus had all been killed.

Uttanka burned with anger. The text says that he told Krishna that he, who had the power to stop it, had let his own kinsmen be destroyed, and that he would curse him for it.

## Krishna's reply
Krishna did not argue. He told Uttanka, in the plainest way, that he had tried everything, and that he was willing to explain. He asked him not to curse in haste, since Uttanka's own austerities were too precious to waste. Then, when Uttanka asked, he showed him his *vishvarupa*, the universal form.

Uttanka was overwhelmed, and his anger vanished. He asked for forgiveness and Krishna offered him a boon. Uttanka asked for one thing: that in the desert, where he lived, water might come to him whenever he thought of it.

## The thirsty sage
Krishna granted it. Later, while walking in a dry land, Uttanka became thirsty, and he thought of the boon. In front of him appeared a man, a hunter of a low caste, naked and filthy and surrounded by dogs, who offered him water in a way Uttanka found repulsive.

Uttanka refused. He said he would not take water from such a person, and walked on. The man vanished.

Uttanka was dismayed. He realised, a moment later, that it had been no ordinary hunter. The text says that the god Indra had come, carrying *amrita*, the nectar of immortality, and had offered it in a form Uttanka could not accept. Indra had wished to give Uttanka immortality, as Krishna's boon had asked. Uttanka had refused it because he could not see beyond the form.

## What it leaves us with
Krishna then told him that the water could still come to him. When Uttanka next thought of it, clouds gathered over the desert and poured down rain. These are called, in the text, *Uttanka's clouds*, and the story says that they bring water to the desert of Marwar.

## What it teaches
- **Anger at God is not forbidden.** Uttanka was angry, and Krishna listened.
- **Seeing more of the truth often melts the anger.** The vishvarupa did not answer his charge, it changed his vantage point.
- **A gift may arrive in an unfamiliar form.** The nectar came in a form he judged by appearances and turned away.
- **Prejudice can make us refuse our own blessings.** The story does not hide the discomfort of his refusal.

## A note
This is one of the Mahabharata's less told stories, and the text calls the dogs and the hunter by names that show the caste prejudice of the time. Readers today often find in it a warning rather than a model: the sage's refusal is the mistake.

## A closer look at the vishvarupa
The *vishvarupa* is the universal form of Krishna, shown to Arjuna on the battlefield in the Gita (chapter 11) and, in this story, to Uttanka. In both cases the person who sees it is not the same afterwards.

Look at what it does in each case. Arjuna is shaking with doubt, and the vision gives him a way to go on. Uttanka is shaking with anger, and the vision dissolves it. The form does not give a neat argument. It gives a wider view, and the question "why did you let this happen?" looks different from there.

You may notice something in your own life. When you are furious with someone, a longer view, perhaps a night's sleep, or a talk with an older friend, often does what Uttanka's vision did. It does not erase what happened, but it changes the size of it in your mind.

## Reading it with modern eyes
This story asks us to be honest about two things.

**Our anger.** Uttanka does not hide it. Many people feel they must not be angry with God or fate. The story suggests that it is better to say the feeling aloud and to listen for a reply than to bury it.

**Our blind spots.** Uttanka's refusal of the hunter shows how habit and prejudice can sit inside a person who is otherwise wise and disciplined. Austerity does not automatically cure that. It takes a second look, and sometimes a painful one.

A few questions you can ask yourself:
- Who or what do I turn away from because of how it looks?
- Have I ever refused help because it arrived in a form I did not like?
- Am I willing to stay and listen when I am angry with someone I trust?

## Questions people ask
**Is this story in the Bhagavad Gita?**
No. It is told in the Mahabharata, in the Ashvamedhika Parva, which comes after the war. The Gita is in the Bhishma Parva.

**Did Krishna really not stop the war?**
In the epic, he does try, for instance by going to the Kauravas as a messenger of peace before the fighting. That is why the story is read as a conversation about responsibility and not as a simple answer.

**Where do Uttanka's clouds appear?**
The story places them in the dry lands of the west. It is a memory of a desert that sometimes receives rain.
