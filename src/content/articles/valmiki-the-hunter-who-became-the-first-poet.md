Many of us learned as children that Valmiki was a fearsome robber who said Rama's name backwards and became a sage. It is a lovely story, and we still repeat it. But have you ever asked where it comes from, and whether Valmiki says it himself? Let us look carefully, because the truth is more interesting than the simple version.

## The hunter-robber story, and its source

The popular tale goes like this. A man named Ratnakara lived by waylaying travellers. One day he met the seven sages, who asked whether his family would share the sin of his robberies. He went home and asked them. Each one refused. Shaken, he returned to the sages, who taught him to repeat "Mara, Mara", which turned into "Rama, Rama". He sat so long in meditation that an anthill (valmika) grew over him. When he rose, he was Valmiki, "born of the anthill".

Be clear about the source. The story belongs to later Puranic and retelling traditions, and in many folk tellings; some trace it to the Skanda Purana or the Adhyatma Ramayana, but we cannot be fully sure of the exact chapter. Valmiki's own Ramayana does not tell it. Tellings differ in the name, and in the details.

The tale is still worth keeping, for what it says: no past is too heavy for a person to be changed by it. But it is a tradition about Valmiki, not a statement by him.

## What the Ramayana itself says

The Bala Kanda begins with Valmiki asking the wandering sage Narada whether there is a man alive who is brave, truthful, grateful, steady in his vows, and kind to all creatures. Narada answers with the story of Rama in short. (See [Narada, the sage who carries news](/narada-the-sage-who-carries-news-and-trouble/) for the other side of this meeting.)

Then Valmiki goes to bathe at the Tamasa river. He sees a pair of krauncha birds, in love, and a hunter shoots the male. The female cries. Valmiki's heart breaks, as the text puts it, and he curses the hunter in words that came to him in a measured, singing rhythm. In the verse "ma nishada", he says, in effect, that the hunter will not have peace for many years, because he killed one of a pair lost in love.

Then he wonders at what he has done. Grief had become verse. He called it shloka, from shoka, sorrow. Brahma appears, tells him that this came by Sarasvati's will, and asks him to sing Rama's story. Valmiki sits in meditation, sees the whole story as if in his palm, and writes.

Is it not unusual that the first great poem of the tradition begins in compassion for a bird, and not with a king or a god?

## Why a poem, and not a sermon

Notice how Valmiki's own mind works in the story. He does not set out to compose. He is on his way to bathe, in the ordinary middle of a day. The shloka comes before the plan. Only afterwards does he decide to give it a great theme.

Our teachers often say that is how real learning goes. First something touches you, then you find the words. A poet who begins from grief for a bird is already halfway to writing about a king who weeps for his wife in the forest, and a woman who walks into fire and out of it. The Ramayana is full of tears because its first line was one.

## What Valmiki says of himself

In the Uttara Kanda, as the tradition reads it, Valmiki tells Sita that he is the tenth son of Pracheta, and that he has not spoken an untruth. He says that he has done tapas for thousands of years. Scholars debate the dating and authorship of the Uttara Kanda, and some think it is a later addition. So we should hold this lightly.

## The ashram and Sita

When Rama sends Sita to the forest, it is Valmiki's ashram near the Ganga that gives her shelter. Lakshmana leaves her at the bank in tears, and Valmiki, who has seen her with his inner eye, receives her and says she is like a daughter to him. There Lava and Kusha are born. He teaches them the Ramayana, and tradition says they sang it in Rama's court at the Ashvamedha sacrifice, without knowing he was their father.

Many families in the Telugu and Tamil lands know Sita as Valmiki's guest in these years, and some hold that the ashram's place is still shown by the Ganga. Where exactly it was, no one can say for sure. Think of Valmiki's position. The poet knows the whole story. He is also the host of its most wounded character. He does not take sides in anger. He gives refuge, and he gives the story back to its own family.

## Where this comes from

Narada's meeting, the krauncha birds and Brahma's blessing are in the Bala Kanda of the Valmiki Ramayana. Sita's stay is in the Uttara Kanda. The Ratnakara tale is from later Puranic and folk tradition and does not appear in Valmiki. Kamba Ramayanam and the Telugu Ranganatha Ramayanam follow the epic in outline, and Tulsidas's Ramcharitmanas has its own devotional reading of Valmiki.
