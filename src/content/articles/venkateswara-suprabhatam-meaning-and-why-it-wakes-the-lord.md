Before the sky over the Seshachalam hills has a hint of light, a bell rings at Tirumala. A priest steps in front of the sanctum and the old Sanskrit verses begin, soft at first. If you have ever heard the Suprabhatam on a radio or on someone's phone early in the morning, you know the feeling: the house feels briefly quieter, and the day feels as if it has been opened with care.

## Who composed it?

The Venkateswara Suprabhatam (వేంకటేశ్వర సుప్రభాతం in Telugu, வேங்கடேச சுப்ரபாதம் in Tamil) is attributed to Prativadi Bhayankaram Annangaracharya. Tradition holds that he lived in the 15th century and was a disciple of Manavala Mamuni, the Sri Vaishnava teacher. Exact dates and the details of his life are not firmly known, and different accounts offer different stories. What is firm is the hymn itself, which has been sung at Tirumala for generations.

## The first verse

It opens with "Kausalya supraja Rama purva sandhya pravartate", a line that surprises many first-time listeners. It speaks to Rama, the good son of Kausalya, and says that the morning twilight has begun. Why Rama, when the temple is of Venkateswara? Tradition holds that the Lord of the seven hills is Vishnu, and Rama is Vishnu too. The verse is borrowed in spirit from the Valmiki Ramayana, where the sage Vishvamitra wakes the young Rama with these words. Here, the devotee wakes the Lord the same way, and says: arise, the day's work awaits.

## The four parts

The hymn is usually sung in four sections:

- **Suprabhatam.** The waking itself. The Lord is called to rise, the gods and sages are waiting at the door, the sun is climbing.
- **Stotram.** Praise of the Lord of Tirumala, His beauty and His compassion, and of the sacred hill.
- **Prapatti.** Surrender. Here the devotee gives up the sense of "I will manage" and takes refuge at His feet.
- **Mangalasasanam.** A closing set of blessings, wishing all good to the Lord Himself, as one wishes well for a beloved elder.

Listen to the movement across them. It goes from calling out, to praising, to laying down one's burden, to blessing. That is how many people pray in their own way.

## Why wake a god?

The question is fair, since the Lord does not sleep. The answer in temple tradition is that the deity is treated as a living guest, a king in His own house. We do not wake Him because He needs it, but because we need to serve. A child who does not have to be told what to offer is no longer a child. The Suprabhatam is the first service of the day, and it teaches a posture: begin by giving, not asking.

There is another thought in it too. We wake a loved one gently, not in a hurry. The ritual softens the first minutes of our day, and that is something we can take home. Many families play the Suprabhatam at their own puja time, and sit for a moment before the day takes hold.

## How it is sung at Tirumala

The sevas at Tirumala begin before dawn, and the Suprabhatam seva is the first. It is sung in the early hours, with the sanctum doors opened afterwards for the day's worship. Timings and rules for devotees who wish to attend are set by the temple and change from time to time, so check with the official sources before you plan to go. ## How the world came to know it

M.S. Subbulakshmi's recording of the Suprabhatam, made in the 1960s, is what carried the hymn to homes far from Tirumala. Her voice is the one most people think of when they hear the first line. For many families in the South, and for many beyond it, the morning is not complete without it. If you have an old record or cassette of it in a cupboard, you may well be holding a piece of family history.

## In the month of Margazhi

There is a pretty detail worth knowing. Tradition at Tirumala holds that during the month of Dhanurmasa (Margazhi in Tamil, roughly mid-December to mid-January), the Suprabhatam is replaced by the Tiruppavai of Andal, the thirty verses in which a young girl wakes her friends and calls them to the Lord. So the same idea, waking in love, is sung in two voices through the year. If your family knows the Tiruppavai from childhood, you already know the feeling.

## A word on the meaning of "supra-bhatam"

The word itself is simple. "Su" means good, and "prabhata" is the dawn. A good morning, then, is exactly what the hymn offers, to the Lord and to whoever listens. It is also a small lesson in how to treat the people at home. Say a kind word before the first demand of the day. Many grandmothers did this without ever naming it, and the Suprabhatam is the temple's version of the same habit.

## How to listen at home

You do not need to follow every word. A simple way: light the lamp, sit before a picture of Venkateswara, and let the recording play. If you want to learn, find a printed text with meaning in Telugu or Tamil, and read along for one section each week. The pronunciation will find you.
