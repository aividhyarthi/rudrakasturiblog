**Source:** Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva (Krishna's embassy to Hastinapura). The banana-peel tale is from folk tradition.

Imagine you are Krishna. You have come to the capital of your enemies, with an offer of peace. The king invites you to a feast, with the finest food in the land. You say no, and walk to the house of a servant's brother.

## An envoy in Hastinapura
Before the war, Krishna went to Hastinapura on a last mission of peace. He was welcomed with great ceremony. Duryodhana invited him to dine in the palace. Krishna declined.

He explained, in the text's words, that food is to be accepted when offered with affection, or when a person is in need. Neither was true here. Duryodhana's invitation was not meant out of love, and Krishna was not in need. So he did not eat in the palace.

Instead he went to the house of Vidura.

## Who was Vidura?
Vidura was the half-brother of Dhritarashtra and Pandu, born to a maidservant and Vyasa. Because of his birth, he was never in line for the throne, but he was known throughout the epic as the wisest and most honest person at court. Tradition says he was an incarnation of Dharma, who had been cursed by the sage Mandavya to be born as a human.

Vidura had warned Dhritarashtra again and again, from the dice game to the eve of the war, that he was heading toward ruin. The old king listened, nodded, and did not act. Vidura kept speaking anyway.

## The meal
In the epic, Krishna went to Vidura's house and was received with warmth. Vidura, who had little, offered what he had. Krishna told him in private that he had come to him as to a friend, and the two spoke about the coming war. Kunti, the Pandavas' mother, was also at Vidura's house.

Krishna's choice is not a minor gesture. It is a public statement: *honesty and love have more weight with me than a king's table.*

## The folk story
In the villages of North and South India, another version is told. Vidura was not at home when Krishna arrived, and his wife, overcome with joy, began to feed Krishna bananas, forgetting herself so completely that she threw away the fruit and offered the peels. Krishna ate them with delight. When Vidura came home and saw what was happening, he was horrified and corrected her, and Krishna said that the peels had tasted sweeter than the fruit would have.

This is not in the Mahabharata. It is a devotional tale, and it carries a point that the epic also makes: *what matters is the love in the offering.* It is useful to know the difference, and to enjoy both.

## What it teaches
- **Be careful whom you eat with.** The epic treats hospitality as a moral act.
- **Honesty has a quiet reward.** Vidura was never given the throne, but he was given Krishna's visit.
- **Love is the real offering.** The same lesson appears in the Gita: *a leaf, a flower, a fruit, or water offered with love, I accept* (9.26).
- **A true counsellor keeps speaking even when no one listens.**

## A closer look at Vidura's counsel
Vidura is not only a good host in this story. He is the speaker of a famous body of advice, the *Vidura Niti*, which appears in the Udyoga Parva, the same book as Krishna's mission. In it he talks to the troubled Dhritarashtra at night about how a wise person lives: how to speak, whom to trust, and why anger and greed ruin kings.

The scene is worth imagining. A king cannot sleep, so he calls the one man he knows will not flatter him. That tells you something about Dhritarashtra as well. He knew what honesty sounded like, and he still did not act on it.

## What Krishna did next
After the visit to Vidura, the embassy went on. In the epic's account Krishna put the Pandavas' case in the Kuru court, asked for a modest settlement, and was refused. Duryodhana even tried to seize him. The text describes Krishna then showing a vast form that frightened the court into silence. We are not told that he returned to the palace for a meal.

This helps you see why the choice of Vidura's house mattered. It was not a small act of manners. It showed the court, before the war began, where Krishna's trust lay.

## Questions people ask
**Did Vidura take part in the war?**
He did not fight. He is remembered as a counsellor who tried to prevent the war and then stood apart from it.

**Is the banana-peel story wrong, then?**
It is not in the epic, but it is not meant as history. Folk tradition uses it to make a gentle point about love in giving. You can enjoy it as a story without mistaking it for the text.

**What should I take from this for my own home?**
Think of how you receive guests. A plain meal given warmly can mean far more than an elaborate one given for show.

## At your own table
Many families have a saying that the guest is a god. You can practise it in small ways: greeting a visitor at the door, offering water first, and putting your phone away while they eat.

Notice also who you invite. It is easy to feed the people who can do something for us. The story nudges us to also remember the old neighbour, the teacher, the helper who rarely sits at anyone's table.
