*ధృతరాష్ట్రుడు రాత్రి నిద్రపట్టక విదురుడిని పిలిపించాడు, ఆ రాత్రి విదురుడు చెప్పిన నీతులే విదుర నీతి. ఆ మాటలన్నీ నిజమని రాజు ఒప్పుకున్నా, కొడుకు మీది మోహంతో పాటించలేకపోయాడు.*

Have you ever lain awake because you already knew the answer to a problem and did not want to act on it? That is the mood of this chapter. The war has not yet begun. Messages are going back and forth. And in his palace the old king Dhritarashtra turns on his bed and cannot sleep.

## The night the king sent for Vidura

Tradition holds that after hearing the reports of Sanjaya and the Pandava envoys, Dhritarashtra grew uneasy. He told the doorkeeper to bring Vidura (విదురుడు), his half-brother, the one man in the palace who did not flatter him. "Prajagara" means wakefulness, and the section takes its name from that night.

Vidura begins in a way you may not expect. Before any teaching, he describes who loses sleep. As the text has it, in substance: the one who faces a stronger rival, the one who has been robbed, the one ruled by desire, and the thief. Then he asks the king, gently, which of these he is. It is a physician's question, not a lecture.

## Who is wise, and who is a fool

Much of the Niti is a set of portraits. Paraphrasing, the wise person:

- is not carried away by anger or by joy;
- does not boast of what they know, nor despise others' learning;
- begins work only after thought, and finishes what is begun;
- keeps counsel and does not reveal plans before they are ripe.

The fool is the mirror image. He gives advice to those who did not ask, puts trust in the untrustworthy, quarrels without cause, and wishes for what is beyond his reach. A thread running through these verses is that a person who cannot bear to hear the truth because it is bitter has already chosen his fall. Flatterers are easy to find, Vidura says, but one who speaks what is pleasant to hear and also useful is rare, and so is the one who listens to him.

## Anger, greed and the six faults

Vidura speaks of anger more than once. Anger, he says, is the enemy inside the body, dearer to us than any outside foe because we feed it ourselves. Greed, likewise, is a thirst that grows with drinking. Elsewhere in the section he lists faults a person who wants to prosper should give up, usually given as sleep, drowsiness, fear, anger, laziness and delay. Versions of this list differ slightly in the manuscripts, so treat the exact six as the commonly cited form. If this theme speaks to you, read [what the Gita says about anger](/bhagavad-gita-on-anger-krishna-teaching/), where Krishna traces the same chain from desire to ruin.

## The verse about giving up one for the many

The line people quote most is about sacrifice. In substance, Vidura says that one may give up an individual for the sake of the family, a family for the village, a village for the country, and the whole earth for the sake of the Self. This verse is widely cited as coming from the Prajagara section; you should check the exact numbering in a critical edition before quoting a chapter number.

Notice what Vidura is saying to the king. The individual to be given up is Duryodhana. He is telling a father, as plainly as courtesy permits, that sparing the son will cost the family and the kingdom. Elsewhere in the epic Vidura is also said to have urged abandoning the child at birth, when the omens were bad. The king did not agree then either.

## Why the advice went unheard

Here is the question behind the chapter, and it is ours rather than the text's: why does a man who agrees still not act? Dhritarashtra answers Vidura more than once, in effect, by saying you are right, speak on. He wants the comfort of the truth without its price. Vidura does not soften it. He compares those who ignore good counsel to the one who drinks poison because it is sweet.

The Mahabharata itself offers the answer: attachment. Dhritarashtra's blindness was not only in the eyes. The epic keeps asking us whether we are any different when we know what is right at home or at work, and put it off.

You may also find it worth reading [how Vidura's own house became the place Krishna chose](/vidura-and-the-hospitality-krishna-chose/), which shows what happens when a person's conduct matches his counsel. And for the other voice that spoke to this king, the one who told it as it happened, see [Sanjaya](/sanjaya-the-man-who-saw-the-war-from-a-distance/).

## Where this comes from

The talk is in the Udyoga Parva of Vyasa's Mahabharata, in the section called Prajagara, which many editions place among the early chapters of the parva. Vidura's longer teaching continues into the following chapters (traditionally including the Sanatsujatiya, which is a separate teaching by Sanatsujata). Manuscripts and regional recensions vary in length and numbering, so verse numbers in popular booklets sometimes differ. Telugu and Tamil prose retellings often compress it into a short list of maxims; the original is a long and varied conversation.
