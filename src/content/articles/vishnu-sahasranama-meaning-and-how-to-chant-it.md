In many homes, there is a thin, worn booklet kept near the puja shelf, its spine soft from use. On Thursday mornings or on Ekadashi, someone opens it and begins, a little slowly, a little off-key. Maybe you have seen it, or maybe you have wondered if you are even allowed to try. You are.

## Where it comes from

The Vishnu Sahasranama sits in the Anushasana Parva of the Mahabharata. The war is over. Bhishma, the grandfather of the Kurus, lies on his bed of arrows, waiting for the sun to turn north. Yudhishthira, heavy with grief and the weight of kingship, comes to him with questions. Who is the one god? Who is to be worshipped? What is the best dharma, and what frees a person from the cycle of birth?

Bhishma's answer, with Krishna standing nearby, is the thousand names of Vishnu. Tradition holds that this is the best way to hold the whole question in one place: name the Lord by all that He is.

## The opening verses

The text begins with a few dhyana shlokas, verses of meditation. Many families start with "shuklambaradharam vishnum", a short verse that pictures Vishnu in white, moon-bright, four-armed and calm, and asks that obstacles be removed. Then come verses of remembrance and a salutation to Vyasa. After these, the main thousand names begin, and most printed books start with "Om vishvam vishnur vashatkaro". We also have a separate piece on [why the Sahasranama begins with Vishvam and how to read it](/vishnu-sahasranama-why-it-begins-with-vishvam-and-how-to-read-it/).

## What does a thousand names mean?

A thousand here is a way of saying more than you can count. No single name covers everything, so the text keeps naming, and each name opens a different window. One name speaks of what He does, another of what He is, another of how He feels to a devotee.

Some you may already know:

- **Vishnu**, from the root meaning to pervade. The one who is in everything and beyond it.
- **Keshava**, often taken to mean the one with beautiful hair, or the one who is the source of Brahma and Shiva. The meaning is read in more than one way.
- **Madhava**, of Madhu, or the husband of Lakshmi (Ma, the goddess, and dhava, lord).
- **Govinda**, the protector of cows, and by extension the one who can be found by the simple and the pure of heart.

Do not worry if you cannot hold all of these in your head. A name chanted with a little attention does its work slowly.

## Adi Shankara and the commentary

Tradition holds that Adi Shankara wrote a bhashya, a commentary, on the Sahasranama. In it each name is explained on the lines of Advaita, the school of non-duality. The Sri Vaishnava tradition has its own famous commentary by Parasara Bhattar, written in the spirit of Vishishtadvaita. Two schools, two ways of reading, and both love the same names. Read our note on [Shankara, Ramanuja and Madhva](/shankara-ramanuja-madhva-three-ways-to-see-god-and-the-soul/) if you want the bigger picture.

## How families chant it

- **When.** Many chant on Thursdays, or on Ekadashi, when the day is already given to Vishnu. Others chant every morning after the bath and lamp. You can read about [Ekadashi](/ekadashi/) and its days on our page.
- **Where.** Near the lamp, facing east if your home allows it. A photo or small idol of Vishnu, Venkateswara or Narayana is enough.
- **How long.** At an easy pace, about 25 to 40 minutes. Listening to a recording while following the text is a gentle way to begin.
- **How.** Sit comfortably. Light the lamp. Begin with the dhyana verses. Close with the phalashruti and a prostration.

## The phalashruti

At the end, the text speaks of the fruits, the phalashruti. It says that one who recites or even listens with faith is freed of fear and sorrow, and comes closer to the Lord. Treat this as the tradition's own promise of inner peace, not as a guarantee of results in the world. No chant replaces medical care or honest work, and no scripture asks you to think so.

## A small way to begin

If a thousand names feels like a mountain, climb a little each day. Some families read the dhyana verses and the first hundred names on weekdays and keep the full chanting for Thursday or Ekadashi. Others keep the whole text for a festival morning, with the family sitting together and the youngest holding the lamp.

You may also pick one name for the week. Say Govinda each time you wash your hands, or Keshava each time you leave the house. It sounds small, but a name remembered through ordinary hours is what the whole text is trying to teach. Over time the longer chant starts to feel less like a task and more like a visit to somebody you already know.

## What if I get it wrong?

You will, at first. Sanskrit has long compounds, and the names run together. Teachers in many families say the same thing: begin with a recording, repeat after it, and let an elder correct you when you meet one. A child who stumbles through a verse with love is not turned away at the door.
