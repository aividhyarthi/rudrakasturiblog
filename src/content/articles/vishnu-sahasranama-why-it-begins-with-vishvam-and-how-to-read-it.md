If you wake early in a South Indian home, there is a good chance you will hear it before you see the light: a recording of a voice, a woman's voice, calm and unhurried, chanting a long string of names. For many families, M. S. Subbulakshmi's recording of the Vishnu Sahasranama is the sound of the morning.

What is it, and what does it say?

## Where it begins
The Vishnu Sahasranama is in the *Anushasana Parva* of the Mahabharata. The war is over. Bhishma, the great elder, is lying on a bed of arrows, waiting for the Sun to turn north so that he can leave his body. The Pandavas come to him, and Yudhishthira, who has just become king, asks him questions. *Who is the one god in the world? What is the best path? What should we say to be freed from sorrow?*

Bhishma answers with a hymn of a thousand names of Vishnu. He says that whoever recites them with devotion will pass beyond sorrow. It is, in a sense, the king's last lesson from the grandfather.

## The first names
> **om viśvaṁ viṣṇur vaṣaṭkāro bhūta-bhavya-bhavat-prabhuḥ**
> **bhūta-kṛd bhūta-bhṛd bhāvo bhūtātmā bhūta-bhāvanaḥ**

The very first name is **Vishvam**, "the universe". Not a god somewhere above, but the whole of what is. The second, **Vishnu**, means *the one who pervades*. **Vashatkara** is the cry in the Vedic sacrifice, the one at whose call the offerings go to the gods. **Bhuta-bhavya-bhavat-prabhuh** is "the lord of what was, what will be and what is." Look at how it opens: it begins with *everything* and then names the one who is within it.

## What the thousand names do
The names are not random. They run through qualities (*Achyuta*, never-falling), forms (*Padmanabha*, with a lotus at his navel), actions (*Janardana*) and symbols (*Shankhabhrit*, holder of the conch). Many of the names are the names of the Gita, and of the avatars. Reading them is like looking at one very large jewel from a thousand angles.

## Who commented on it
Adi Shankara wrote a commentary (*bhashya*) on the Vishnu Sahasranama, explaining each name in the light of Advaita. Parashara Bhattar, in the Sri Vaishnava tradition, wrote another. That two such different traditions commented on it shows how widely it was loved.

## The shortcut
A popular verse says that Shiva told Parvati: *sri rama rama rameti rame rame manorame, sahasra-nama tat-tulyam rama-nama varanane.* "I delight in Rama, Rama, Rama. This name Rama equals the thousand names." The verse is from the Padma Purana. It is often said at the end of the Sahasranama for people who have little time. There is something sweet in the tradition's own permission to take the short way.

## How families read it
- **On Wednesdays and Ekadashi,** the days of Vishnu.
- **In the morning,** after a bath and before the day begins.
- **With a dhyana verse first,** often *shuklambara-dharam vishnum shashi-varnam chatur-bhujam, prasanna-vadanam dhyayet sarva-vighnopashantaye*, which means *I meditate on Vishnu in white garments, moon-coloured, four-armed, with a smiling face, to remove all obstacles.*
- **In a group,** a family sings it together, with a lead voice. This makes it easier to learn.
- **Read slowly.** It takes about 20 to 25 minutes.

## Starting small
You do not have to read all thousand. Begin with the first ten verses, or choose a section and read it every Wednesday. Over a year you will know it.

## Is it a ritual or a text?
Both. Some people use it as a daily practice. Others read it as poetry, noticing how many names are really descriptions of qualities we hope to develop: *Achyuta*, the one who does not fall; *Dhanvantari*, the physician; *Sukhada*, the giver of ease. It is difficult to read it and not think a little about one's own character.

## The setting: Bhishma's last days
The Anushasana Parva is set in the weeks after the war, while Bhishma waits for the Sun to turn north, which is considered an auspicious time to depart. He teaches Yudhishthira on many topics: the duties of kings, the nature of dharma, gifts, fasts and the path to liberation. The Sahasranama is the culmination. His death is remembered on *Bhishma Ashtami*, in the month of Magha, when many families offer water for him.

## The closing verses
The hymn ends with a *phala-shruti*, a list of the fruits of reciting it: freedom from fear, peace of mind, and the removal of sorrow. As with other such lists, read it as *encouragement*, not a contract. Many people who recite it say that the calm they feel while reciting stays with them for hours.
