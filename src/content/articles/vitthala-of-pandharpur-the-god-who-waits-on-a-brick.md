Most gods are shown seated on a throne or striding with a weapon. At Pandharpur, a dark figure stands on a brick, arms resting at his waist, as if he has just arrived and is wondering why you took so long. If you have ever kept a guest waiting at the door, or been kept waiting by someone you love, you may already understand something of this image.

## The story of Pundalik

The story, as tradition tells it, is about a devotee named Pundalik. In some versions he began as a careless son, who later turned toward his parents and served them with great care. One day the Lord came in the form of Krishna, with Rukmini, to meet Pundalik on the bank of the Bhima river. Pundalik was massaging his parents' feet and could not stop. Without turning, he threw a brick behind him and said, in effect, stand on this and wait.

Tradition holds that the Lord did not mind. He stood on the brick, hands on his hips, and waited until Pundalik had finished. Then, moved by the devotion, he agreed to stay there for ever. Details differ from one telling to another, so listen to the versions you hear in your own family.

## The image

The Pandharpur murti is dark, standing, two-armed, on a low brick, with hands resting on his hips. He wears a tall crown-like head covering. There is no weapon in the hands. He is simply there.

His names are many: **Vitthala**, **Vithoba** (affectionately, "Vithu"), **Panduranga**, **Pandurang**. Scholars debate the origin of the word Vitthala. Some link it to the brick (vit in Marathi), others to older roots, and the matter is not settled. Rukmini, his consort, has her own shrine in Pandharpur and is called **Rakhumai** or Rukmabai, "mother Rakhumai", with great tenderness.

## The river and the town

Pandharpur stands on the Bhima, which is called **Chandrabhaga** there because the river bends in the shape of a crescent. Devotees bathe before the darshan. The town is one of the most important pilgrimage places of Maharashtra, and many consider it the southern Kashi of the Marathi-speaking land.

## The warkari saints

What makes Vitthala truly alive is the line of saints who sang of him in Marathi, the common language of the people:

- **Dnyaneshwar** (Jnaneshwar), the young saint of Alandi, whose Dnyaneshwari is a Marathi commentary on the Gita. Tradition places him in the late 13th century.
- **Namdev**, a tailor by family, whose abhangs are loved across the north and south. Dates are uncertain.
- **Eknath**, of Paithan, a householder and writer.
- **Tukaram**, of Dehu, a grain merchant turned poet, whose abhangs are sung in homes everywhere. Tradition places him in the 17th century.

Their devotion drew people of every caste and station, and it is part of why the warkari path is called a people's tradition. You can read about the yearly walk in [Ashadhi Ekadashi and the Pandharpur wari](/ashadhi-ekadashi-and-the-pandharpur-wari/).

## Why the stance?

Teachers give a few answers. One: the hands on the hips show a Lord who is patient, and not anxious to leave. Two: he is standing on a brick, which is a humble thing, a builder's brick, to remind us that God can be met on the plainest ground. Three: he stands because he is waiting for you. You do not have to climb to him. He is already there, and he is not in a hurry.

Another thought: in most temple worship the devotee is told to purify, to learn, to do a long ritual. At Pandharpur, the story begins with a man who cannot stop serving his parents, and the Lord waits. The suggestion is that service to those near us is a form of worship, and that the Lord is not offended by being asked to wait.

## Vittala in Karnataka

South of the Maharashtra border, the name takes another form. Purandara Dasa, the saint-singer often called the father of Carnatic music, sang again and again of **Vittala**, and many of his songs close with the line "Purandara Vittala". The great Vittala temple at Hampi, with its stone chariot, belongs to the same name. Some accounts speak of a link between the Hampi shrine and the image of Pandharpur, and of a long exchange between the two lands. Historians and devotees tell this differently, so treat any neat single story with care.

What is clear is this: in Marathi abhang and in Kannada devaranama, a God who stands waiting is sung in the simplest language, to the simplest people.

## The call of the devotees

Walk near the temple and you will hear one cry above all: Vitthal, Vitthal, or Jai Jai Ram Krishna Hari. It is not a slogan. It is the sound of people who have walked a long way and are glad. In the queue for darshan, the rich and the poor stand in the same line, and when the moment comes, many hope to bow close to the image, though the arrangements at the temple change with the crowd and the season. Visitors are told to expect a crowd, especially on Ekadashi, and to follow the temple's own instructions.
