A bow lies in the royal hall of Mithila. Many kings have tried to lift it, but none can even move it. King Janaka has made a vow: the man who strings Lord Shiva's bow will marry his daughter, Sita. Then Rama steps forward.

This is the moment remembered during Vivah Panchami, the sacred day associated with the wedding of Lord Rama and Goddess Sita. The festival brings together one of the Ramayana's most loved episodes: the meeting of a prince raised in Ayodhya and a princess of Mithila, whose lives become part of a story told across India and beyond.

In 2026, the Hyderabad and New Delhi panchang references consulted for this guide list Vivah Panchami on **Monday, 14 December**. The Panchami tithi begins on the previous evening in the New Delhi calculation, so families should follow their local panchang for the exact observance.

## The story begins in Mithila

Rama and Lakshmana travel with Sage Vishvamitra. Their journey brings them to Mithila, where King Janaka rules. Sita, Janaka's daughter, is known for her grace, strength and wisdom. Janaka has decided that she will marry the person who can lift and string the great bow of Shiva.

Kings and warriors arrive, confident of their strength. One after another, they fail. Rama does not approach the bow with arrogance. At Vishvamitra's instruction, he steps forward, lifts it and draws it. The bow breaks with a sound that shakes the assembly.

Janaka recognises that Rama is the one worthy of Sita. Messengers travel to Ayodhya, and King Dasharatha arrives with the royal family. The wedding is celebrated with great joy.

## What does the wedding represent?

The wedding is more than a royal celebration. In devotional readings, Rama represents dharma, the responsibility to act rightly even when life becomes difficult. Sita represents strength, dignity and steadfastness. Their marriage is remembered as a partnership rooted in respect and shared duty.

The story also reminds us that the Ramayana is not only about the triumph of a hero. It is about the choices made by every person in the story: Janaka's promise, Rama's obedience, Sita's resolve and the responsibilities carried by both families.

## Why is Mithila important?

Mithila, in the present-day region spanning parts of Bihar and Nepal, is central to Sita's story. Janakpur in Nepal is especially associated with the wedding tradition and its Janaki Mandir. During Vivah Panchami, devotees gather for processions, ceremonial reenactments and celebrations of the divine wedding.

In many parts of North India, temples hold special pujas and recitations. In South Indian homes, the day may be remembered through Rama and Sita worship, reading the Sundara Kanda or the wedding episode, and singing devotional songs. The form of observance differs, but the story is shared.

## How to observe Vivah Panchami at home

A simple home observance can include:

1. Clean the puja space and place images of Rama, Sita, Lakshmana and Hanuman.
2. Light a lamp and offer flowers, fruit or a simple sweet.
3. Read the wedding episode from the Ramayana tradition followed by your family.
4. Sing a Rama bhajan or recite *Sri Rama Jayam*.
5. Share food or sweets with family, neighbours or someone who may need a meal.

Some devotees perform a symbolic wedding ceremony for Rama and Sita. This is a temple or family custom, not a requirement for everyone.

## Is Vivah Panchami a wedding day for everyone?

The festival celebrates the divine wedding, but customs around auspicious dates for human marriages differ. In some regions, Vivah Panchami is not treated as a preferred wedding muhurta, despite its association with Rama and Sita's marriage. Families should follow their own tradition and consult a qualified priest for personal ceremony dates.

## Questions people ask

**Is Vivah Panchami the actual wedding anniversary of Rama and Sita?**

It is traditionally observed as the day of their wedding in Mithila, as narrated in the Ramayana tradition.

**Is Janakpur the main place for Vivah Panchami?**

Janakpur is one of the most important centres of the celebration because of its association with Janaki, or Sita, and the Janaki Mandir.

**Which text tells the story of the wedding?**

The wedding is narrated in the Bala Kanda of the Valmiki Ramayana. Later Ramayana traditions also tell the episode in their own poetic and devotional styles.

The bow in Janaka's hall is remembered not simply because it breaks, but because the moment brings two lives and two royal families together. Vivah Panchami invites devotees to remember a marriage associated with dignity, commitment and dharma.

*The details of local celebrations and family rituals vary by region.*
