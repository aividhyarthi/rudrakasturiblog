Few phrases are used as often, and explained as rarely, as Sanatana Dharma. It is on posters, in speeches and in online arguments. So what does it actually mean?

## Breaking the words apart
*Sanatana* means eternal, ancient, without a beginning or end. *Dharma* is harder. It comes from a root meaning to hold or to support. Dharma is what upholds: the law of nature, the order of society, your duty and your character, all at once.

So Sanatana Dharma is, literally, the eternal order, the way of things that holds.

## Where it appears in the old texts
The phrase is not new. It is found in several places:
- In the **Mahabharata**, which repeatedly uses *sanatana dharma* for the unchanging duties that apply to everyone, such as truth, non-violence and compassion.
- In the **Bhagavad Gita**, for example in verse 1.40, where Arjuna worries that the ancient family dharmas will be lost if war destroys the family.
- In **Manusmriti**, which says that truthful and gentle conduct is the eternal dharma.
- In the Buddhist **Dhammapada**, where the Buddha says that hatred is not ended by hatred, and that *esa dhammo sanantano*, this is the eternal law.

So it was a shared moral idea long before it became a name.

## How it became a name
In the 19th century, as the word Hinduism came into English use, Indian reformers and teachers began to use Sanatana Dharma as the name for the tradition that lasts through changes of time and place. Organisations such as the Sanatana Dharma Sabha used the phrase, and Annie Besant's Central Hindu College in Varanasi taught from it.

Today, many Hindus use the two words for the same thing. Others point out that the phrase carries a particular meaning, the tradition understood as universal and timeless rather than as one belief system among others.

## What it asks of a person
Ask teachers across traditions and you will hear the same short list of things, often called the *samanya dharma*, the dharma common to all:
- Truthfulness
- Non-violence
- Not stealing
- Cleanliness
- Self-control
- Compassion and generosity

Beyond these, each person has their own duties by age, work and role, called *sva-dharma*. Krishna in the Gita says it is better to follow your own dharma imperfectly than another's perfectly.

## What it does not require
The phrase does not demand that you agree with a single book, a single god or a single practice. Hindu tradition has space for devotion to Shiva or Vishnu or the Goddess, for philosophy like [Advaita](/advaita-vedanta-in-plain-words/), and for people who follow the path of karma or knowledge or devotion. That variety is, for many of its followers, a feature and not a flaw.
