A tiny black dot on a baby's cheek. A lemon and seven green chillies hung at the shop door. A pumpkin smashed in front of a new house, with red kumkum. A grandmother waves salt and chillies around your head and throws them in the fire. You smile and let her. This is *drishti*, and it is very old.

## What it is
*Drishti* means a look or sight. In Hindi, it is *nazar*. The belief is that a glance of admiration, or more often envy, can bring harm to a person or a thing. A baby who is praised too much, a beautiful house, a good business: they are said to be at risk.

The belief is nearly universal. The evil eye is known in the Middle East, the Mediterranean, Latin America and South Asia, with local names and charms. In India, it is part of daily life.

## Common customs
- **Black dot (*drishti bottu*, *kajal tika*)** on a baby's cheek or forehead, so that the child will be "less perfect" and not attract the look.
- **Lemon and green chillies** hung at doors and on vehicles.
- **Pumpkin or ash gourd** (*kumbalakai*) smashed in front of a new building or at a ritual.
- **Waving salt, chillies, mustard or a coconut** around a person and burning or breaking it.
- **A scary face** on a house under construction.
- **A cloth or a black thread** tied on the wrist or ankle.

## Where it comes from
Nobody knows exactly. Historians see it as an expression of something very human: the fear that good luck can be spoiled by other people's envy. Some old texts mention protective rites against envy and illness in infants, and many families have passed customs down.

## What psychology says
There is no scientific evidence that a look can harm. But the customs may have value:
- **They are rituals of care.** A grandmother who waves salt around a baby is saying she loves the child.
- **They give a sense of control.** When something is out of our hands, doing something calms us.
- **They discourage showing off.** A culture that says "don't boast, it may attract the evil eye" is also a culture that teaches humility.

## When it becomes a problem
Most of these customs are harmless. Problems arise when:
- **Someone is blamed.** "The neighbour gave nazar to the baby" can damage friendships and families. Never accuse someone.
- **Medical help is delayed.** A fever, a persistent cry or poor feeding in a baby needs a doctor, not only a ritual. Please see a doctor first.
- **Money is spent.** Someone offers to remove nazar for a fee. Don't pay.
- **Fear takes over.** If you are living in constant dread of nazar, it can become anxiety. Talk to someone.
- **Something unsafe is done.** Never use fire near a baby, never put anything in a baby's mouth or eyes, and never apply kajal to the eyes of a newborn, as it can cause infection.

## A calm way to treat it
Keep the traditions that you love, as a way of connecting with the family. And treat them as an addition to proper care. A black dot is fine. A visit to the paediatrician is essential.

## What the tradition says about protection
Hindu tradition also offers prayers for protection in place of charms, such as the Durga verse in [which god to pray to for fear and courage](/which-god-to-pray-to-for-fear-and-courage-hanuman-narasimha-durga/) and the Hanuman Chalisa. Many families do both.

## A lesson in it
Perhaps the best use of the belief is a good habit: be generous in your praise but not envious in your heart. If you admire a baby, say *Tatha astu* or *God bless*, and mean it.

## Questions people ask
**Is it wrong to follow these customs if I do not believe in them?**
Not at all. Many people keep a black dot or a lemon at the door as a nod to family and memory, not because they fear a glance. You can hold the custom lightly.

**What if my elders insist?**
Speak kindly. You can say, "We will do the dot, and we are also going to the doctor on Monday." Most grandmothers are happy with that, because what they want is the baby's safety.

**What if I really feel something after a visitor praised my child?**
A baby may be fussy or unwell for many reasons, from a vaccine to a tooth to an infection. Look first for these. If the cause is not clear, call the doctor. A calm mind helps the baby too.

**Is the evil eye mentioned in the Vedas?**
Some old texts have protective verses against illness and ill will, but it is not accurate to say that every modern custom is described in scripture. Many of them are family and folk traditions that grew over time.

## For parents of small babies
A few practical notes that matter more than any charm:
- If you use a dot, place it on the forehead or cheek, well away from the eyes, and use something clean and skin-safe.
- Keep burning materials away from the baby's room.
- Do not give the baby anything by mouth that a doctor has not approved.
- Wash your hands before touching the baby, and ask visitors to do the same. This protects better than a lemon, and your guests will understand.

## When someone praises you
It is easy to feel nervous when someone says, "What a lovely child!" Try these instead of silently worrying:
- Say "thank you" and add "God bless" together.
- Mentally offer the praise to the deity you pray to. Many people say "it is all by his grace".
- Remember that most compliments are meant with love.
