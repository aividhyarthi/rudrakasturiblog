It is not only a spiritual question. It is a very ordinary one. The colleague who takes credit gets promoted. The contractor who pays bribes builds a bigger house. A neighbour who has never done an honest day's work seems to be doing very well. And you, who keep your accounts clean, are struggling to pay the fees.

It stings, and it makes you wonder whether honesty is a mistake. The Mahabharata has a scene where this exact feeling is spoken aloud.

## Draupadi in the forest
The Pandavas are in exile in the forest. They have lost the kingdom in a rigged game. Draupadi, who was dragged into the court and insulted, sits down beside Yudhishthira and says, in effect: *You have followed dharma all your life. And look at where it has brought us. Duryodhana, who cheated, who insulted me, sits on the throne and eats well. Where is the justice in this?* (Vana Parva, the dialogue of Draupadi and Yudhishthira.)

What the epic does next is notable. It does not tell her she is wrong to ask. It lets her say all of it, with anger. Then Yudhishthira answers, and it is not a cheap answer.

## Yudhishthira's reply
He says that he does not follow dharma because he expects a reward, like a trader who bargains. He follows it because it is the right thing to do, and because a mind that begins to doubt dharma has already lost something. He also says, in the words of an old saying, that *dharma protects those who protect it*. Draupadi is not satisfied, and the text lets her remain unsatisfied. Bhima adds his own, rougher view. The epic holds several voices in the same room.

That is, I think, its honesty. It does not say the question is foolish. It says the question is real and the answer is slower than we want.

## What the Gita says about the other side
Krishna describes the kind of person Draupadi is angry about.

> **idam adya mayā labdham imaṁ prāpsye manoratham**
> **idam astīdam api me bhaviṣyati punar dhanam**
> (Bhagavad Gita 16.13)

*This I have got today. That wish I shall fulfil. This wealth is mine, and more will be mine.*

The verses that follow (16.14 and 16.15) have the same man say, *I have killed that enemy and I shall kill others. I am the lord, I enjoy, I am successful and strong and happy.* And Krishna's verdict, at 16.16, is that people caught in this net of desire, bewildered by many thoughts, fall into a hell.

Now, notice what Krishna does *not* say. He does not say they will be punished next Tuesday. He describes a state of mind, a restless and anxious grasping that never says *enough*, and says that this itself is the trap. From the outside, the person looks successful. From the inside, the Gita says, they are running on a wheel.

## Ravana, Kamsa, Hiranyakashipu
Think of the great villains of the stories: Ravana, Kamsa, Hiranyakashipu. Each of them was powerful and rich for a long time. The stories do not hide this. They also do not hide how each one ended. The lesson is not that every bad person falls in a week. It is that *time is long*, and what looks permanent often is not.

## What to do with the anger
- **Say it honestly,** the way Draupadi did, to a friend or in a prayer. Anger swallowed turns into bitterness.
- **Do not compare accounts.** You see a person's visible success and none of their sleepless nights.
- **Keep your own conduct clean.** It is the one thing fully in your control (Gita 2.47).
- **Seek justice where there is a lawful way.** Dharma does not ask you to be a doormat. File the complaint, make the record, talk to a lawyer where needed.
- **Do not let their story become yours.** If you spend your life tracking someone else's fortune, they have taken something that you did not agree to give.

## One thought
Yudhishthira's answer was never that justice is certain by Friday. It was that *you do not give up the thing that makes you who you are because someone else got away with giving it up*. That is a hard thing to hear, and a hard thing to practise. But many who have tried say it is the only thing that gives a quiet night.
