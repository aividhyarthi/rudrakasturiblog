A child looks up at the idol and asks the question every grandparent has had to answer: why does he have an elephant's head? The answer given at the evening lamp is usually a story. It is a good story, and like many in the Puranas it comes in more than one version. Let us walk through them, and then through what the form itself is said to teach.

## The Shiva Purana version

This is the telling most people know. Parvati was about to bathe at Kailasa. She wanted a guard at the door who would obey only her. Tradition holds that she took the paste of turmeric and oil from her own body and shaped it into a boy, then gave him life.

Shiva returned, and the boy, not knowing him, barred the way. Words turned to a fight, and in anger Shiva's attendants, and then Shiva himself, struck off the boy's head. When Parvati saw her son, her grief was fierce enough to shake the worlds.

Shiva promised to restore the child. He sent his ganas to bring the head of the first living being they found facing north, and they returned with an elephant's head. Shiva set it on the boy, gave him life again, and named him the leader of the ganas: Ganapati, Ganesha. He also decreed that he would be worshipped first before any undertaking. That is the root of the custom we explore in [why Ganesha is worshipped first](/why-ganesha-is-worshipped-first/).

## Other tellings

Here the honest answer is: it depends on whom you ask. The Puranas were composed over centuries and in different regions, and they do not agree.

- In some tellings, Ganesha is born with an elephant head from the start, shaped so by Shiva and Parvati.
- In one telling, the head was lost when the planet Shani looked at the child, and the elephant head replaced it.
- In other versions, the story is tied to the Vinayakas, obstacle-making beings, and Ganesha takes charge of them.
- Some tellings place Ganesha's origin in battle with a demon, with his form arising to meet a need in the world.

You will also meet differences in who struck the head, where the elephant was found and how the boy was named. None of these cancels the others. Many teachers say each telling lights one side of the same truth.

## What the form is said to teach

Teachers read Ganesha's body as a lesson in itself. These are traditional readings, offered by many, not a single fixed doctrine.

**The elephant head.** The elephant is strong, wise and gentle. The head is read as a sign of wisdom and of power that does not need to be loud.

**Big ears.** Listen more than you speak. Large ears take in everything and keep what is useful.

**Small eyes.** Small eyes focus. They are said to teach concentration, and the ability to look closely at what matters.

**A small mouth, hidden under the trunk.** Speak little, and speak with care.

**The long trunk.** It can lift a huge log or pick up a needle, a sign of skill that scales from the great to the very small.

**The large belly.** It is said to hold the world's joys and sorrows without being disturbed. Some tellings say it holds the universe.

**One tusk.** Tradition tells two stories. In one, Parashurama struck it in a fight at Kailasa. In another, Ganesha broke his own tusk to write down the Mahabharata as Vyasa spoke it. Either way, the lesson is of sacrifice made for the sake of something larger.

**The mouse.** The mouse is his vahana, his vehicle. It is small, restless and always nibbling. Teachers read it as the ceaseless desire of the mind, carried and mastered by Ganesha, not destroyed.

**The modaka and the noose and goad.** The sweet represents the joy that comes from effort and knowledge; the goad urges forward and the noose holds back what must be restrained.

## The names that carry the story

His many names keep these tellings alive. Gajanana means the elephant-faced one, Ekadanta the one-tusked, Lambodara the one with the hanging belly, Vighnaraja the king of obstacles. In Telugu he is Vinayakudu (వినాయకుడు) and in Tamil Pillaiyar, the beloved child, a name that reminds you he is first of all a son. Each name is a small window into one of the stories, and reciting the 108 names is, for many families, a way of retelling the whole of it.

## Why does it matter?

You might ask whether a story needs to be true to be useful. In the Puranic tradition, a story is a vehicle. The tale of the beheaded boy asks us to look at anger, grief and repair: a parent's love, a father's mistake, and the generous act of making things whole. The elephant head is not an embarrassment to be explained away. It is the sign of a god who accepts the odd and the broken, and makes them holy.

Which version did you grow up with? Many people only learn the Shiva Purana telling from films and picture books, and do not know that grandparents in another region may tell a different one.
