A cousin in Pune posts photographs of a Hanuman Jayanti procession in spring. A month or so later, an aunt in Hyderabad is fasting and visiting the temple for the same festival. And in the winter, in Chennai, a friend is queueing for a laddu garland at the Hanuman temple for what he also calls Hanuman Jayanti. Is someone mistaken? No. Several traditions are being honoured, each with its own reasons.

## The main dates

Let us put them side by side, remembering that these are common customs, not every family's.

**Chaitra Pournami (March or April).** In much of North India, Maharashtra and Gujarat, Hanuman Jayanti is kept on the full-moon day of the month of Chaitra, often at sunrise, which is said in tradition to be the time of his birth. This is the date that the national calendars and news usually carry.

**Vaishakha Krishna Dashami (April or May).** In Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, many families and temples keep Hanuman Jayanti on the tenth day of the dark fortnight in Vaishakha. This is also when the Hanuman deeksha, a period of discipline and vow-keeping that devotees take for 41 days or a shorter span, is concluded at places like Kondagattu in Telangana. The details differ from temple to temple.

**Margazhi Amavasya with Moola nakshatra (December or January).** In Tamil Nadu, Hanumath Jayanti is celebrated in the Tamil month of Margazhi, on the new-moon day when the star Moola is present. Many Hanuman temples, such as those with great garlands of vada or butter, hold their big celebration on this day.

**Margashira in Karnataka.** In parts of Karnataka some lists give Margashira Trayodashi, in the same winter season as the Tamil date. Karnataka also has other local practices, so check with the temple you attend.

## Why do the sources differ?

Honestly, the sources do not give a single answer. Hanuman is a figure of both the Ramayana and the later Puranas, and different texts and local lore place his birth on different days. Different regions follow different calendar systems as well: the North counts months from the full moon (purnimanta), while Andhra, Telangana, Karnataka, Maharashtra and Tamil Nadu largely follow the new-moon system (amanta), so the same fortnight may be named for a different month depending on where you stand.

On top of that, each region keeps a tradition linked to its own temples, saints and stories. The Tamil date is tied to the star Moola; the Andhra date is linked to its deeksha practice and to Vaishakha. Whether these represent separate events, a remembered birth, a victory day, or a day of special worship, the tradition does not settle it. We should say plainly that scholars and priests offer several explanations, and none is universally accepted.

## Two broad streams

If you look closely, the dates fall into two broad streams. One is the spring festival, Chaitra Pournami and Vaishakha, when the year is waking and Rama Navami has just passed. The other is the winter festival of Margazhi, the month of early-morning worship, when the Tamil and Karnataka traditions remember him. That is why people often speak of two dates, even though the detailed lists are longer. Each stream has its own temples, songs and foods, and neither asks the other to give way.

## How to decide for your family

You do not need to resolve a scholarly argument. Here is a sensible order:

1. **Ask your elders.** What date did your grandmother keep? That is your family tradition, and it is a good enough answer.
2. **Ask your temple or priest.** If you worship regularly at a Hanuman or Anjaneya temple, follow the day they celebrate. You will find people to celebrate with.
3. **Follow your region's panchangam.** The dates are published in your local calendar; check ours on the [festivals page](/festivals/) or the [Hyderabad panchangam](/panchangam/hyderabad/).
4. **If you have no tradition,** choose the one closest to where you live and keep it, or honour more than one. Nobody will count.

There is nothing wrong with marking two days. Some families mark Chaitra Pournami in spring and the Margazhi day in winter, and they say that loving Hanuman twice a year does him no harm.

## How to keep the day

Whichever date you take, the observance is much the same: a morning bath, a lamp before Hanuman's picture, vermilion and flowers, offerings of betel leaves, vada, jaggery or a sweet, and a reading. Saturday and Tuesday are Hanuman's usual days of worship in many homes, and a Jayanti falling on one of them is felt to be especially fortunate. You can recite the Chalisa; if you are new to it, begin with [Hanuman Chalisa: meaning of the first five verses](/hanuman-chalisa-meaning-first-five-verses/), or read his story in [Hanuman: birth, strength and devotion](/hanuman-birth-strength-and-devotion-the-stories/).

A simple fast is common but not required, and children can help by stringing flowers or drawing a small mace and a sun on the floor.

## What to remember

Do not let the question of the right date become an argument at the family table. Elders in one village may have kept one day for generations, and a young couple who moved from another state may bring a second. Welcome both.

Dates are the frame. The picture inside it is a servant of Rama, strong and humble, who remembered his purpose when reminded. If you spend the day in that spirit, you have kept Hanuman Jayanti, whichever page of the calendar it fell on.
