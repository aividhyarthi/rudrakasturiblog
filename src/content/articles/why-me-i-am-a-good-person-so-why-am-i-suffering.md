You woke up early. You never cheated anyone. You helped your family, you lit the lamp, you went to the temple on the days you said you would. And then the call came, or the report, or the letter. And the first thing in your head was three words.

*Why me?*

If you have asked this, you are in very good company. Rama lived it in the forest. Draupadi put her questions to the whole Kaurava court. Yudhishthira, who never told a lie, asked it for thirteen years. This is probably the oldest question there is, and the tradition does not dodge it. So let us not dodge it either.

## First, a gentle correction
Many of us carry a quiet belief: *I did good, so good should come back to me.* It is a natural belief, and it is partly true. Good actions do bear good fruit. But the belief has a hidden second half: *so if bad came, I must have done bad.* That second half is cruel, and the tradition does not teach it.

Look at who suffers in the epics. Rama gave up a kingdom on the morning of his coronation. Sita was carried away from the forest hut. Harishchandra lost his kingdom, his son and his name. The Pandavas, who followed dharma more closely than anyone, lived in the forest in disguise. Nobody reading those stories says, *they must have deserved it.* We say: *what a thing to bear.*

## What Krishna says
Arjuna also asked a version of this. He saw his own teachers across the battlefield and wanted to put down his bow. Krishna did not give him a theory of fairness. He gave him a few lines that I would like you to read slowly.

> **mātrā-sparśās tu kaunteya śītoṣṇa-sukha-duḥkha-dāḥ**
> **āgamāpāyino 'nityās tān titikṣasva bhārata**
> (Bhagavad Gita 2.14)

In plain words: *Cold and heat, pleasure and pain come from the meeting of the senses with the world. They come and they go. They do not stay. Learn to bear them, Arjuna.*

Notice what is *not* said. He does not say pain is unreal or that you should smile. He says it passes, and he says there is a skill in bearing it, *titiksha*, which is not the same as suppressing it. It is closer to carrying a heavy load without letting it bend your spine.

## Five lenses, not one answer
The honest truth is that the tradition has no single answer to why me. It gives five lenses, and different seasons of life need different ones.

**1. Karma that has already begun to ripen.** Vedanta speaks of *prarabdha*, the part of your past actions that has already started to bear fruit, like an arrow that has left the bow. You cannot call it back. But this is not a punishment ledger that you can read. Krishna says, *gahanā karmaṇo gatiḥ*, the way of karma is deep, hard to see through (4.17). Anyone who tells you exactly what you did to deserve this pain is claiming more than the Gita does.

**2. The nature of the world.** The world is a mixed place. The Gita calls it *duhkhalayam ashashvatam*, a home of sorrow and impermanence (8.15). Rain falls on good and bad fields alike. Some pain is not a message. It is just weather.

**3. Growth.** The Katha Upanishad (1.2) separates two roads, *shreyas*, what is good for you, and *preyas*, what is pleasing right now. Hard seasons often push us from one road to the other, though no one should say this to a person in the middle of grief.

**4. Grace.** Krishna says: *Those who think of me alone and worship me, I carry what they lack and protect what they have* (9.22). The promise is not that the road will be smooth. It is that you will not walk it alone.

**5. Steadiness.** In the end the Gita keeps returning to one thing: *not to be broken by what comes*. That is the real aim of the lines on pain and pleasure.

## What if I cannot accept any of this today?
Then do not. A heart in pain is not a classroom. The tradition itself makes room for protest. Draupadi argues with Yudhishthira in the forest about the justice of it all (Vana Parva), and he does not silence her. The Alvars and Nayanmars complain to God like a child. Say it honestly. Then do one small, right thing today: call someone, eat, light the lamp, walk. Great teachings are meant to be slow companions, not instant cures.

## A line to carry
Krishna also says: *na hi kalyāṇa-kṛt kaścid durgatiṁ tāta gacchati*. No one who does good ever comes to a bad end (6.40). It does not say, *no one who does good will suffer*. It says that nothing good you have done is wasted, and that in the long view you are not lost.

## If you are in real distress
If the pain feels too heavy, if you cannot sleep or eat for days, or if you have thoughts of ending your life, please talk to someone today: a doctor, a counsellor, a trusted person. In India, Tele-MANAS (14416) is free and open round the clock. Faith and help are not opposites.
