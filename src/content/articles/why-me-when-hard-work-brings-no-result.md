You studied for two years. You got up at five. You skipped the weddings and the festivals. And the result came, and it was not what it should have been. Or you built something for ten years and it did not work. Or you waited for a job, a visa, a match, and the answer was no.

The question that rises is not just *why me?* It is *what was it all for?*

This is a place where the Gita has a very particular thing to say. Many people know the verse, but few have sat with it. Let us read it again.

## The line everyone knows
> **karmaṇy evādhikāras te mā phaleṣu kadācana**
> **mā karma-phala-hetur bhūr mā te saṅgo 'stv akarmaṇi**
> (Bhagavad Gita 2.47)

*Your right is to the action alone, never to its fruits. Do not let the fruit be your motive, and do not be attached to inaction.*

People often read this as *work and do not expect anything*. That is not what it says, and if it were it would be unkind. Read the last part: do not let the fruit be your *only* reason, and do not fall into not acting. It is not telling you to stop wanting a good result. It is telling you that the result is not entirely in your hands, and that your peace should not depend on it.

## Why the result is not only yours
Krishna explains this near the end of the Gita, when he is summing up.

> **adhiṣṭhānaṁ tathā kartā karaṇaṁ ca pṛthag-vidham**
> **vividhāś ca pṛthak ceṣṭā daivaṁ caivātra pañcamam**
> (Bhagavad Gita 18.14)

*The seat of the action, the doer, the many kinds of instruments, the various efforts, and, fifth, the unseen factor (daiva).*

So five things go into any result, and your effort is only one. The place and the circumstances, the instruments you have, the doer and his abilities, the effort, and what is beyond all of that. You are fully in charge of one of the five. This is not an excuse for failure. It is a way of letting go of the idea that every result is a verdict on you.

## Shabari, who waited for years
In the Aranyakanda of the Valmiki Ramayana, Rama and Lakshmana come to the hermitage of Matanga. There, an old woman named Shabari was waiting. She had served the sages there all her life, and her teachers had told her that Rama would one day arrive. Years passed. She grew old. As the text tells it, she went on living in that hermitage, keeping her teachers' word that he would come.

She could not know if the day would come. When Rama finally arrived, she welcomed him with the fruits and roots she had gathered. The Ramayana does not describe her waiting as a waste. It shows her ready when the moment came.

## The Pandavas in the forest
The Pandavas spent thirteen years in exile, twelve in the forest and one in disguise. Nothing guaranteed that they would get their kingdom back. They trained, they prayed, they listened to sages, and they carried on. When the time came, they were ready. The effort had not been wasted. The result simply arrived on a timetable that was not theirs.

## Rama on the morning of the coronation
Rama had waited his whole life to be king. Everything was ready, and by evening he was in the forest. If anyone had cause to say that effort and virtue are not enough, it was he. The Ramayana shows him accepting it without bitterness, and that is the hardest kind of effort there is.

## What to do when the result does not come
- **Separate what you control from what you do not.** Make two lists. Work on the first.
- **Ask what the effort gave you,** even if it did not give you the prize: a skill, a habit, a friend, a clearer idea of what you want.
- **Do not decide your worth from one result.** Five factors, remember.
- **Keep one small daily action.** The Gita's yoga is to keep working steadily.
- **Look at the plan, not at yourself.** *What should I change?* is a better question than *what is wrong with me?*

## If the low mood does not lift
Repeated failure can wear a person down. If you feel hopeless for more than two weeks, or sleep and appetite suffer, please talk to a doctor or counsellor. Tele-MANAS (14416) is free in India.
