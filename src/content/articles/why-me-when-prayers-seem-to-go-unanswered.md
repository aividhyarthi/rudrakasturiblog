You lit the lamp every morning. You went to the temple on the day you had vowed. You fasted. You said the name of God on the bus and in the hospital waiting room. And nothing has changed.

There comes a day when the lamp seems like just a lamp. And the question you are afraid to say aloud comes: *is anyone there?*

If you are at that point, I will not tell you that you simply need more faith. I will show you what the texts say to people at exactly this place.

## Krishna's list of four
In the seventh chapter of the Gita, Krishna says something that surprises many readers:

> **caturvidhā bhajante māṁ janāḥ sukṛtino 'rjuna**
> **ārto jijñāsur arthārthī jñānī ca bharatarṣabha**
> (Bhagavad Gita 7.16)

*Four kinds of good people worship me, Arjuna: the distressed, the seeker of knowledge, the seeker of wealth and the wise.*

Look at who comes first: *arta*, the person in pain. And two verses later he says, *udārāḥ sarva evaite*, all of these are noble (7.18). No one is told that praying out of distress is lower than praying out of wisdom. The person who turns to God because they have nowhere else to turn is on the list, and at the head of it.

So if you are praying from a hospital corridor, you have not failed at religion. You are praying the way the Gita first names.

## Gajendra, who tried everything first
In the Bhagavata Purana (Canto 8, chapters 2 to 4) there is an elephant king, Gajendra, who goes to a lake and is caught by a crocodile. He pulls and pulls. The story says it went on for a very long time. His family tries to help and cannot. Only when his own strength is finished does he turn, and the text gives us a prayer of great beauty, with no demand in it, only surrender. Vishnu comes.

The old commentators say: *the help came when the effort had run out.* That does not mean we must wait until we have run out. It means the story is for those who are at the end.

## Draupadi, and the cloth that did not end
In the Mahabharata (Sabha Parva), Draupadi is dragged into the court and the cloth is pulled from her. She first holds on with her hands, and then she holds up both her hands and calls on Krishna. In the story, her cloth does not run out. People have always wondered whether Krishna was late. The tradition answers: *the help came in the form of what she needed, which was that the cloth did not end.* It was not what she first asked for. She had asked that the whole thing not happen.

## Prahlada, who prayed in a fire
Prahlada (Bhagavata, Canto 7) prayed through poison, elephants and fire. His faith was not rewarded with an easy life, since his father kept trying to destroy him. The story is not about being spared trouble. It is about a child who could not be made to hate, and in the end the one who had tormented him could not stand against him.

## What I can honestly say
- **Prayer is not a vending machine.** The traditions are clear about this, in every one of these stories.
- **The answer is often a different shape.** Strength, a person who turns up, a door that opens for someone else.
- **Dry seasons are in the record.** Many saints write of long seasons when God felt absent, and kept the practice going.
- **Keep one small thing.** One lamp, one verse, one minute of silence. When you have nothing, a small thing is enough.
- **Combine prayer with action.** Arjuna prayed and fought. Draupadi cried and also argued. Gajendra pulled before he prayed.

## One line for the heart
> **ananyāś cintayanto māṁ ye janāḥ paryupāsate**
> **teṣāṁ nityābhiyuktānāṁ yogakṣemaṁ vahāmy aham**
> (Bhagavad Gita 9.22)

*To those who think of me alone and worship me, ever steadfast, I bring what they lack and keep what they have.* It does not say the road will be smooth. It says you will be carried.
