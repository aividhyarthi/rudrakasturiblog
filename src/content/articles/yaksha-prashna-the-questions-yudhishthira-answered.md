*అరణ్యవాసంలో నీళ్ల కోసం వెళ్లిన నలుగురు పాండవులు సరస్సు దగ్గర పడిపోయారు, యక్షుని ప్రశ్నలకు సమాధానం చెప్పినది ధర్మరాజు ఒక్కడే. చివరికి ఆ యక్షుడు తన తండ్రి ధర్మదేవుడే అని తెలిసింది.*

When a day goes badly, we often ask one question: why me? The scene at the lake asks a different one. It asks: when everything you love has fallen in front of you, what do you say, and how much do you really know?

## The thirsty brothers

Tradition holds that this takes place in the twelfth year of the forest exile. A Brahmin's fire-sticks are carried off by a deer, and the Pandavas chase it. Tired and thirsty, Yudhishthira (ధర్మరాజు) sends Nakula for water. Nakula finds a clear lake and hears an unseen voice: answer my questions first, or drink and die. Thirst wins. Nakula drinks and falls. One by one, Sahadeva, Arjuna and Bhima go, each ignoring the warning, and each falls. The mighty Bhima and the great archer are laid low by a lake and a voice.

Finally Yudhishthira goes himself, finds his brothers lifeless, and hears the same voice. He does what none of them did: he stops, listens and agrees to answer.

## The questions

The Yaksha asks a great many questions, dozens in most editions, though the number and order differ. Here are the most quoted, in paraphrase.

- What is heavier than the earth? A mother. What is higher than the sky? A father.
- What is swifter than the wind? The mind. What is more numerous than blades of grass? Worries, or thoughts.
- Who is the friend of the traveller? Learning. Of the one at home? The spouse. Of the sick? The physician. Of the dying? Charity, or good deeds.
- What is the enemy that is hard to conquer? Anger. What is the disease that cannot be cured? Greed.
- Who is truly a Brahmin? Not birth or learning, Yudhishthira says, but conduct.
- Who is happy? One who has no debt, is not away from home, and cooks a simple meal in his own house.

Mark how plain these are, and how each answer turns outward questions into inward ones: not what is the world made of, but what holds a life together. No Sanskrit grandeur, no theory. A grown person can hold each one in the mind during a long walk.

## The greatest wonder

The most famous exchange is about wonder. The Yaksha asks what the greatest wonder in the world is. In substance, Yudhishthira replies that day after day countless beings go to the abode of Yama, the god of death, and yet those who remain want to live for ever. What could be more astonishing?

Notice that he does not say death is bad. He says our response to it is the strange thing. Everyone has seen a cremation, or knows a family where there has been loss, and yet we plan, quarrel and hoard as though we are the exception. ## The question of the path

Another much-quoted answer concerns the way. The Yaksha asks what the true path is. Yudhishthira's reply is a famous one, though its words are rendered in different ways: reasoning has no firm footing, the scriptures disagree, there is no sage whose word settles everything, and the truth of dharma is hidden in a cave, so the path is the one that great people have walked. Some read it as humility, some as a defence of tradition, and others as a call to follow living example rather than argument. Which do you hear?

The pause itself deserves a thought. Four strong men, each in a hurry, drank first and asked later. Yudhishthira alone was patient enough to treat an unseen voice as a person to be answered. Many readers see in this the difference between strength and steadiness: the lake does not test what you can lift, but whether you can wait, listen and tell the truth when you are thirsty and frightened.

## The choice and the reveal

After the questions, the Yaksha is pleased and offers to revive one brother. Yudhishthira chooses Nakula. The Yaksha is surprised: why not Bhima or Arjuna? Yudhishthira answers that Kunti has one son still living, himself, and Madri should also have one, so that both mothers are equal in his eyes. This impartiality delights the Yaksha, who restores all four.

Then the Yaksha reveals himself as Dharma, Yudhishthira's father, who had come to test him. He blesses the brothers, and promises that they will remain unrecognised in the thirteenth year. ## Where this comes from

The scene is in the Aranyaka Parva (Vana Parva) of Vyasa's Mahabharata, in the section known as the Yaksha Prashna. Editions differ in the number of questions and the order of answers, so a list from a popular book may not match the one in your copy. Some of the most quoted lines also circulate as free-standing verses in Telugu, Tamil and other languages. The question about the path and the greatest wonder are the ones most reliably found in all versions.
