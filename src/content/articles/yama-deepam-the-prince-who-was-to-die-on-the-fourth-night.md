Have you ever noticed a tiny lamp, apart from all the others, sitting alone at the edge of a doorstep on the evening before Naraka Chaturdashi? It is not for Lakshmi. It does not face the house. It looks outward, to the south, into the dark. This is the Yama Deepam, and it comes with one of the most tender stories of Deepavali.

## The story as families tell it

A king named Hima had a son who, the astrologers said, would die at sixteen. More exactly, on the fourth day after his wedding, a snake would bite him. The king married him anyway, as kings and parents do, hoping the stars were wrong.

The bride was a clever young woman. Knowing the prophecy, she planned the fourth night with care. She did not let her husband sleep. She laid all her gold and silver ornaments, and every coin she had, in a great heap at the door of the bedchamber, and lit lamps all through the house until there was no dark corner. Then she sat with him and sang, and told him stories, hour after hour.

At midnight Yama, Lord of Death, came in the form of a snake. The glitter of the heap and the blaze of the lamps dazzled his eyes, and he could not enter. So he climbed onto the heap of ornaments and sat there. The songs went on. Something in him, tradition says, was moved, and he listened until the sky grew pale. At dawn he slid away, and the prince lived.

That night is remembered as Yamadeepadana, the giving of a lamp to Yama — Death came, and went away as a listener.

## What the story is really about

Look at what the young wife did. She did not fight Yama. She did not argue with the stars. She stayed awake, kept the light on, and filled the night with beauty. Many families read the heap of ornaments as an offering and the songs as the real weapon. Ask yourself honestly: when fear arrives, do you reach for argument, or for presence?

## The custom today

On Dhanatrayodashi, in the evening, many families light a lamp for Yama.

- Use a small clay lamp, often with a sesame or mustard oil wick. Some homes use a lamp made of wheat flour.
- Place it outside the house, at the doorstep or courtyard edge, with the flame facing south.
- Light it after dusk, around pradosha time.
- Some offer a short prayer that the family be spared untimely death. There is no single fixed mantra, and a simple silent prayer is fully accepted.

You do not need to do more than this. If you live in a flat, the lamp can go just outside your door or on the balcony ledge facing south, if there is one. If the direction is impossible, do not worry; the intention is what the custom protects.

Children often ask why the lamp is lit for Yama at all, when we usually pray to be spared him. The answer many elders give is that Yama is also Dharmaraja, the keeper of order, and that one does not hide from him but greets him politely at the threshold. A lamp is a courteous way of saying: we see you, and we are not afraid tonight.

## Dhanvantari on the same day

The same day is honoured as the day Dhanvantari, the physician of the gods, rose from the churning of the ocean holding a pot of amrita. This is why Dhanatrayodashi is tied to health as well as wealth, and why many families remember the healer with a lamp. For which god people pray when health is the worry, see [which god to pray to for health and recovery](/which-god-to-pray-to-for-health-and-recovery-dhanvantari-and-shiva/).

In Telugu homes the day is Dhanatrayodashi (ధనత్రయోదశి), and in Tamil homes it often passes quietly in the Deepavali bustle, with the Yama Deepam observed mostly where a family has inherited the habit. In many South Indian homes the day's main activity is buying a little metal or cleaning the prayer shelf, and the doorstep lamp is the part grandmothers insist on. ## Where this comes from

Be careful here, because this story is often told as if it were scripture. It is not in Vyasa's Mahabharata or Valmiki's Ramayana. It is a folk and Puranic tradition, told mostly in North India, and some people say Maharashtra is where the telling is strongest. We cannot point you to a specific chapter, so treat the names (Hima, the wife, the snake) as the storyteller's frame. The practice of giving a lamp to Yama on Trayodashi is described in later dharma-shastra digests, which is a different thing from the story of the prince. Dhanvantari's rising from the ocean is told in the Puranas, including the Bhagavata, though tellings differ about the exact day he is celebrated.

The story may be later than the custom, or the custom may be older than the story. We do not know. That uncertainty is part of why it is worth asking elders in your own family.
