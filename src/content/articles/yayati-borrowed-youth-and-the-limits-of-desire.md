The story of Yayati, told in the Adi Parva of the Mahabharata, is the ancestry of the Pandavas and Kauravas, and one of the sharpest teachings on desire.

## The curse
Yayati had married Devayani, the daughter of the sage Shukracharya, and kept her friend Sharmishtha as a second wife in secret. When Devayani discovered it, her father cursed Yayati with premature old age. Shukracharya softened the curse: Yayati could pass his old age to any of his sons who would agree to take it.

## The exchange
Yayati asked each of his sons. The elder ones refused. The youngest, Puru, agreed, and Yayati took Puru's youth and enjoyed it for a thousand years.

## The realisation
At the end of a thousand years Yayati, still unsatisfied, spoke the verse the epic is famous for: that desire is never quenched by enjoying its objects, as fire is not quenched by pouring ghee on it, but only grows. He gave Puru his youth back, took his old age, and made Puru king. Puru's line became the Kurus.

## What the story teaches
- **Desire feeds on itself.** More does not mean enough. The tradition is clear that contentment comes from within.
- **Using others for your pleasure has a cost.** Yayati's pleasure was paid for by his son's youth.
- **It is never too late to see clearly.** After a thousand years, Yayati admitted it. He did not wait until death.
- **Duty to the one who sacrifices.** Yayati's gratitude to Puru was shown by giving him the kingdom.

## For today
Notice what you reach for when you are restless: the next purchase, the next scroll, the next goal. Ask whether more of it will be enough. If the honest answer is no, you have heard Yayati's lesson for the price of one minute.
