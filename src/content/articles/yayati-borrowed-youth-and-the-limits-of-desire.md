The story of Yayati, told in the Adi Parva of the Mahabharata, is the ancestry of the Pandavas and Kauravas, and one of the sharpest teachings on desire.

## The curse
Yayati had married Devayani, the daughter of the sage Shukracharya, and kept her friend Sharmishtha as a second wife in secret. When Devayani discovered it, her father cursed Yayati with premature old age. Shukracharya softened the curse: Yayati could pass his old age to any of his sons who would agree to take it.

## The exchange
Yayati asked each of his sons. The elder ones refused. The youngest, Puru, agreed, and Yayati took Puru's youth and enjoyed it for a thousand years.

## The realisation
At the end of a thousand years Yayati, still unsatisfied, spoke the verse the epic is famous for: that desire is never quenched by enjoying its objects, as fire is not quenched by pouring ghee on it, but only grows. He gave Puru his youth back, took his old age, and made Puru king. Puru's line became the Kurus.

## What the story teaches
- **Desire feeds on itself.** More does not mean enough. The tradition is clear that contentment comes from within.
- **Using others for your pleasure has a cost.** Yayati's pleasure was paid for by his son's youth.
- **It is never too late to see clearly.** After a thousand years, Yayati admitted it. He did not wait until death.
- **Duty to the one who sacrifices.** Yayati's gratitude to Puru was shown by giving him the kingdom.

## For today
Notice what you reach for when you are restless: the next purchase, the next scroll, the next goal. Ask whether more of it will be enough. If the honest answer is no, you have heard Yayati's lesson for the price of one minute.

## A closer look at the famous verse
The verse Yayati speaks is among the best-known in the Mahabharata. It runs roughly: *na jatu kamah kamanam upabhogena shamyati, havisha krishnavartmeva bhuya evabhivardhate.* In plain words, desire is never put out by enjoying what it wants. Like a fire fed with ghee, it only blazes higher. The same thought is found in other Sanskrit texts as well, which tells you how widely it was shared.

Notice who says it. This is not a teacher scolding from outside. It is a king who has lived a thousand years of pleasure and is reporting from the inside. That is why it has force.

## The people in the story
Yayati's story starts earlier. In the epic, he first meets Devayani when he finds her in a dry well, and he helps her out. Devayani is the daughter of Shukracharya, the teacher of the demons. Sharmishtha is the daughter of the demon king Vrishaparva, and she had been Devayani's friend before a quarrel made her Devayani's servant. Yayati had two sons with Devayani, Yadu and Turvasu, and three with Sharmishtha, Druhyu, Anu and Puru.

When Yayati asked his sons to take his old age, Yadu refused, and tradition says Yayati cursed that his line would not rule. Yet from Yadu came the Yadavas, the clan of Krishna. And from Puru, who agreed, came the Kurus. It is one of the epic's quiet ironies. ## Questions people ask

**Was it fair of Yayati to take his son's youth?**
The epic does not praise it. The story is clear that the pleasure was borrowed, and the sorrow of the exchange is felt. Puru's willingness is honoured, but Yayati is the one who learns.

**Why did Puru agree?**
The text presents it as love and respect for his father. In many readings, he is a model of a son who puts the family first. It is also a reminder that parents should not ask this of their children.

**Does the story say pleasure is bad?**
No. It says that chasing more pleasure to reach "enough" does not work. Enjoyment in its place is fine. The problem is the belief that the next one will finally fill the gap.

## A small practice
Next time you want something badly, wait three days. If you still want it, buy or do it with an open heart. If not, you have learned how much of the wanting was just noise. ## Reading it with modern eyes
Yayati's borrowing has a modern face. Parents sometimes lean on a child's savings, time or career to ease their own lives. Nations and companies borrow from the future in the same way. The story does not need us to be dramatic about it. It asks a plain question: whose youth am I spending, and for what?

The kinder side is Puru's. A child who steps forward out of love is giving something precious, and a wise elder will not let that gift be taken for granted. Yayati's best moment is when he hands everything back.
