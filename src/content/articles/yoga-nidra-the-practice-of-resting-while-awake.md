It is nine at night. You are tired but not sleepy. Your body wants to lie down, and your mind is still racing through the day. If you try to sleep, you roll for an hour. If you try to read, the words slide.

There is an old practice for exactly this state, and it is done lying down.

## What it is
*Yoga nidra* means "yogic sleep". It is a guided practice in which you lie on your back, close your eyes and follow a voice, or your own memory, as attention moves slowly through the body and breath. You are not asleep. You are in a state of deep rest, with a kind of quiet awareness. The aim is to relax the body and settle the mind without effort.

## Where it comes from
The idea of a state between waking and sleep appears in ancient texts. The Mandukya Upanishad speaks of four states of consciousness: waking, dreaming, deep sleep and a fourth, *turiya*, that underlies the others. Tantric traditions developed practices of body awareness called *nyasa*, in which parts of the body are touched and mentally consecrated.

The practice as we know it was assembled in the twentieth century by **Swami Satyananda Saraswati**, who founded the Bihar School of Yoga in Munger. In the 1960s he developed a structured system of "rotation of consciousness" through the body, drawing on those older sources, and taught it widely. Most of the yoga nidra recordings and classes in the world come from his method or those who followed it.

## What the research says
Researchers have studied yoga nidra in small trials. Some have found that regular practice is linked with lower stress and anxiety, better sleep and improved wellbeing in groups such as students, nurses and people with insomnia. The studies are modest in size and quality, and more research is needed. It is fair to say that it appears to be a safe and helpful relaxation practice for many people, and not a treatment for any condition.

## A step-by-step practice
Here is a simple version that takes about fifteen minutes.
1. **Lie down** on your back on a mat or bed, with a thin pillow if needed. Let your feet fall apart and your arms rest a little away from your body, palms up. Cover yourself with a light cloth, since the body cools as it relaxes.
2. **Settle.** Take three slow breaths. Feel your weight on the surface under you.
3. **Set an intention,** a *sankalpa*. Choose a short, positive sentence in the present tense, such as *I am calm* or *I am at ease*. Say it three times in your mind.
4. **Move your attention through the body.** Slowly, in order, bring awareness to each part: right thumb, fingers, palm, wrist, arm, shoulder; then the left side; then the right leg, the left leg; then the back, the front, the head and the face. Spend a few seconds on each. You are not trying to relax them; you are just noticing.
5. **Follow the breath.** Notice the natural rhythm of breathing, in and out, for a minute or two. Count backwards from 27 to 1 on each out-breath, if you like.
6. **Welcome opposites,** if you wish: feel heaviness, then lightness; warmth, then coolness. Let them come and go.
7. **Picture something calm,** a lake, a quiet room, a lamp.
8. **Repeat your intention** three times.
9. **Return.** Deepen the breath. Wiggle fingers and toes. Roll to your side, stay there for a moment, and sit up slowly.

If you fall asleep, it is fine. Many people do. If your mind wanders, bring it back gently, without scolding.

## When it helps
- **At night,** as a way of winding down before sleep. See also [Yoga for sleep: an evening routine](/yoga-for-sleep-an-evening-routine-that-helps-you-wind-down/).
- **In the afternoon,** as a short reset instead of a nap, 10 to 15 minutes.
- **After a stressful day,** to let the body settle.

## A note of care
- **If you have a history of trauma,** or if lying still with your eyes closed brings up difficult memories, go gently. You can keep your eyes open, stay for a shorter time, or practise with a teacher or therapist.
- **If you have severe depression or another mental-health condition,** speak to your doctor before starting a new practice.
- **If you are pregnant,** lie on your left side, or with the upper body raised.
- **Do not practise while driving** or in a place where you need to be alert.
- **It does not replace treatment** for insomnia or anxiety. If your sleep problems continue, please see a doctor. Tele-MANAS (14416) is free in India for emotional support.

## Why it fits an old idea
The tradition has always said that rest is part of practice, not its opposite. The Gita (6.17) speaks of *yukta-svapnavabodha*, "regulated sleep and waking", as the condition of a person who is free from sorrow. Yoga nidra is a modern form of that old advice: to learn how to rest well.
