*యోగవాసిష్ఠం వసిష్ఠుడు రాముడికి కథల ద్వారా చెప్పిన పెద్ద అద్వైత గ్రంథం. అందులో చూడాల రాణి భర్త శిఖిధ్వజుడికే గురువై అతన్ని జ్ఞానం వైపు నడిపిస్తుంది, ఈ కథ చాలా మందికి ఇష్టం.*

Imagine a young prince, home from pilgrimage, who stops eating and stops talking. He sits and asks one question: what is the use of all this, the palaces, the people, the days? His father is alarmed. That prince is Rama, and the long answer he receives is the Yoga Vasistha (యోగవాసిష్ఠం).

## What this text is

The Yoga Vasistha is a very large Sanskrit work of non-dual teaching, in which Sage Vasishtha instructs Rama. Its method is unusual. It does not simply state a doctrine. It tells story after story, some very strange, to show that the world is made in the mind, as a dream is. If you want a plain introduction to that idea first, try [Advaita Vedanta in plain words](/advaita-vedanta-in-plain-words/).

Now a careful point. Tradition attributes the work to Valmiki, and it is sometimes called the Maha Ramayana. But scholars place it many centuries after Valmiki's epic, and the exact date is debated. So take Vasishtha here as a voice in a later philosophical work that borrows the Ramayana's frame, not as a record of what was said to Rama in the epic.

## Chudala and Shikhidhvaja, in the telling

The story of Queen Chudala comes late in the text. In the telling, she and her husband, King Shikhidhvaja, are a happy royal pair. Chudala begins to study the self deeply and awakens. The king, though, remains restless. He wants to renounce the kingdom and go to the forest, feeling that the palace itself is the obstacle.

Chudala tries to speak to him, but he thinks of her as a woman of the household and will not listen. So he leaves, and rules nothing. She looks after the kingdom for years. Then, through her inner powers, she learns how he is doing, and she comes to him in the forest, in the disguise of a young sage's son called Kumbha, as the story goes.

Kumbha is welcomed. The king, who would not hear his wife, now listens gladly to a young stranger. Kumbha teaches him, and the king grows in understanding. Then come the tests, and the tellings differ on their details, so we give only the outline. In the telling, the king is first asked to give up what he still owns, then his hermitage, and finally even the pride of being a renouncer. Another episode has Kumbha taking a woman's form by night, as Madanika, and offering the king a life together, to see whether his freedom is real or only a vow. The king's reaction shows that something in him is still holding on.

At the end, when he lets go even of his idea of renunciation and sits at peace, Chudala drops the disguise and tells him who she is. They return to the kingdom and rule together, free within, in the manner of what the text calls a living liberation (jivanmukti).

## Why this story is told

Look at what the tale is not saying. It is not saying that the household is a prison, or that women need men to teach them. Chudala is the first to wake. The king's problem is a belief, that freedom is in a place: the forest, the robe, the cave. The queen's answer is that bondage is a thought, and a thought can be dropped at court as easily as in the woods.

Many readers also notice that Chudala hides who she is. Why? Because he will listen to the sage's son, but not to the wife. The story, in its gentle way, points at our habit of weighing a teaching by who says it. Compare how [Ashtavakra](/ashtavakra-the-boy-with-eight-bends-who-silenced-a-court/) silenced a court as a boy: wisdom does not wait for the right age or the right face.

## What the king learned

The king in this story does not fail for lack of effort. He has given up the throne, the people, the comforts. What he lacks is the knowledge that none of those things were what bound him. The text puts it, in effect, that you can be tied to a cave as strongly as to a palace. A reader with a busy job and a full household may find this comforting. It does not say stay or go. It says: look at what you are holding.

## How to read it

Do not try to read the whole at one sitting. A good approach: begin with the first section, where Rama's sorrow is described, and one story at a time. Read slowly, a page a day. Do not worry if a story's logic seems like a dream. That is intended. And if some lines trouble you, as the Madanika episode troubles many readers, do not force a meaning. Put it aside, and keep the central idea.

## Where this comes from

The Yoga Vasistha (also called Laghu and Maha Yoga Vasistha in different versions) is a single large work with sections on dispassion, the seeker, creation, existence, dissolution and liberation. The Chudala story is placed in the later part. Tellings of the details differ between the Sanskrit text, abridged versions and regional retellings, and we have told it in broad outline. Please check a good translation before quoting any particular line.
