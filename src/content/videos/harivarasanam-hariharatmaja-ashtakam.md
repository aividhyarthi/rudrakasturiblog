## What the hymn says
The eight verses praise Ayyappa in many ways. This is a loose summary and not a word-for-word translation.

- **Verse 1.** The one who enchants the whole universe, whose sandals are worshipped, who destroys foes and who dances without ceasing: the son of Hari and Hara. The opening word, "Harivarasanam", gives the hymn its name.
- **Verses 2 and 3.** He whose praise is the cry of "Sharanam" (refuge), who is the leader of the host of spirits, dear to the devotees who bow to him and a wish-fulfilling tree to them, a lord of the sacred syllable Om and a lover of songs.
- **Verses 4 to 6.** The one who rides a horse and holds the mace, honoured by the Vedas and the guru's grace; worshipped in the three worlds, three-eyed, a divine teacher who grants what we think of; the one who removes the fear of worldly life, is adorned with sacred ash and rides a white mount and a divine elephant.
- **Verses 7 and 8.** His gentle smile and beautiful face, the tenderness of a young elephant; he is dear to those who take refuge in him, an ornament of the Vedas, the life of the good, who delights in music.
- **Every verse ends** with the line: "I take refuge in the divine son of Hari and Hara."

## How this helps
Devotees sing or listen to Harivarasanam at the end of the day to quieten the mind, and during the 41-day Ayyappa vratam. It is a bedtime prayer: gentle and slow, a way to close the day in surrender. For those who have never been to Sabarimala, it is a way to feel connected to the temple.

## How and when to listen
- **Time:** at night, before sleep. During the Mandala season, pilgrims and devotees at home listen to it after the evening prayer.
- **Where:** at the Ayyappa shrine, at the end of the evening bhajan, or at home before sleep.
- **How:** sit quietly, light a lamp if you wish, and listen to the verses all the way through. Many devotees say "Swamiye Saranam Ayyappa" at the end.

## The story behind it
Ayyappa is called Hariharaputra: the son of Hari (Vishnu, in his Mohini form) and Hara (Shiva). The hymn takes this name for its refrain. It is a twentieth-century composition. Its author is widely reported to be Kumbakudi Kulathur Srinivasa Iyer, and it became famous through its singing at Sabarimala and through recordings.
